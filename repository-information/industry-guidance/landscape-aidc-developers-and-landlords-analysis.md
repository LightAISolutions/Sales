## 1. Executive read

**Three judgments, and all three are the module's own rather than findings of the record. They are labelled as analysis wherever they appear.**

**One — dominance in this segment cannot be ranked, and the record says so itself.** Digital Realty's dossier reaches the conclusion directly: the claim that any company is the largest AI-data-centre landlord is *unfalsifiable rather than true or false*, because every published ranking measures something else — colocation revenue share, markets rather than operators, active IT load — and the private platforms that captured AI demand publish no comparable megawatt or revenue figure, so a category ranking would omit half the field by construction. CyrusOne's supplies the measurement of how bad it is: published third-party rankings of that one company disagree **by a factor of about 4.5**, 3,026 MW of tracked capacity against 674 MW of active IT load, because each is estimating a different thing from the outside. **So what incumbency rests on here is not share. It is the capital structure that lets a landlord commit equity years before a lease exists — and the evidence for that is the securitisation market, not a league table.**

**Two — the threat is not share loss, it is that the scarce asset moved.** Land was the constraint and is not any more; an **energised interconnection** is. That is why seven of the thirteen challengers are converted bitcoin miners rather than property developers: they were not competing with Vantage in 2023, and they did not win a bid. They already owned substations, and the market re-priced what they owned. The incumbents' answer — funding their own substations, buying behind-the-meter generation, signing take-or-pay with utilities — is the *same* answer, bought rather than inherited. **A landlord's differentiator in this cycle is time to power, and time to power is now a procurement problem rather than a real-estate one.**

**Three — tenant credit in this segment is manufactured, and its price is quoted.** Four parties routinely sit in one lease, and where the tenant of record is thin the paper is made bankable by a third party. The corpus carries the price in basis points: Hut 8's River Bend notes at **6.192 %** with BBB− ratings behind a Google backstop; Cipher's Amazon-guaranteed paper at **6.125 %** and its Google-backstopped paper at **7.125 %**; Galaxy's Phase II notes at **9.875 %** with an unrated, levered single tenant and no disclosed enhancement. **That spread — roughly 375 basis points between the best-enhanced and the unenhanced — is the most useful single number in the segment**, and it is a price for credit rather than for construction. It is also paid in equity: TeraWulf gave Google about 14 % of pro forma equity for the service, Cipher warrants over about 5.4 % of itself, and Hut 8 — on the same Fluidstack template — gave neither and got the backstop anyway.

## 3. Who dominates, and on what basis

**The organising choice.** The power-conversion landscape had to be written *stage by stage* because its nine incumbents held nine different stages of one chain. This one has the opposite problem: the nine incumbents all do broadly the same thing, and the record will not order them. So the section is organised **by what each one's incumbency actually rests on**, and it says out loud that the four bases are not comparable.

**Basis 1 — a securitisation franchise.** The three largest private platforms are financing machines first. Vantage **originated the data-centre ABS asset class in 2018**, opened the sterling and euro DC-ABS markets, runs regionally ring-fenced equity, and claims $60B+ raised globally since late 2024; its own dossier reads the capital-markets franchise rather than design IP as the durable moat, at moderate confidence. Switch is the **largest single data-centre ABS issuer since 2024** on its own attribution — $4.2B across five issuances plus the largest green data-centre CMBS at $2.4B — and retired 100 % of its $6.5B take-private bank debt through securitisation by July 2025. STACK has run **ten ABS series since 2019**. Compass took the **first Moody's AAA on hyperscale ABS collateral**. QTS set the sector's fastest securitisation pace and capped it with a **$4.6B Baa2 green bond at $12.5B peak demand**. None of those is a market-share statement, and all of them are checkable.

**Basis 2 — a standardized building product.** QTS's **Freedom** design is one repeatable 36–72 MW water-free building stamped across roughly two dozen US mega-campus sites with in-house construction management and a 3,000+ acre power-ready land bank. Compass's is industrialized further: **84 % fabricated offsite**, 80–90 % of power-centre work moved to the factory, a Schneider Electric plant on its own Red Oak campus under a $3B agreement, up to 1,500 Siemens medium-voltage skids, 1.27 design PUE and 100 % uptime on 2025 deliveries — at the price of a smaller footprint (195 employees, ~1.3 GW of listed campus capacity against multi-GW peers). Vantage replicates one 'sustainable by design' blueprint across 41 campuses behind a newly centralized chief procurement office. **For a vendor this is the most actionable basis of the four**, because a standardized product means one specification decision propagates fleet-wide.

**Basis 3 — proprietary engineering.** Aligned's Delta³ source-capture arrays run ~1 % of IT load in fan power against ~10 % for CRAC/CRAH rows, behind 50+ patents, with DeltaFlow rejecting 350 kW/rack and converting live halls air-to-liquid with zero downtime, an in-house Advanced Cooling Lab and localized manufacturing in Brazil — a decade-long build-not-buy pattern. Switch holds **950+ issued and pending patent claims** and a self-authored facility standard (Tier 5 Platinum, explicitly *not* an Uptime Institute rating), proven by two consecutive world-first NVIDIA bring-ups in its halls. Equinix has crossed from power consumer to power developer — the **>100 MW Bloom fuel-cell fleet**, ~1.25 GW of advanced-nuclear commitments and LOIs, and a take-or-pay structure where it funds 100 % of grid upgrades — and its dossier's own framing is that these function as **procurement standards for the whole colocation tier**.

**Basis 4 — being the one that has to publish.** Digital Realty is the listed benchmark: a REIT that must distribute most of its taxable income and therefore cannot retain earnings to fund a $4.25–4.75bn annual development programme, so it issues equity and notes and — increasingly — manages third-party capital for a fee (a $3.25bn US hyperscale fund closed March 2026 with 20 % retained and the mandate; fee income up 98.3 % in FY2025). Its incumbency is real and is also **the only one an outsider can audit**, which is why its numbers do double duty across this module.

**And the honesty note the section owes the reader.** Those four bases are not four measures of one thing. A securitisation franchise is a cost-of-capital fact. A standardized product is an operating fact. A patent estate is a legal fact. A REIT's disclosure is a regulatory obligation. **Flattening them into an ordering would misrepresent all four, and the two members most likely to top such a list — QTS and Vantage — publish no revenue at all.** The module's own assessment, stated as analysis: in this segment the useful question is not who is biggest but **what each landlord's incumbency is made of, because that decides what can take it away.**

**The constraint all nine now share, and it is not power or capital.** It is **consent**, and four dossiers say so independently in the same words. QTS's reads it as the binding constraint after the Dane County/DeForest withdrawal — the first outright loss of a $10B-class site, despite a $50M community package and 750 MW of new renewables. Compass's says consent rather than capital and records the Prince William Digital Gateway (2,000 acres, with QTS) dying in court in April 2026. Vantage's records Port Washington passing **the nation's first anti-data-centre referendum** at 66 %, requiring voter approval for TIF incentives over $10M — around the TIF for its own Lighthouse campus. And the answer is now collective: Compass, QTS, Vantage and Digital Realty **co-founded the Infrastructure for America coalition** in September 2026, and three of them plus EdgeCore have signed a Ratepayer Protection Pledge or endorsed Governor Abbott's Texas standards. **Four rivals lobbying together is the clearest signal in the segment that the constraint is external to all of them.**

## 4. Who threatens, and on which route

**Thirteen challengers is the largest challenger set S2 has met, and they are not a smaller version of the incumbents.** They split cleanly into three routes, and only the third is attacking the same thing the incumbents sell.

**Route 1 — the converted miners, attacking with an energised power position (7 of 13).** Applied Digital, Core Scientific, TeraWulf, Cipher Digital, Hut 8, IREN and Galaxy Digital did not win a bid against Vantage. They owned substations, cheap power contracts and energised interconnections for a different business, and the market re-priced the asset under them. Cipher is furthest through: self-mining hashrate fell from ~23.6 EH/s to ~11.6 EH/s, the joint ventures were sold, one site remains, and **the registrant renamed itself Cipher Digital Inc. on 20 February 2026** with the stated reason that it aligns with becoming an HPC data-centre developer. IREN is liquidating rather than diversifying — $172M of voluntary ASIC impairments in two quarters, bitcoin dropped from the boilerplate, no EH/s figure published since October 2025. **The tell that this is a route and not a coincidence: not one of the seven is competing on building product, cooling IP or design standard. Every one of them is competing on a date.**

**Route 2 — the energy-first developers, attacking by owning the generation (2 of 13).** Crusoe energised 200+ MW at Abilene within a year of groundbreaking and a substation in under six months, on a procurement list that reads like the supplier index — GE Vernova turbines, Bergen engines, **ON.energy MV-BESS at 5 GW**, Form Energy iron-air, Blue Energy and Aalo nuclear. Fermi America goes further and treats the utility as the supplement: about 1.5 GW of turbines landed and 6 GW of gas permitted **before any tenant**, financed by three equipment lenders at 12–13 %, with an AP1000 programme docketed at the NRC. Both are betting that behind-the-fence generation beats the interconnection queue — and both carry the same exposure in its acute form. Crusoe's dossier records two single-counterparty decisions reshaping the company inside 2026 (Oracle/OpenAI halting Abilene expansion; Google managing it off Wyoming). Fermi's records a warehouse line that **punishes the absence of a 400 MW lease by November 2026** and a single signed tenant whose lease is conditional.

**Route 3 — the ladder specialists, attacking one rung rather than the whole thing (4 of 13).** Tract is the purest: it sells the **powered, permitted ground** and leaves the vertical work to others — and its own dossier records that the product has **not yet been sold to anyone outside the group**, the only recorded transactions being two Storey County parcels bought by its own affiliate's project companies. PowerHouse buys or options sites, secures utility capacity and zoning, builds powered shells, and at ABX-1 sold the stabilized building to its tenant; its counters are expressly "in planning, underway, or completed" and the boilerplate grew from 4.1 GW to ~10 GW in 21 months while the delivered base stayed at one building. EdgeCore is the sponsor-financed single-tenant build-to-suit executed at project level, with a verifiable operating base of **about 62 MW in two buildings** behind a "1.8 GW delivered or under development" headline. Prime is the recapitalisation case — five equity rounds, an acquisition line, a shelved bond, project-level high-yield debt, **about 151 MW operating after nine years**.

**What the three routes have in common, and it is the module's own assessment.** None of the thirteen is attacking an incumbent's ranking — there is no ranking to attack. Route 1 attacks with a date, Route 2 with a fuel supply, Route 3 with a rung. So **the threat to this segment's incumbents is not share loss to a cheaper rival: it is that the scarce asset moved from land to an energised interconnection**, and a miner already owned one. That is why the incumbents' response is procurement rather than product — self-funded substations at Vantage, QTS, Compass, EdgeCore and Switch; behind-the-meter microgrids at Vantage (700 MW, VoltaGrid); fuel cells at Equinix and STACK; a battery as an interconnection accelerator at Aligned; and at CyrusOne, co-location on someone else's electrical asset at three of its four largest campuses.

**The counter-threat the challengers carry and the incumbents do not.** Route 1's power position came with a tenant problem. Core Scientific's 10-K states **CoreWeave is 100 % of colocation revenue and ~83 % of total Q2 2026 revenue**; Applied Digital's filings show two customers at 100 % of continuing revenue with an **unnamed** hyperscaler at ~69 % of a $36bn backlog; Galaxy's single tenant is CoreWeave and its risk factor concedes it. That is the other half of §1's third judgment: the credit layer exists because Route 1's tenants are thin, and its price is the 375-basis-point spread.

**And the threat that carries no challenger label at all.** Three of the eight adjacents are not suppliers to this segment — they are **parties that can host a campus without being a landlord**. Talen Energy is the corpus's worked example of a merchant generator selling from an existing fleet, and the FERC record its Susquehanna arrangement produced now governs everyone. Intersect Power put solar, wind, batteries and gas on the same site as a hyperscaler's data centre **and then became part of the hyperscaler** — a $12bn combined exit and, since 10 March 2026, a Google subsidiary. Eolian converts a battery interconnection into speed-to-power, with CyrusOne's DFW7 the first marquee ERCOT case. **A tenant that can buy a powered site from a generator does not need a landlord**, and that route is being built by companies the registry classes as adjacent precisely because they are not landlords.

## 7. What it means for the active engagement — the seller's play

Written for §10.10's two implicit role paths. The segment is a **buyer class**, so both paths end here and the advice differs by route, not by product.

**For the AIDC-power seller (the §10.10 chain-first path).** Three things decide the call.

1. **Establish the route before the product.** An incumbent is buying a standardized product decision that propagates fleet-wide; a Route 1 converted miner is buying a date; a Route 3 ladder specialist may not be buying equipment at all, because it sells the parcel before the fit-out. Pitching a cooling architecture to Tract is a category error.
2. **Lead with lead time, then with the specification.** The dossiers say this in chorus: transformers at 75–110 weeks, generator step-up units at 100–150+ weeks, medium-voltage switchgear sold out through 2028, and Data Center Frontier flagging "minimal procurement buffer" on TeraWulf's 2H-2027 Anthropic schedule with exactly those three items named as the choke point. **In this cycle a factory-built unit deliverable in months is worth more than a better unit deliverable in three years**, and the segment has already voted with reservations rather than orders — Digital Realty's $373m and Switch's $1.9bn Schneider agreements, Compass's $3bn Schneider plant on its own campus and up to 1,500 Siemens skids.
3. **Know who signs.** The general-contractor bench holds the contract with the landlord, not with the tenant in the press release, and the specification is usually written by the engineering firm rather than by either. Where a landlord has centralized procurement — Vantage's chief procurement office, QTS's repeat-GC model, Compass's factory — one qualification decision is worth a fleet.

**For the BESS seller (the §10.10 product-first path).** This segment is where storage stops being a grid asset and becomes an interconnection instrument, and there are three live sockets in it.

1. **The interconnection accelerator.** Aligned's Calibrant BESS is framed explicitly as that — 31 MW / 62 MWh, October 2025 — and CyrusOne's DFW7 sits on top of Eolian's Chisholm Grid battery for the same reason. The pitch is not arbitrage revenue; it is **a date**.
2. **The ride-through requirement.** NOGRR 282 requires new ≥75 MW electronic loads in ERCOT to ride through voltage and frequency disturbances rather than trip, which practically mandates buffering between grid and GPUs. Hut 8 states Beacon Point's design incorporates battery systems to mitigate voltage impacts and support grid integration. **That is a code-driven product category at the data centre itself** — and the mechanism belongs to `power-infra-aidc-2026-08` and `large-load-interconnection-2026-09`; what belongs here is that it is a named, funded requirement at a named campus.
3. **The MV-BESS commitment at scale.** Crusoe's ~5 GW ON.energy medium-voltage BESS commitment is among the largest battery-UPS commitments anywhere, and it sits in Route 2 rather than in the incumbent tier — which is where a seller should look first, because energy-first developers own the power decision outright.

**And the discipline both paths share.** Never quote a capacity figure back to this segment without its rung, and never quote a lease value without saying which of the five measures it is. Both tests are taught in `the-campus-as-a-power-project`, which is public — so an analyst on the account already has them, and a contributor who gets them wrong in the room is corrected by a colleague with less access.
