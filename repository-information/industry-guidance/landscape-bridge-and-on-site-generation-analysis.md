## 1. Executive read

**The segment's own definition says it converts the interconnection queue into a calendar. The module's organising judgment is that it converts that calendar into a balance-sheet position — and that the roster's role labels therefore mark a claim on DELIVERY rather than on manufacturing.**

The proof is a single arithmetic fact on the largest incumbent's own file. GE Vernova's 116 GW of committed gas capacity splits into **53 GW of equipment backlog and 63 GW of deposit-backed slot reservations** — *more of it is reservations than orders.* And the corollary runs all the way down the roster: **two of the eight incumbents manufacture no prime mover at all.** VoltaGrid buys INNIO Jenbacher engines; ERock buys Power Solutions International blocks. Between them they hold the two largest contracted behind-the-meter AI books in the corpus, and neither builds an engine.

That is why the dominance section is written as **four kinds of incumbency** rather than as a league table. Three heavy-frame OEMs *are* the queue and set everyone's date. Two gen-set duopolists own the standby fleet by installed base and dealer network and are spending irreversible capital to cross into prime. One engine-plant builder sells medium-speed plants past 450 MW. And two platform operators sell delivered megawatts with somebody else's machines inside them. Those four incumbencies are measured in incompatible units — gigawatts under contract, percentage share of a generator market, megawatts of engine plant booked, gigawatts of customer contract — and the corpus contains no ranking across them.

The threat section is the cleanest in the series to write, because `bridge-power` already enumerated the three ways out of a factory queue and the registry's five challengers fill all three. What the module adds is size and honesty: the largest challenger by revenue guidance has repriced on a master-agreement **ceiling** of 2.8 GW against ~1.2 GW contracted; the one with the widest product span books **less than 10 %** of its power-generation business as prime power today; the fourth-ranked gen-set OEM builds none of its own large engines and publishes no financials at all; and the permitting-wedge challenger's whole plant output is ~250 MW a year against gigawatt-class procurement.

**And the incumbents' answer to all five challengers is the same single move: add capacity.** Caterpillar to ~3× 2024 large-engine output plus a ~1.5 GW medium-speed restart; Cummins $450M for 20 GW more to reach 55 GW by 2030; Siemens Energy +30–50 % over FY26–28; GE Vernova from ~20 to 24 to 30 GW a year; Mitsubishi +30 %. **VoltaGrid's own dossier states the consequence against itself** — *"the moat is time-limited — recip capacity is scaling industry-wide … and interconnection reform would collapse the speed premium."* That is the segment's clock, and it is why §10's review gate is a capacity date rather than a policy date.

### 2a. The hardest line, and the one that decides the module — `bridge-power` pre-declares the handoff MORE COMPLETELY THAN ANY NEIGHBOUR EVER HAS

`bridge-power` (*Bridge Power: Building the Plant Before the Grid Arrives*, public, track 3, `updated` 2026-09-02) is the first entry in this segment's `READ_NEXT` and it is **this segment's mechanism lesson in full**. Its `ways-to-eat-the-same-gas` section teaches **the same four machine classes the segment's own definition names** — heavy-frame and aeroderivative gas turbines, reciprocating engines, fuel cells and linear generators — with advantages and disadvantages for each. Its `the-turbine-queue` section teaches **the slot-reservation instrument**: that turbine delivery rather than construction sets when new gas power can exist, that slot reservations became assets in their own right, that developers pay to hold manufacturing positions before projects are final, and that a builder holding reserved slots can promise a power date a rival physically cannot.

**And it names not one vendor while doing it.** Every actor in those two sections is anonymised by construction: *"one supplier's heavy-frame book"*, *"one operator locked roughly 3.8 gigawatts of a single engine maker's production"*, *"one vendor's spark-ignited unit"*, *"a planned plant on the order of 250 MW a year"*, *"three manufacturers make the machine"*. §10.6 (t)'s pre-declaration test was introduced at session 6 on a **tile** that declined a ranking, and fired at session 8 on a **callout** that taught an instrument and named no holder. **Here it fires on two entire sections that teach the whole of buying criterion 1 and the whole of buying criterion 2's machine axis, vendor-blind, on purpose.** That is the cleanest statement of the layer-4 boundary any neighbour has made, and it settles this module's scope before a word of it was drafted.

Session 8's line holds exactly and needs no amendment: **a market condition taught vendor-blind belongs in the mechanism lesson; one named vendor's own instrument belongs in the landscape.** So the module carries **no** four-machine comparison, **no** efficiency ladder, **no** three-ways-out-of-a-queue taxonomy and **no** explanation of what a reservation is. It carries the holders: GE Vernova's **63 GW of deposit-backed slot reservations** against a 53 GW equipment backlog at ~20 % cash down selling into 2031; Siemens Energy's reservation fees *back for the first time since the early-2000s boom* with large-frame slots nearly full through 2028; Mitsubishi Power's public confirmation that customers now pay to hold production slots and its own CEO's observation that this makes things hard for developers who must post a deposit very early; NRG's slot reservation agreements expanded from 1.2 GW to 3.6 GW **without a press release and without a firm order**; VoltaGrid's ≥3.8 GW of locked INNIO Jenbacher production; Rolls-Royce's AVK agreement, which its own dossier describes as a guarantee of production slots in exchange for committed order volumes; and Piller's build slots *"now being released for 2028"*.

**§7.23's test — the one number both neighbours cite doing a different job in each — holds on the slot itself.** In the lesson a reserved slot is *a mechanism that explains why the escape routes exist at all*. In the module it is **a position on a named company's balance sheet that a buyer with no permit can own**: Grid United's reservation is grid equipment's example, and here it is NRG holding 3.6 GW of GE Vernova slots with no final investment decision, no named site and no signed counterparty.

## 3. Who dominates, and on what basis — four incumbencies, measured in four incompatible units

**Group 1 — the three heavy-frame OEMs, who do not compete for the campus so much as set its date.** Together they supply roughly **two-thirds of the turbines in gas plants under construction worldwide**, against global orders around 110 GW at end-2025 and manufacturing capacity of 60–70 GW. Mitsubishi Power's own CEO puts the three-OEM total at roughly 60 GW a year. That gap is the segment's founding fact.

- **GE Vernova** is first by committed position and the file's clearest read on the whole segment: **116 GW committed = 53 GW equipment backlog + 63 GW deposit-backed slot reservations** at ~20 % cash down, reservations now selling against **2031** delivery at ~$3,000/kW implied combined-cycle pricing, roughly triple three years ago, with Q2 2026 alone converting 10 GW of reservations into orders. Data-centre electrification orders ran ~$0.7B in 2024, above $2B in 2025 and **above $5B in the first half of 2026 alone**. Its own strategy read calls the pricing power **scarcity rent rather than a durable moat**, because Siemens Energy nearly doubled unit sales year on year and competitor supply lands in the same 2028–2030 windows it is now selling. It also holds the segment's only *fast* line: LM6000 five-minute starts across 1,300+ fielded units, LM2500XPRESS packages powering Crusoe, and TM2500 mobile units installed in **11 days**.
- **Siemens Energy** is the only book from which a gigawatt campus can be written in one go, and the one that productised on-site power first. Gas Services booked **€10.0B in a single quarter** (book-to-bill 2.65; 73 turbines = 15 GW) with US data centres the named driver, on a €73B segment backlog inside a group backlog of €162B, and Q3 FY26 delivered the highest quarterly group margin in company history at 14.2 %. Its AIDC product is the **Eaton alliance's standardised 500 MW SGT-800 block** — grid-independent, *"eliminating diesel backup gensets"*, 99.99 %+ availability, hydrogen-ready to 75 vol %, claiming up to two years off time-to-power. Its expansion is deliberately **+30–50 % rather than maximal**, which its own dossier reads as managing the scarcity rather than spending it. **And its slot position is the sector's constraint stated as a fact: large-frame slots nearly full through 2028, with reservation fees back for the first time since the early-2000s boom.**
- **Mitsubishi Power** is the distant third by order book and **the only one of the three deliberately declining the volume** — 35 GW of large-frame backlog across 80 units against GE Vernova's 116 GW and Siemens Energy's 95 GW, with its focus stated as large-frame projects for **core utility customers** rather than data centres. Its data-centre offer is explicitly **two-tier**: FT8/FT4000 aeroderivative packages (29–140 MW, 614 units installed) as *a temporary bridge until a grid connection arrives*, and H-25/H-100 industrial frames (about 40–120 MW) with absorption chillers as permanent behind-the-meter cogeneration. **Three honesty notes travel with it and its own dossier states all three**: it is a brand of Mitsubishi Heavy Industries and **no Mitsubishi Power revenue figure exists or can be derived**; the three-way backlog comparison is not like-for-like, because the other two include reservations and Mitsubishi does not split its out; and it **exited battery storage entirely** in July 2026, selling Prevalon for up to $365M, which leaves the storage half of its own stated behind-the-meter answer as somebody else's product.

**Group 2 — the two gen-set duopolists, who own the standby fleet and are buying their way into prime.** Third-party market data puts **Caterpillar at about 18 % and Cummins at about 16 %** of the data-centre generator market, and Caterpillar with Solar Turbines at **33 % of tracked permitted behind-the-meter data-centre capacity**. Both are capacity stories rather than demand stories and both say so.

- **Caterpillar** has the widest footprint across the on-site chain of any member: C175/3516E diesel standby, G3520 Fast Response and G3500K/H gas prime, Solar Turbines at 1–39 MW with more than 16,000 units installed, its own switchgear, transfer switches, microgrid controller and a January-2025 battery line — sold through **150 dealers and more than 1,800 branches**. Diesel sets are sold into **2028** and gas prime into **2029**; the backlog is **$72.1B**, nearly doubled in a year; large-engine capacity is going to roughly **3× 2024** and turbines to 2.5×; and about **1.5 GW of medium-speed (former MaK) capacity is being restarted** with shipments from Q4 2026. Its own group president's number is the commercial point: **a continuous-duty gas gen-set yields roughly forty times the lifetime services opportunity of a standby diesel.**
- **Cummins** reaches furthest *down* the electrical chain from the engine — QSK95 and Centum gen-sets, in-house alternators (STAMFORD/AvK/MARKON, 7.5–11,200 kVA, also sold to competitors' packagers), PowerCommand paralleling switchgear, transfer switches to 4,000 A, digital master control and, since 2025, batteries — with a **$12.4B Distribution segment** of ~15,000 people servicing it for life. High-horsepower sets are **sold out into the second half of 2028**; $200M and 9 GW of capacity are already added and **$450M more is committed to add 20 GW for 55 GW by 2030**; Power Systems margin hit a record **29.5 %** in Q1 2026. Its own Analyst Day named its three gaps aloud — emissions-certified after-treatment, a large-megawatt gas platform, and battery and hybrid integration — which is a company describing its weakness in a slide deck.

**Group 3 — the engine-plant builder.** **Wärtsilä** sells medium-speed 4-stroke plants built from 6–23 MW sets and **scaling past 450 MW per plant**, at up to 52.1 % electrical efficiency on the 31SG with 2-minute starts and 30-second grid connection on the 50SG/50DF. It booked **789 MW of US data-centre projects in 2025** plus further Q1 2026 orders. The fact that decides how to read it: the engine business is the **retained** half. Its storage business is folding into a 50/50 joint venture with RCT Solutions after a **60 % order collapse**, and its own dossier is explicit that data-centre exposure continues *regardless of the storage outcome*. **In this segment Wärtsilä is an incumbent because of the business it kept, not the one it is leaving.**

**Group 4 — the two platform operators, and the sharpest structural fact in the roster: neither makes a prime mover.**

- **VoltaGrid** is the volume leader of behind-the-meter AI-campus power: **4.3+ GW fully contracted through 2028 and a 7.5 GW order book through 2030** — Oracle 2,300 MW across Texas, the Vantage *Frontier* campus at 210 Jenbacher engines for ~700 MW initially, a >1 GW Vantage portfolio partnership — capitalised with $5B of debt and a $1B equity round. Its QPac product stacks 20 MW high-inertia recip nodes to **200 MW of prime power under a single minor-source air permit**, and is marketed explicitly with *"zero reliance on battery storage"*, holding transients on engine inertia plus **62 ABB synchronous condensers with flywheels**. **The moat is engine-supply logistics, not technology, and its own dossier says so**: ≥3.8 GW of locked Jenbacher production — INNIO's two largest orders ever — plus Energy Transfer firm gas, the largest Permian CNG terminal among five, and in-house packaging via Propell.
- **ERock** is the same shape at a different granularity and with a different structure. Public since a June 2026 NYSE IPO, ~$626.6M cash and zero debt, **~$1.7B backlog up about tenfold year on year** after a ~470 MW RockBlock order from Anthropic — its largest ever. Its plants are 500 kW ERT500 units and 1.5–3.5 MW RockBlock strings on **Power Solutions International** natural-gas blocks under a ~$335M five-year exclusive supply agreement, assembled in Houston-area plants ramping toward **~1.2 GW/yr by end-2026**, production *"completely sold out through 2027 and into 2028"*. Its Meta/El Paso Electric structure is the template competitors now have to answer: a **$473–500M plant owned by the utility, fully funded by Meta under a commission-approved rate, operated by ERock** — a merchant bridge sale converted into regulated paper. **And the irony its own dossier records rather than hides: its single-source engine supplier is majority-owned by China's Weichai Power, so the FEOC-clean platform has a China dependency one layer down.**

**The honesty note this section owes the reader.** A gigawatt of contracted turbine slots, a percentage of a generator market, a megawatt figure for engine plants booked, and a gigawatt of signed customer contract are **not measures of one thing**. No source reachable through these seventeen dossiers ranks across them, and flattening them would misrepresent all four. **The module's own assessment, stated as analysis rather than as a finding of the record: in this segment incumbency is a claim on delivery rather than on manufacturing — which is why two of the eight incumbents build no engine, and why the largest manufacturer's committed position is more reservations than orders.**

## 4. Who threatens, and on which route — five challengers, three routes, and a clock that runs against all of them

The routes are §2b's. What follows is size and honesty.

**Change machine class.** **Rolls-Royce Power Systems** has the widest single-vendor span in on-site power anywhere in this corpus — mtu diesel sets to 4,000 kVA, lean-burn gas sets, the **Kinetic PowerPack diesel-rotary UPS**, the EnergyPack battery line, the EnergetIQ microgrid controller and, since February 2026, **prefabricated 10/20/30 MW gas power-plant modules with a stated 12–18 month lead time**. It has been re-rated by data centres more completely than any other gen-set OEM: divisional margin from 13.1 % to 20.3 % in eighteen months, book-to-bill 1.8×, and Citigroup making Power Systems the **largest single contributor to the listed parent's sum-of-the-parts, ahead of Civil Aerospace**. Three checks its own dossier supplies: **prime power is less than 10 % of its power-generation business today**, targeted at 15–20 % by 2030; trade surveys of gigawatt-scale recip awards list it **on product attributes with no named deal**; and **no hyperscaler, landlord or neocloud is named as a customer in any source**. **Rehlko** is the fourth-ranked gen-set OEM and the only private, leveraged one — ~1.7 GW of hyperscale backup awards in a single 60-day window, capacity up more than 400 % planned through the decade, the only gen-set OEM here that also makes a three-phase static UPS and 15 kV metal-clad paralleling switchgear. Its route into gas prime power is **Clarke Energy**, INNIO Jenbacher's largest authorised distributor across 27 countries — *so it reaches the prime-power campus without manufacturing the engine*, which is the structural difference from the other three gen-set OEMs. Two facts bound it: **its 700–4,000 kW G-Drive engines are co-developed with and built by Liebherr at Colmar in France**, so its capacity ceiling is a partner's investment decision and its American supply chain is a tariff exposure it publishes nothing about; and **it discloses no revenue, EBITDA, backlog or headcount in any first-party source, any year.**

**Change where the machine comes from.** **ProEnergy** refurbishes retired Boeing 747/767 **GE CF6-80C2** cores into its own-spec **PE6000** — 48 MW ISO, 8,800 Btu/kWh, five-minute start, LM6000-interchangeable but OEM-independent — and quotes **2027** against LM6000 waitlists of three to five years. Its AIDC book is 21 PE6000s (>1 GW) to two unnamed projects on five-to-seven-year bridge structures plus 13 units (650 MW) to Crusoe. Its proof of model is the **2.4 GW WattBridge ERCOT peaker fleet** it developed, built and then sold — CPS Energy taking 1,632 MW for ~$1.387B and South Texas Electric Cooperative 768 MW for $768M — **retaining long-term O&M on all of it** and recycling ~$2.16B into contracted AIDC power. **Its own dossier states the window is closing**: GE Vernova aero output rose 32 % year on year in Q1 2026 and the recip incumbents are adding tens of gigawatts.

**Build a machine nobody queues for.** **Bloom Energy** is the largest challenger by any revenue measure — 2026 guidance raised twice to **$3.9–4.2B**, the first $1B+ quarter, an Oracle master agreement reaching **up to 2.8 GW**, AEP firming 1 GW / $2.65B, Brookfield's financing framework expanded fivefold to $25B, and a **55-day** first deployment against multi-year interconnect waits. **And its own dossier supplies the discount**: the 2.8 GW is a *ceiling* with about 1.2 GW contracted, management disclaims more than ~six months of demand visibility, the manufacturing ramp is the binding constraint, and a short report on scandium sourcing plus a securities class action are live. Its permitting case is the real one — non-combustion, **0.003 lbs/MWh NOx**, zero water — and the check is that gas-fed cells emit CO₂ roughly on par with modern combined cycle. **Mainspring Energy** is the permitting wedge in its purest form: a linear generator at **sub-1.5 ppm NOx with no after-treatment at all**, admitting it to non-attainment areas that exclude diesels, recips and turbines — *a claimed ~46 % of planned data-centre capacity* — plus a flat **30 % ITC** under §48E after OBBBA classified linear generators as qualified fuel cell property, with no Chinese supply-chain exposure. **The constraint is scale and it is not close**: today's Bay Area output plus a delayed ~$175M Pittsburgh plant is about **250 MW a year** against gigawatt-class procurement, and there is no named hyperscaler logo.

**The counter-threat, and it is not size.** Every incumbent's answer to all three routes is the same move — **add capacity** — and it lands in the same eighteen months. Caterpillar ~3× large-engine output plus the 1.5 GW medium-speed restart from Q4 2026; Cummins $450M for 20 GW more; Siemens Energy +30–50 % over FY26–28; GE Vernova ~20 → 24 → 30 GW a year and *"largely sold out of 2030 deliveries by end of 2026"*; Mitsubishi +30 %. **The module's own assessment, stated as analysis: the challengers here are not attacking a product position, they are renting a window — and the incumbents are closing it with capital rather than with price.** VoltaGrid, an incumbent, states the same clock against itself.

## 7. The seller's play — §10.10's two paths

**The storage seller.** This segment is the one every BESS bridge or buffer proposal is priced against, and the public curriculum already says where the socket is — *the socket is not the campus, it is the moment*: the bring-up, the transient and ride-through duty, and the conversion at the end of a bridge. What this module adds that no lesson can is **which named counterparty has closed that moment and which has left it open.** Three things. **First, the architecture decision is made before anyone talks about batteries, and one incumbent markets the closure explicitly** — VoltaGrid's QPac is sold with *"zero reliance on battery storage"*, holding transients on engine inertia plus 62 ABB synchronous condensers with flywheels, which means every QPac campus is a site where the storage layer was designed out at the architecture stage rather than lost in a bake-off. **Second, four members have an open socket and one of them says so in its own roadmap** — ProEnergy has no storage offering anywhere in its platform against >1.65 GW of announced AIDC turbine capacity; Mainspring's islanded no-diesel microgrids still carry N+1 and transient-coverage duties with no storage partner announced; Rehlko manufactures no battery product at all while its own AI-readiness language describes exactly the problem a battery solves; and **ERock's own bridge narrative concedes that sites transition to "permanent utility feeds supplemented by battery storage" after the ~5-year bridge**, with a CEO who previously ran a storage software company. **Third, the counter-example is the instructive one**: Caterpillar and Cummins both now pair engines with batteries — Caterpillar's Joule award pairs G3520K gas sets with a grid-forming battery, and Cummins' largest-ever storage order was written to *utility-defined requirements* for managing AI load oscillation at the interconnection. **The engine OEMs are buying the answer; the platform operators are designing around it.** That is the map.

**The AIDC power seller.** **Move one: establish whether the account holds a slot or a quotation**, because in this segment those are different instruments with different prices and only one survives somebody else's schedule slipping. Reservations here are deposit-backed at around 20 % cash down, they sell against 2031, and a buyer holding one can promise a date a rival physically cannot — which is also why one adjacent member holds 3.6 GW of reserved slots with no firm order, no final investment decision and no named site. **Move two: establish which of the four kinds of incumbency the vendor on the project actually is**, because two of the eight incumbents manufacture no prime mover and the machine inside their product is somebody else's supply constraint. An account that has locked a platform operator has locked that operator's engine supplier too, one layer down. **Move three: read buying criterion 3 as the sorting mechanism rather than as a compliance box.** Emissions permitting is where the challengers' entire case lives — the non-attainment wedge, the no-SCR machine, the non-combustion machine — and it is the criterion an incumbent seller is least likely to raise first. The live aggregation question under the mobile-turbine bridge model is the specific thing to ask about: a project structured on the answer being *no* is carrying a regulatory bet, and it is worth knowing whether the customer has priced it.
