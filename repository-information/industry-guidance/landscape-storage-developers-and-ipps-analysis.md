## What this is

`CLASSROOM-CURRICULUM-PLAN.md` §10.1 splits the market-structure layer in two. **Layer 3** is the generated segment lesson `segment-storage-developers-and-ipps` — public, analyst-visible, and carrying no judgment at all: the registry's definition, its six buying criteria, the 34-row player table with each member's role and basis, the normalized figures the dossiers carry, the graph edges among members, the last twelve months of developments, and the policy fence. **Layer 4 is this module**, and it exists because the lesson states the record and stops.

The record, stated, does not answer the question a seller has. It does not say why nineteen incumbents can share one segment without any of them holding it. It does not say which of the six buying criteria actually decides a deal, or — the finding this segment turns on — **who is in the room when it is decided**. It does not say what each of the twenty-seven ranked players is *trying to do*. Those are judgments, they rest on the `strategyRead[]` field the generator is forbidden to touch (§10.4, "no `strategyRead` — that is analysis, and analysis is the landscape's"), and they belong at the contributor tier because `INTEGRATED-REMEDIATION-PLAN.md` §7.2 decision 2 put them there.

**This is the one module class that names and ranks covered companies.** The 2026-08-29 content-scope directive keeps single-company analysis out of guidance modules; the landscape modules are the developer-approved exception, and the guidance they give is still to a group — the seller reading it, about the players it sells against and to.

## 1. Executive read

**Nineteen incumbents is not a concentration, and treating it as one is the first mistake this segment invites.** In the two segments S2 has already read, the incumbency question was *who leads* — which ranking house do you believe (integrators), or on which of four different bases does each of four incumbents stand (cells). Here the registry places nineteen companies in "the established leading set" of a single segment, and no ranking house publishes a table they all appear on. What the nineteen actually share is one thing: **they own grid-connected batteries at scale.** What separates them is the structure that pays for the battery, and on that axis the nineteen fall into four groups that do not compete with each other for the same dollar.

**The buyer class that the registry says "decides which integrator's product gets installed" is, on its own record, mostly not the party that decides.** This is the single most useful sentence the segment produces, and it comes from the members themselves rather than from any outside view. Apex's own strategy read states it flatly: "the parties who must approve a battery are the tax-equity banks … and the minority co-owners, not Apex alone." Plus Power's names five financing syndicates and an independent-engineer process "every supplier must clear". Arevon's describes a transferability machine whose ITC dependence "makes FEOC compliance economically existential". ENGIE's records three consecutive minority sell-downs that put "an infrastructure fund's diligence between a vendor and a signature". Criterion 1 — *bankability, the independent engineer's acceptance and the lender's approved-vendor list* — is not a soft preference in this segment. It is a description of where the veto sits.

**The eight challengers are not attacking the nineteen. They are attacking the contract form.** Read them side by side and not one is trying to out-scale an incumbent: Spearmint runs pure merchant with its own trading desk, Hunt Energy Network runs thirty-two batteries deliberately sized at 9.9 MW to stay out of the full interconnection study, Available Power develops that same class and sells at notice-to-proceed, Strata originates a twenty-year utility toll and then sells the battery with the contract attached, GridStor buys other people's late-stage projects with a bank's balance sheet behind it, esVolta mixes contracted California with hedged Texas, Recurrent develops inside a manufacturer, and Excelsior buys batteries rather than building them. Eight challengers, eight different answers to *how does a battery get paid for* — and that, not scale, is the contested ground. A threat section padded to `cells-and-chemistry`'s proportions would have missed it entirely.

**One dated gate reaches nearly the whole segment and it is in none of the date fields.** Not one `policyExposure[]` entry across the 34 members carries a future `effectiveDate` — of the 142 entries the fence holds, 96 carry a date at all and the latest of those is 2026-09-01. A sort of the fence returns nothing. The nearest real gate is the prohibited-foreign-entity **material-assistance cost ratio**, which is indexed to the calendar year and therefore steps on **1 January 2027**; it is stated inside two members' `exposure` prose and in no date field anywhere. §10 resolves it in writing, with the three nearer candidates rejected by name.

**Ownership is in motion across the segment to an unusual degree**, and for a seller this is the difference between a signature and a wasted year: the counterparty who signs, the entity that owns the asset at commercial operation and the owner who sets the next procurement policy can be three different organisations inside twelve months. Ten of the thirty-four carry a live or just-closed ownership event in their dossier — §6 lists them.

## 3. Who dominates, and on what basis

**Nineteen incumbents, four groups. The groups are defined by what pays for the battery, because that is what the record separates them on.**

**Group 1 — the market-makers (NextEra Energy Resources, ENGIE North America, AES Clean Energy).** These are the only three that appear on an independent owner table. S&P Global Commodity Insights, working from government filings, measured US operating battery storage at 30 June 2026 and ranked NextEra first at **5.779 GW**, ENGIE second at **3.662 GW** and AES Clean Energy Development third at **1.978 GW**. That is the closest thing this segment has to a ranking, and it names three companies out of nineteen. NextEra's own dossier reads it as "the market-maker rather than a realistic account" — it sets the price and speed benchmarks (storage buildable in 16–18 months against 4–5 years for a gas peaker), anchors the domestic cell industry's demand with 2026–2029 needs covered at 1.5× by non-FEOC supply, and self-integrates: it buys cells and DC blocks direct, so a containerised-system vendor "has no integrator channel to ride".

**Group 2 — the standalone-storage franchises (Aypa Power, Plus Power, Arevon, Jupiter Power, Eolian, Key Capture Energy, Terra-Gen, Apex Clean Energy).** Eight companies whose primary business *is* the battery, financed on twenty-year utility tolls, resource-adequacy contracts or merchant trading. This is where the segment's actual procurement happens, and it is where the four buying criteria that are not bankability get exercised: Plus Power dual-sources Tesla and Sungrow per project on bankability and geography; Terra-Gen ran LG, Samsung and BYD side by side at 3.3 GWh scale until a single **8 GWh LG Vertech framework (2026–2029, US-made Arizona LFP, about $1.43bn)** ended it; Arevon is seven-for-seven Tesla across roughly 5.6 GWh with, on its own record, no second-vendor precedent at all. Aypa's Brookfield transaction — **about $7bn of enterprise value for a book the buyer describes as "95% contracted … average remaining contract life of 17 years"** — is the clearest public mark of what a contracted standalone-storage platform is worth.

**Group 3 — the developers for whom storage is an attachment (Lightsource bp, Intersect, Invenergy, Clearway Energy, RWE Clean Energy).** Large, credible, hyperscaler-facing developers whose storage book is small, new or invisible. Invenergy is the sharpest case and its dossier says so as a finding rather than a gap in passing: **no battery integrator, cell supplier, PCS vendor or storage EPC is named in any Invenergy release, microsite, counterparty release or trade-press article read** — the only chemistry statement anywhere is "lithium iron phosphate". Lightsource bp is "#1 US solar developer" whose US storage business "barely exists yet" while its APAC arm buys Chinese systems freely. Intersect became a Google subsidiary on 10 March 2026 and is now, on its own dossier's reading, a captive developer with no third-party customers.

**Group 4 — the merchant generators selling from an existing fleet (Talen Energy, Vistra, NRG Energy).** These are in the segment because they sell firm power to AI load, not because they buy batteries. Talen has **15.7 GW of PJM generation** and the Susquehanna arrangement that produced the FERC co-location rulebook everyone now lives under. Vistra runs **43,641 MW hedged by a five-million-customer retail book** against a battery fleet of **624 MW** — down from 1,024 MW between two filings. NRG doubled its fleet to about 25 GW with the LS Power acquisition that closed 30 January 2026. For a battery seller these three are the segment's most misleading members: they carry the largest numbers on the page and the smallest procurement.

**What no group has is a ranking that covers it.** The dossiers are unusually blunt about this. Vistra's own read says the description of it as "the largest merchant battery-storage owner in the country" **should be dropped rather than qualified**, because no independent dated ranking of storage owners by fleet size exists — Wood Mackenzie, ACP and the EIA all decline to publish one, and the closest available table measured megawatts brought online during one year. Clearway's records that no independent tracker measures its battery fleet at owner level at all, and that its 1,439 MW figure is its own, corroborated only by summing its published project table. RWE's says the company "does not appear in the independent top five of US owners at all" and that the **931 MW under construction figure still in circulation is stale company material from October 2024 that should be retired**. Three separate incumbents telling you the league table does not exist is the segment's most reliable fact about itself.

## 4. Who threatens, and on which route

**Eight challengers against nineteen incumbents. None of the eight is trying to become one of the nineteen, and that is the finding.** In `cells-and-chemistry` eleven challengers were attacking four incumbents along a shared axis — the format ladder — and the module's job was to explain why the axis had not moved the incumbency. Here there is no shared axis. Each challenger is a different answer to the question *how does a battery get paid for*, and the incumbents are not defending against them because the incumbents are not in those markets. **Four routes, eight runners.**

**Route 1 — pure merchant, carried by a trading desk (Spearmint Energy, Hunt Energy Network).** Spearmint owns **350 MW / 700 MWh operating in ERCOT**, single-source Sungrow, financed on tax credits and private credit rather than utility contracts, and monetises through its own desk. Hunt Energy Network is the same idea at a different size: **thirty-two distribution-connected batteries at 9.9 MW each** — deliberately under the threshold at which a project enters ERCOT's full interconnection study — plus a 100 MW transmission battery, "100% merchant" in its own words, with an in-house Level 4 QSE and a five-minute optimisation platform it also sells to third parties. Its dossier's judgment is that the trading platform "is the company's competitive asset and the reason it can stay merchant as ERCOT revenues compress". **This route is where the segment's revenue risk is concentrated**: Enverus measured average ERCOT battery revenue falling from **$149/kW in 2023 to a projected $17/kW in 2025**, with ancillary-services revenue down about 90%, and Modo separately measured merchant revenue at **$43/kW for 2024–25 against $192/kW in 2023** — two series that disagree, which no source reconciles.

**Route 2 — originate the contract, then sell the battery under it (Strata Clean Energy, Available Power).** Strata originates land, interconnection and a utility toll, converts the package to cash by selling to a fund-backed owner, and keeps the EPC, O&M or asset-management contract: all three flagship batteries — Ventura, Scatter Wash, White Tank — were sold to fund-backed owners before or during construction. Its own dossier explains why in one sentence: **the company has no financial sponsor and is financed by bank revolvers on a founder-owned balance sheet, so "a 20-year toll is worth more to a fund than to Strata, which is why the tolls are sold."** Available Power runs the same model one size down — 9.9 MW batteries on leased commercial land, sold at notice-to-proceed — and is the route's cautionary case: its storage business has been publicly dormant since March 2024, no battery operates under its ownership, and the parent has redirected its identity and capital to edge micro data centres.

**Route 3 — the sponsor's balance sheet buying finished projects (GridStor, Excelsior Energy Capital).** GridStor is what a bank's infrastructure platform looks like when it decides to own batteries directly: created by a Goldman Sachs Asset Management fund around a pre-acquired project, growing by **five late-stage purchases from four originators in eighteen months**, contracted with SCE, APS and a Fortune 500 counterparty, financed project by project with one recurring lender. Its own read says the sponsor's equity "is not the binding constraint". Excelsior is the route's purest form — **a buyer of batteries rather than a builder** — and is the counterparty on two of the largest US-made storage supply frameworks in the whole corpus: Fluence's 2.2 GWh Gridstack Pro agreement (July 2024) and LG Energy Solution Vertech's 7.5 GWh agreement (December 2024), **9.7 GWh reserved**. Its dossier's second judgment is the one a seller needs: that volume **is a procurement option sized to the pipeline, not a delivery schedule** — neither release names a project, Lydian's first three batteries use CATL cells, and its chief executive said the two frameworks "do not have an impact on Lydian's three projects".

**Route 4 — mixed books and captive channels (esVolta, Recurrent Energy).** esVolta runs contracted resource-adequacy batteries in California beside hedged-and-merchant batteries in Texas, a structure its dossier judges financeable "because the credits and the corporate facility carry it, not because the hedge is large" — **$258m of project debt against 980 MWh**, with the hedge counterparty, tenor and volume never disclosed. Recurrent is the captive case: a developer inside a manufacturer, supplying demand to its own group's battery arm, with **intersegment eliminations of $790.1m in FY2024** showing the flow's scale. That structure is a genuine advantage on cost, schedule and warranty alignment, and it is simultaneously how Recurrent inherits Canadian Solar's FEOC, AD/CVD and Section 232 exposure at exactly the point where US tax equity is most sensitive to it.

**Why none of the eight has taken anything from the nineteen.** Three reasons, all in the record. **Their fleets are an order of magnitude smaller** — the largest challenger position on the page is esVolta's 490 MW / 980 MWh in ERCOT against NextEra's 5.779 GW. **Six of the eight are for sale, being sold, or owned by someone who will decide the next procurement** — Key Capture is the incumbent equivalent, but esVolta is being sold by Generate Capital with Barclays and Truist mandated, and both dossiers make the same point: the outcome, not the current sponsor, decides the next three years of procurement. And **the route they are attacking on is the one the market is repricing**: merchant ERCOT, where revenue fell about 84% from 2023 and interconnection applications fell 50% in H2 2025 while the median queue lengthened to 4.1 years — which, as ENGIE's own dossier notes, *protects incumbents*.

## 5. Each player's bet — the twenty-seven

One row per incumbent and challenger, as `CLASSROOM-CURRICULUM-PLAN.md` §10.6 requires. **All twenty-seven are carried; none is trimmed.** This is the longest bets table the corpus has held — 27 rows against the integrators' 18 and the cells' 15 — and the reason to carry them all is the one session 1 gave: §10.6 says one row per incumbent and challenger, and the honest reading is that all of them belong, not that the table should be cut to the interesting ones. In a segment whose distinguishing feature is that it holds nineteen incumbents, cutting the table would destroy the very thing the module is about.

Every bet is drawn from that member's `strategyRead[]`, which is the dossier's own analysis, and **the dossier's own confidence label travels with it in the last column** — never blended with fact. The module's table carries the same four columns: Player · Role · The bet (analysis, not fact) · Dossier's own confidence.

**The nineteen incumbents.** NextEra — plan around it rather than sell to it: domestic supply locked through 2029 at 1.5× coverage, self-integration removing the integrator channel, and the only reopening scenario a Korean/US cell ramp slipping. ENGIE — recycle capital rather than grow the balance sheet, funding growth by selling minority stakes (Ares, CBRE, Ares again) while retaining control, and answer ERCOT revenue compression with co-located compute. AES Clean Energy — complete the take-private and sell development services rather than own generation, with the Fluence entanglement being stepped back from. Aypa Power — carry a 95%-contracted, investment-grade book into Brookfield's ownership, with local permitting rather than capital now the binding constraint. Plus Power — convert one new twenty-year utility toll per year into a franchise, dual-sourced per project, with no public FEOC posture yet and Corazon forcing the question. Arevon — stay single-integrator at CAISO scale and let the domestic-supply doctrine do the compliance work. Jupiter Power — buy big and quietly inside the safe-harbour window, never co-market, and hedge loudly with domestic supply. Eolian — make the American-built argument the product, and prove that a battery interconnection is data-centre currency. Key Capture Energy — decide late and on terms, with the sale process the gating condition on every framework. Terra-Gen — solve FEOC with one long-dated Korean-US supply chain rather than by diversifying among Chinese OEMs. Apex Clean Energy — farm down bankable operated projects to long-dated foreign capital while retaining control and operating fees. Lightsource bp — run a geographically split playbook, FEOC-clean in the US and Chinese-supplied in APAC. Intersect — be Google's procurement channel behind one interconnection. Invenergy — re-weight growth capital toward gas plants built and transferred to regulated utilities for data-centre load. Clearway Energy — hold a contracted rather than merchant fleet so ERCOT ancillary collapse never reaches the revenue line. RWE Clean Energy — reverse the retrenchment with EUR 17bn to 2031 and pivot the offshore book into gas and LNG. Talen Energy — convert into a PJM gas company with a nuclear anchor, funded with debt, earning from the capacity market more than from the Amazon contract. Vistra — run the integrated merchant hedge and buy operating gas faster than it contracts it. NRG Energy — start from the customers and buy the generation to match, with the LS Power acquisition doubling the fleet.

**The eight challengers.** Spearmint Energy — develop-to-hold merchant in ERCOT on a single integrator and a private-credit stack. GridStor — buy late-stage projects with a bank's sponsorship and contract them before construction, with procurement deliberately un-committed pending Treasury guidance. Available Power — develop 9.9 MW batteries under the interconnection-study threshold and sell at notice-to-proceed, now dormant inside a parent that has moved to edge data centres. esVolta — carry a mixed contracted-and-merchant book financed by credits and a corporate facility, with the sponsor sale deciding the next procurement. Strata Clean Energy — originate the toll, sell the battery under it, keep the service contract. Hunt Energy Network — own and trade thirty-two distribution-connected batteries with an in-house desk, now adding transmission-scale assets and fuel-oil peakers. Recurrent Energy — run the captive channel inside a manufacturer and monetise finished assets to deleverage. Excelsior Energy Capital — reserve US-made battery volume as an option sized to the pipeline and buy rather than build.

## 7. What it means for the active engagement — the seller's play

`CLASSROOM-CURRICULUM-PLAN.md` §10.10 sets two implicit role paths. **The storage seller** reaches this module through the product and market-access tracks; it is the terminal segment of that path, because this is the desk that signs. **The AI-data-centre power seller** reaches it through the power chain, and arrives at a different half of the same roster.

**The storage seller — four moves.** *First*, find the veto before you find the buyer. In this segment the approving parties are routinely the tax-equity banks, the minority co-owners and the independent engineer, and at least four members say so in their own strategy reads. Qualifying the developer and not the syndicate is the characteristic way to lose a year here. *Second*, read criterion 4 against the project's vintage, not the company. FEOC binds by construction start: a buyer with safe-harboured 2025 positions and a buyer starting construction in 2026 are two different customers inside the same company, and several members are explicitly both. *Third*, treat criterion 6 — dual-sourcing and geography — as the entry point it demonstrably is. Plus Power split its fleet between Tesla and Sungrow per project; Terra-Gen ran LG, Samsung and BYD side by side at 3.3 GWh scale; Key Capture built on Sungrow frameworks and Powin. **A segment whose sixth criterion is dual-sourcing has, by construction, a second slot** — and Arevon is the instructive exception, seven-for-seven with, on its own record, no second-vendor precedent to point at. *Fourth*, ask who owns the counterparty this quarter. Ten of thirty-four are mid-transaction.

**The AI-data-centre power seller — three moves.** *First*, know which four of the nineteen are actually selling power rather than buying batteries. Talen, Vistra, NRG and Constellation carry the segment's largest generation numbers and its smallest storage procurement; reading their fleet size as a battery opportunity is the single most expensive misread available here. *Second*, the co-location rulebook is being written now and the segment is where it is being written: the Susquehanna docket produced FERC's December 2025 finding that PJM's tariff was unjust and unreasonable for co-located load, and the June 2026 show-cause orders to all six RTOs. Powered-land and data-centre lines appear across the segment — Apex's Glass Mountain, Strata's Entitled Power Land, GridStor's hyperscaler segment, Invenergy's AI-factory-ready sites, Eolian's energy-plus-load — and in every case the dossier rates it Low or Moderate confidence and records that no customer, site, megawatt figure or contract has been published.

**And one move both readers share.** The market-makers are intelligence, not pipeline. NextEra's own strategy read says it in those words, and the reason generalises: its hub economics, its earnings-call supply-chain language and its order book are the best public indicator of domestic-supply tightness in the whole corpus — which is worth more to a seller than a meeting it was never going to get.

## 10. Freshness gate — the `reviewBy` judgment, resolved

`CLASSROOM-CURRICULUM-PLAN.md` §10.6 sets `reviewBy` from "the nearest dated gate among the members' `policyExposure[]` and the developments the module leans on, else six months from `updated`". The six-month default would be **2027-03-14**. The resolved value is **2027-01-01**, and the reasoning is written out here rather than left to a sort — S2 session 1's discipline, and session 2's finding that **a dated gate can sit inside a `policyExposure[]` entry's `exposure` prose rather than in its `effectiveDate` field**.

**The sort returns nothing at all, and that is stronger than last session's finding rather than a repeat of it.** In `cells-and-chemistry` a sort of the fence returned a *wrong* answer (2027-02) while the prose held the right one. Here there is no answer to sort: **the 34 members carry 142 `policyExposure[]` entries between them, 96 of which carry an `effectiveDate`, and not one of those 96 is later than 2026-09-01.** A generator implementing "earliest future `effectiveDate`, else `updated` + 6 months" — which is exactly what `scripts/build-classroom-segments.py` does for the segment lesson — falls through to the default, and the segment lesson's own `reviewBy` is accordingly 2027-03-14. A hand-authored module can read the prose, and must.

**Taken: 1 January 2027 — the prohibited-foreign-entity material-assistance cost ratio's next calendar-year step.**

Two members state the mechanism between them, and neither states it in a date field. `profile:available-power` @ v1 records, inside its FEOC `exposure` paragraph, that "the prohibited-foreign-entity material-assistance rules apply to storage projects beginning construction from 2026, with **non-PFE cost ratios rising from 55 per cent (2026) to 75 per cent (2030)**". `profile:aes-clean-energy` @ v2 records, inside its own FEOC paragraph, its 10-K's description of a limit on "the percentage of total project costs that may be derived from products that are mined, produced or manufactured in China, **with varying permissible percentages depending on the calendar year and applicable technology**". Read together: the ratio is **indexed to the calendar year**, its 2026 value is 55% and its 2030 value is 75%, so it is not constant across the intervening years. **What the record fixes is not the 2027 figure — which no member states — but the date the ratio next moves: 1 January 2027.** This is §10.6's third example of a gate in its own words, *a rate that changes on a schedule*, and it is the gate on **buying criterion 4**, which the registry itself names for this segment. It reaches every member financing on the ITC, which on this roster is nearly all of them.

## 11. The Scraper interest seed — asked from scratch, and ADDED

Step 9 of `.claude/rules/industry-guidance.md` requires a matching `SCRAPER_INTEREST_TOPIC_SEEDS` entry for a new module, and S2 has now answered the question both ways: session 1 added an integrator seed, session 2 declined because `topic-bess-technology` already owned the cell and chemistry layer and session 1's own seed comment said so in as many words. **§7.21 required this session to ask again from scratch, because a developer-and-IPP layer is a different beast from a technology layer. The answer is yes.**

**The test session 1 set, applied here.** The question is not "is this topic interesting" but "does an existing seed score *this layer*". Walking the roster: `topic-bess-technology` owns the cell and chemistry layer; `topic-bess-integrators` owns the integrator layer — rankings, GWh shipped, containerised systems, DC and AC blocks, LTSAs; `topic-bess-bankability` owns certification, warranty and the safety standards; `topic-china-policy` owns tariffs, export control and FEOC as *content rules*; `topic-utility-procurement` and `topic-federal-interconnection` own the interconnection and docket layers; `topic-grid-infrastructure` and `topic-grid-equipment-shortage` own the wires and the equipment. **Not one of them scores the owner layer** — who owns the batteries, on what contract, financed how, and who is buying or selling the platform.

**The first draft of this seed was wrong, and the check that caught it is the point.** The draft proposed `tolling agreement`, `resource adequacy`, `merchant battery`, `standalone storage`, `independent power producer`, `tax credit transfer`, `battery storage owner` and `storage platform sale`, on the reasoning that the segment's **contract forms** are its distinctive nouns. Checking every term against all 144 already in the array showed **two of the eight collide**: `topic-storage-offtake` — labelled *Storage EPC, offtake & tolling* — already carries `tolling agreement`, `resource adequacy`, `offtake` and `capacity contract`, and `topic-capacity-markets` carries `interconnection queue`, `capacity market`, `capacity auction` and the grid-operator names. **The contract layer is already scored.** Adding a seed that repeated it would double-count one article in the digest's topic band, which is the trap the roster's own `supply chain` and `warranty` notes record twice and the reason session 2 declined its seed entirely.
