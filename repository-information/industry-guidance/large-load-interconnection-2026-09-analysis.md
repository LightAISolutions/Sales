# Large-Load Interconnection — The Federal Rulebook Above the Fence — Analysis & Study Source

**Provenance:** research synthesis, not a document ingest — compiled 2026-09-13 from public primary sources only: the Federal Register full text of FERC Order No. 2023 (88 FR 61014) and Order No. 2023-A (89 FR 27006); the Federal Register notices instituting the six FPA section 206 large-load proceedings (91 FR 37968–37975, 24 June 2026); FERC's own orders as filed by the parties — *PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.*, 193 FERC ¶ 61,217 (2025) (co-location), *Southwest Power Pool, Inc.*, 194 FERC ¶ 61,031 (2026) (HILL/HILLGA) and 195 FERC ¶ 61,196 (2026) (CHILLS); FERC's Unified Agenda entry for Docket No. RM26-4-000 (91 FR 53150); the Sunshine Act notice for the 18 June 2026 open meeting (91 FR 36126); and the Texas Legislature's own record for SB 6 (89R, 2025). **No source document was ingested** — nothing under `industry-guidance/sources/` belongs to this module, and step 1 of the Industry Guidance Command does not apply. Composed **against** the six utility study guides (`dominion-energy`, `oncor`, `aep`, `southern-company`, `xcel-energy`, `entergy`) and `burns-mcdonnell` rather than restating them: those guides own the state layer, and this module owns only what sits above it. Feeds the in-app module `large-load-interconnection-2026-09` in `Profiler.gs` (lane: The AI Data-Center Wave). ## What this is

The corpus can already walk a gigawatt campus from a request to an energized substation. Six utility guides teach the state layer with precision — the anatomy of a large-load tariff, the minimum-demand arithmetic, cost causation versus socialization, SB 6 clause by clause, and the discipline of separating requests from contracted from energized megawatts. What the corpus has never had is **the layer above that**: the federal rulebook, which is where the interconnection *of generation* has been standardised since 2003, where co-location is decided, and where — as of June 2026 — the interconnection *of load* became a live proceeding in all six jurisdictional RTOs at once.

The gap was not a matter of emphasis. Before this module, the string **"Order 2023" appeared nowhere in the 154 study guides, the eight guidance modules, or the guidance analyses**, and not one FERC docket number appeared anywhere in the seven guides that teach this subject. The guides carry state dockets in abundance and with rigour — `PUR-2025-00058`, `26AL-0137E`, `E002/RP-24-67` — and Dominion's guide builds an entire method on reading them. The method was taught; the federal docket set was empty.

This module fills exactly that hole and stops at its edges. It does not re-teach the six-term tariff anatomy (four guides already do), the minimum-demand arithmetic (Dominion works it numerically), SB 6's clauses (Oncor is exhaustive), or the requests-versus-energized discipline (four guides quantify it). It teaches five things the corpus does not have:

1. **The jurisdictional seam** — FPA section 201 / 205 / 206, what each instrument does, and the statutory reason ERCOT sits outside all of it.
2. **Order No. 2023 as an object** — docket, dates, the reforms with their numbers, and the single most useful fact about it for anyone selling into a load interconnection: **it is not about load, and never was.**
3. **The co-location record** — Susquehanna, the rejected amended ISA, the section 206 show-cause proceeding, and the December 2025 order that rewrote what a co-located load owes the grid.
4. **The federal large-load standard being built right now** — the DOE ANOPR, Docket RM26-4, and the six section 206 orders of 18 June 2026 with their deadlines.
5. **The first federal dollar figures in the corpus** — the generator study-deposit ladder, the commercial-readiness deposits, the withdrawal-penalty structure, the study-delay penalties FERC imposed on *transmission providers*, and SPP's HILL deposits, which are the first FERC-accepted study fees for a *load* anywhere.

## 1. Executive read

**A large load answers to two regulators, and until 2026 only one of them had written it a rulebook.** The state commission writes the retail tariff, the certificate and the terms of service; the corpus teaches that layer well. FERC writes the transmission tariff, the interconnection procedures and the rules on co-located load — and for load, FERC had written almost nothing.

- **The federal *pro forma* interconnection procedure is for generators, and has been since 2003.** Order No. 2003 required public utilities to file standard generator interconnection procedures for generating facilities **greater than 20 MW**. Order No. 2023 (Docket RM22-14-000, 88 FR 61014, published 6 September 2023, effective 6 November 2023, 336 Federal Register pages) rebuilt those procedures into a first-ready, first-served cluster process. Order No. 2023-A (RM22-14-001, 89 FR 27006, 16 April 2024, effective 16 May 2024) set it aside in part on rehearing.
- **The single most load-bearing fact in this module:** in the full Federal Register text of Order No. 2023, the phrase **"large load" appears zero times** and **"load interconnection" appears zero times**. The two occurrences of "co-location" refer to co-located *generating facilities* sharing a point of interconnection, not to load. Order No. 2023-A contains zero occurrences of "large load", "load interconnection", "co-location" or "data cent-". A seller who reads "FERC reformed interconnection in 2023" and assumes it governs a campus's connection date has read the wrong rule.
- **Order No. 2023 nonetheless matters to a campus — indirectly and enormously.** It sets the pace at which *new supply* can reach the grid the campus wants to draw from, and it is the origin of the "batch studies replacing serial ones" that the utility guides refer to without attribution.
- **Co-location is the one place FERC has already acted on load, and it acted twice.** On 1 November 2024 the Commission rejected an amended interconnection service agreement for the Susquehanna arrangement, 2–1 (*PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.*, 189 FERC ¶ 61,078 (2024), Docket ER24-2172). On 20 February 2025 it instituted a section 206 show-cause proceeding into PJM's co-location provisions (190 FERC ¶ 61,115, Docket EL25-49-000). On **18 December 2025** it found PJM's tariff unjust and unreasonable and directed compliance filings (193 FERC ¶ 61,217).
- **On 18 June 2026 the federal layer arrived for load, in every jurisdictional RTO at once.** Rather than issue a NOPR in the large-load rulemaking, FERC issued **six separate FPA section 206 show-cause orders** — one to each RTO/ISO and its transmission owners — Dockets **EL26-67** (PJM), **EL26-68** (SPP), **EL26-69** (NYISO), **EL26-70** (MISO), **EL26-71** (CAISO), **EL26-72** (ISO-NE), reported at 195 FERC ¶ 61,211–61,216. Sixty days to justify the status quo or file tariff changes; a 90-day abeyance available on request.
- **ERCOT is not among the six, and the reason is statutory, not an oversight.** ERCOT is not a FERC-jurisdictional transmission provider, which is precisely why Texas legislated: SB 6 (89th Legislature, 2025) did by statute what FERC is now doing by section 206 elsewhere.
- **The first FERC-accepted large-load interconnection process is SPP's, and it has real prices.** HILL and HILLGA were accepted 14 January 2026 (194 FERC ¶ 61,031, Docket ER26-247-000), effective 15 January 2026. A HILL is **10 MW or more at 69 kV and below, or 50 MW or more above 69 kV**. The study costs **a $10,000 non-refundable application fee plus a $100,000 deposit**, with **$200,000 more** if an electromagnetic-transient study is needed and **$25,000** for a supplemental study at the host transmission owner's option.
- **The bargain the federal layer is now offering is the same one in every forum: flexibility for speed.** SPP's CHILLS (195 FERC ¶ 61,196, Docket ER26-1323, effective 1 July 2026) is an as-available service with a **one-year minimum and seven-year maximum term**, curtailable behind all firm service. FERC's December 2025 PJM order directs two new *Contract Demand* services for loads "willing and able to limit their use of the transmission system". Texas's SB 6 makes curtailability a condition of connecting at all.

**The one sentence a seller should carry:** the tariff sets what a campus pays and the federal layer sets whether it may connect at all — and a load that will accept curtailment is now, in three separate forums, being offered a faster answer than one that will not.

**The one question a buyer should ask:** *"Which regulator owns the thing that is delaying me?"* A study queue in PJM, MISO, SPP, NYISO, CAISO or ISO-NE is federal and is being rewritten this quarter; the same delay in ERCOT or behind a vertically integrated utility's own queue is a state matter and is not.

## 3. Two jurisdictions, and where the line actually runs

The Federal Power Act splits the industry at wholesale. FERC has jurisdiction over the transmission of electric energy in interstate commerce and the sale of electric energy at wholesale; the states keep retail rates, siting, and the certificate that lets a plant or a line be built. The corpus teaches the state half and never draws the seam itself.

**The three instruments a reader needs to recognise.**

- **FPA section 205** is the utility's own filing. A transmission provider files a tariff change; it takes effect on the date proposed unless FERC suspends or rejects it. Every RTO change described in this module that began with the RTO — SPP's HILL, SPP's CHILLS, MISO's ERAS — is a section 205 filing, and the Commission's answer is an order *accepting* it, often "subject to condition" with a compliance filing due in 30 days.
- **FPA section 206** is the complaint route, and the Commission can start one on its own motion. It is how FERC reaches a tariff whose owner has not volunteered to change it: the Commission institutes a proceeding, sets a **refund effective date** (in the six large-load dockets, the date of Federal Register publication, 24 June 2026), and puts the burden on the record to show the existing tariff is unjust and unreasonable. EL25-49 and EL26-67 through EL26-72 are all section 206 proceedings.
- **An interconnection service agreement** is a filed rate in its own right. Susquehanna is the proof: an *amendment* to one ISA, filed in Docket ER24-2172, became the most consequential co-location decision in the record when FERC rejected it.

**Why ERCOT is not in the six.** ERCOT's grid is not synchronously interconnected with the rest of the country in a way that makes its transmission providers FERC-jurisdictional public utilities, so FERC's section 206 reach does not extend to them. That is the whole explanation for a pattern the corpus already shows without explaining: Oncor's guide spends fifteen sections on a market with exactly one FERC sentence in it, and that sentence is a hand-off. The consequence is not that Texas has no large-load rulebook — it is that Texas's rulebook is a **statute** (SB 6, 89th Legislature, Regular Session, 2025, captioned "Relating to the planning for, interconnection and operation of, and costs related to providing service for certain electrical loads…", effective immediately on 20 June 2025) and an implementing PUCT rule, rather than an OATT amendment. Entergy Texas shows the seam from the other side: it sits in MISO, under FERC's tariff, and under Texas law at the same time.

**The jurisdictional fight is live, not settled.** In the co-location docket Talen argued that asserting jurisdiction over co-location arrangements on the theory that the co-located load receives jurisdictional transmission or ancillary service would "improperly federalize the entire electric system". FERC did not accept blanket jurisdiction over the arrangement; it acted on **PJM's tariff**, which is unambiguously its own, and on the terms under which an *Eligible Customer* takes transmission service on the load's behalf. In June 2026 the Commission stated the boundary in its own words: it has exclusive jurisdiction over the rates, terms and conditions of interstate service to eligible customers on behalf of large loads connecting to the transmission system, while the FPA expressly reserves to the states siting and the terms of retail service.

**What this means for a reader of the utility guides.** Every tariff parameter in those guides — Dominion's 25 MW and 85%/60%, AEP's eight jurisdictions, Georgia's 100 MW rule, Xcel's 50 MW and 80% — is *state* law and is unaffected by anything in this module. What the federal layer decides is the queue the campus stands in, whether a generator may sit behind its fence, and what it owes the grid it leans on when it does.

## 4. Order No. 2023 — what it did, and the word that is not in it

**Identity.** *Improvements to Generator Interconnection Procedures and Agreements*, Order No. 2023, Docket No. RM22-14-000, **88 FR 61014** (published 6 September 2023, pages 61014–61349), **effective 6 November 2023**. On rehearing: Order No. 2023-A, Docket No. RM22-14-001, **89 FR 27006** (published 16 April 2024, pages 27006–27243), **effective 16 May 2024**, which set aside Order No. 2023 in part and clarified it. The rule amends the *pro forma* Large and Small Generator Interconnection Procedures and Agreements and applies to transmission providers, RTO/ISO and non-RTO alike.

**What it actually requires.** The order's own summary of its reforms: replace the serial first-come, first-served study process with a **first-ready, first-served cluster study process**; allocate cluster study costs by a proportional impact method; require **study deposits** and **commercial readiness deposits**; require **site control at the time of the interconnection request**; impose **withdrawal penalties**; eliminate the "reasonable efforts" standard for study deadlines and impose **financial penalties on transmission providers** for late studies; and require a transition process from the serial to the cluster regime.

The numbers, all from the adopted text rather than the proposal:

- **Study deposit, by facility size** (pro forma LGIP § 3.1.1.1): greater than 20 MW and less than 80 MW, **$35,000 + $1,000/MW**; 80 MW to less than 200 MW, **$150,000**; 200 MW and above, **$250,000**. The Commission noted these "range between $55,000 and $250,000 for the smallest and largest proposed generating facilities" and declined to raise them further, reasoning that commercial readiness deposits and site control already deter speculative requests. A single initial study deposit is collected once on entry to the cluster, not before each phase.
- **Commercial readiness deposits.** The initial deposit to enter the cluster study is **two times the study deposit**. The deposit to enter the cluster **restudy** must bring the total to **5% of the customer's network upgrade cost assignment**; the deposit to enter the **facilities study** must bring it to **10%** of that assignment.
- **Withdrawal penalty**, as adopted — the greater of the study deposit or: **2× study costs** on withdrawal during or after the initial cluster study; **5% of network upgrade costs** at the cluster restudy; **10%** at the facilities study; **20%** after executing (or requesting the filing of an unexecuted) LGIA if the agreement is terminated before commercial operation. **The Commission declined to adopt the withdrawal-penalty caps it had proposed** ($1M / $1.5M / $2M), so the later-stage penalties are uncapped. Two exemptions survive: withdrawal after a cluster study report in which assigned network upgrade costs rose **25%** against the previous report, and withdrawal after the facilities study report where they rose **more than 100%** against the cluster study. Withdrawal penalty funds are applied first to studies in the same cluster, then to offset cost increases borne by remaining customers, and only then returned.
- **Study delay penalties on the transmission provider** (new pro forma LGIP § 3.9): **$1,000 per business day** for a late cluster study, **$2,000** for a late cluster restudy, **$2,000** for a late affected system study, **$2,500** for a late facilities study. Penalties do not begin until the **third** cluster study cycle after the compliance filing's effective date, and there is a **10-business-day grace period**. The affected-system penalty applies only to affected system operators that are public utilities.

**And the fact that governs how a load should read all of it.** A full-text search of the Federal Register publication of Order No. 2023 returns **zero occurrences of "large load"** and **zero of "load interconnection"**. The two occurrences of "co-location" concern co-located *generating facilities* filing a single interconnection request. Order No. 2023-A returns zero for "large load", "load interconnection", "co-location" and "data cent". The predecessor it rebuilt, Order No. 2003, framed its scope as generating facilities **greater than 20 MW**. Three federal rulemakings across twenty years standardised how a *generator* connects; none of them addressed how a *load* does.

That absence is why the state layer grew the instruments the corpus teaches — the large-load tariff class, the CIAC, the minimum demand charge, the one-customer certificate. They exist in the space a federal rule never occupied.

## 5. The co-location record

The corpus states the co-location question twice and assigns it to FERC both times, then stops. Here is what FERC actually did with it.

**The arrangement.** A data-center campus takes power from an adjacent generating plant on the generator's side of the point of interconnection, rather than as a network customer drawing across the grid. PJM's tariff had no provisions written for this. The Commission's own definition, adopted in the show-cause order from a PJM guidance document and carried into the December 2025 order, is worth memorising because it is the operative test: **Co-Located Load is end-use customer load that is physically connected to the facilities of an existing or planned Customer Facility on the Interconnection Customer's side of the Point of Interconnection to the PJM Transmission System.**

**1 November 2024 — the rejection.** FERC rejected an amended interconnection service agreement for the Susquehanna arrangement, filed in Docket **ER24-2172**, 2–1. *PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.*, **189 FERC ¶ 61,078** (2024). The Commission's ground was narrow and procedural in form: PJM had not shown the non-conforming provisions were necessary deviations from the *pro forma* ISA, and its reliance on a generally applicable guidance document "raises questions regarding whether PJM intends to offer these terms to all similarly situated interconnection customers" (P 87). The amendment would have raised the co-located load from 300 MW to 480 MW. The rejection decided nothing about whether co-location is permissible — and that ambiguity is precisely what the next two years were spent resolving.

**20 February 2025 — the show-cause proceeding.** The Commission instituted a proceeding under FPA section 206, on its own motion, into whether PJM's tariff is unjust, unreasonable or unduly discriminatory as to co-location. *PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.*, **190 FERC ¶ 61,115** (2025), Docket **EL25-49-000**, consolidated with the technical conference docket **AD24-11-000** (*Large Loads Co-Located at Generating Facilities*) and with **EL25-20-000** (*Constellation Energy Generation, LLC v. PJM*).

**18 December 2025 — the order.** *PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.*, **193 FERC ¶ 61,217** (2025), captioned **Order on Show Cause Proceeding, Directing Compliance Filings**, 141 pages. Its two findings:

1. PJM's tariff is unjust and unreasonable because it "does not contain provisions addressing with sufficient clarity or consistency the rates, terms, and conditions of service that apply to generators serving Co-Located Load and Eligible Customers taking transmission service on behalf of Co-Located Load", and because it does not include **types of transmission service that reflect Eligible Customers taking service on behalf of Co-Located Loads that are willing and able to limit their use of the transmission system under certain conditions**. PJM must set out three options for an Eligible Customer serving a Co-Located Load: **Network Integration Transmission Service on a gross demand basis**, a new **Firm Contract Demand** service, or a new **Non-Firm Contract Demand** service. A paper hearing was established to set the rates, terms and conditions of the two new services.
2. PJM's **behind-the-meter generation** rules "are no longer just and reasonable because loads with BTMG are not fully accounted for in resource adequacy planning and shift costs onto other transmission customers contrary to the Commission's cost causation principles". PJM's BTMG netting provisions date from **2004** and are available only to entities taking NITS; PJM's own filing conceded that "20-years later cost causation principles may dictate a different outcome for co-located data center loads". PJM must propose a **materiality threshold** for how much load at an electrical location a network customer may net with BTMG, with a **three-year transition period** and grandfathering for entities with existing contracts entered for the specific purpose of effectuating a BTMG arrangement.

**Compliance.** Two tracks: a filing **within 30 days** of issuance on how interconnection customers may use provisional interconnection service, request service below nameplate capacity, accelerate the interconnection process, and use surplus interconnection service; and filings **within 60 days** (16 February 2026) on co-location procedures, Eligible Customer identification and the three transmission services. The paper hearing ran to briefs on 16 February 2026, responses 18 March 2026 and replies 17 April 2026, with PJM's proposed tariff revisions filed in Docket **ER26-1479-000**. The Commission returned to the docket at its **18 June 2026** open meeting as item E-2, covering **EL25-49-002**, EL25-49-000, AD24-11-001, EL25-20-001 and ER26-1479-000.

**What a seller takes from this.** The instrument that decides whether a generator may serve a campus behind the fence, and what that campus then owes the grid, is a **transmission service election** — gross-demand NITS, firm contract demand, or non-firm contract demand — made by an Eligible Customer on the load's behalf, priced in a FERC docket, not in a state rate case. Ancillary obligations do not follow the same basis: the December 2025 order assesses **regulation service and black start on a gross demand basis** even where the load has elected a contract-demand service.

## 6. The federal large-load standard, being built now

**23 October 2025 — DOE directs a rulemaking.** The Secretary of Energy, acting under section 403 of the Department of Energy Organization Act, transmitted a letter and an Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking directing FERC to consider reforms "to ensure the timely and orderly interconnection of large loads to the transmission system", including loads seeking to share a point of interconnection with new or existing generation. DOE's framing defined large loads generally as demand **greater than 20 MW**, and the letter asked FERC to take final action **no later than 30 April 2026**. This is a rare instrument: DOE proposing a rule to an independent commission.

**27 October 2025 — the docket opens.** FERC established **Docket No. RM26-4-000**, *Interconnection of Large Loads to the Interstate Transmission System*, and issued a Notice Inviting Comments. Per FERC's own Unified Agenda timetable (RIN 1902-AG47): Notice Inviting Comments **10/27/25**, Notice Extending Comment Deadline **11/07/25**, Comment Period End **11/21/25**, Reply Comment Period End **12/05/25**, **Next Action Undetermined 11/00/26**.

**18 June 2026 — six section 206 orders instead of a NOPR.** RM26-4-000 was item **E-1** at the 18 June 2026 open meeting. What issued were not proposed rules but six separate orders instituting FPA section 206 investigations, one per RTO/ISO and its transmission owners:

| RTO / ISO | Docket | Citation (18 June 2026) | FR notice, 24 June 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. | EL26-67-000 | 195 FERC ¶ 61,211 | 91 FR 37972 |
| Southwest Power Pool, Inc. | EL26-68-000 | 195 FERC ¶ 61,213 | 91 FR 37973 |
| New York Independent System Operator, Inc. | EL26-69-000 | 195 FERC ¶ 61,216 | 91 FR 37975 |
| Midcontinent Independent System Operator, Inc. | EL26-70-000 | 195 FERC ¶ 61,212 | 91 FR 37970 |
| California Independent System Operator Corporation | EL26-71-000 | 195 FERC ¶ 61,214 | 91 FR 37974 |
| ISO New England Inc. | EL26-72-000 | 195 FERC ¶ 61,215 | 91 FR 37968 |

Each notice records the same structure: an order issued 18 June 2026 instituting an investigation into whether that RTO's Open Access Transmission Tariff is unjust, unreasonable, unduly discriminatory or preferential; a **refund effective date equal to the date of Federal Register publication**; and intervention within **21 days of issuance**.

**The five reform categories.** The orders direct each RTO to address: (1) the application process, study procedures and ongoing operational requirements for large load transmission service, including alternative transmission technologies; (2) transparency on network upgrade costs, *pro forma* cost recovery agreements, and mechanisms to prevent cost shifting among transmission customers; (3) rates, terms and conditions for co-location arrangements; (4) transmission services for flexible large loads, co-located loads and load with behind-the-meter generation; and (5) rates, terms and conditions applicable to interconnection customers serving electrically proximate or co-located large load.

**The clock, from 18 June 2026 (a Thursday).** Intervention **+21 days = 9 July 2026**. A resource-adequacy informational report **+30 days = 18 July 2026** (a Saturday; filed the following Monday, 20 July). Abeyance requests, for RTOs intending to file proactive section 205 reforms, **+45 days = 2 August 2026** (a Sunday; 3 August). Show-cause responses **+60 days = 17 August 2026** (the 60th day, 16 August, falling on a Sunday). Stakeholder responses 30 days after each filing. **A granted abeyance is limited to 90 days, moving the filing to 15 November 2026 (a Sunday) and in practice 16 November 2026** — the date MISO and its transmission owners have committed to, and the same month FERC's own Unified Agenda projects for the next action in RM26-4.

**Cost allocation was explicitly not reopened.** The Commission did not modify wholesale cost allocation among transmission customers or transmission owners at the regional level. What it directed instead is a **standardized cost recovery agreement** under which the host load-serving entity provides sufficient financial security to make a minimum contribution toward the transmission owner's revenue requirement commensurate with the network upgrade costs — a federal instrument aimed at the same stranded-cost risk the state large-load tariff answers with a minimum demand charge and an exit fee — plus public, searchable tracking of large-load network upgrade projects and their costs.

## 7. What exists today, region by region

The show-cause orders are an investigation, not a rule. Until they resolve, what a campus faces is whatever its region has already put in its tariff — and only one region has a complete, FERC-accepted large-load interconnection process.

**SPP — the first one, and the one with prices.** SPP filed under FPA section 205 on 24 October 2025; FERC accepted the revisions **subject to condition** on **14 January 2026**, effective **15 January 2026**, with a compliance filing due in 30 days. *Southwest Power Pool, Inc.*, **194 FERC ¶ 61,031** (2026), Docket **ER26-247-000**. Two processes:

- **HILL** — the High Impact Large Load study process. A HILL is a load of **10 MW or more connected at 69 kV or below, or 50 MW or more connected above 69 kV**. An Electric Storage Resource is not a HILL. SPP justified the sub-100 kV threshold by the prevalence of 69 kV lines in its Eastern Interconnection footprint, below NERC's 100 kV Bulk Electric System floor.
- **HILLGA** — the High Impact Large Load Generation Assessment, a separate interconnection process for generation resources needed to serve a HILL.

The study-fee ladder, which is the first FERC-accepted set of study charges for a load anywhere in the corpus: on a requested HILL delivery point change SPP issues a **HILL Delivery Point Study Agreement**, and the transmission customer must return it with a **$10,000 non-refundable application fee** and a **$100,000 deposit within 30 calendar days** or the request is deemed withdrawn. SPP completes the study and issues a report **within 90 calendar days** of receiving the executed agreement, data and deposit. If an **electromagnetic-transient study** is required, an additional **$200,000 deposit**. The host transmission owner may require a **HILL Supplemental Load Connection Study** with a **$25,000** advance deposit, or may find it unnecessary given the Load Connection Study already required. Excess deposit is refunded; costs above the deposit are reimbursed by the customer.

- **CHILLS** — Conditional High Impact Large Load Service, filed 10 February 2026 as amended 29 April 2026, accepted subject to condition on **5 June 2026** effective **1 July 2026**. *Southwest Power Pool, Inc.*, **195 FERC ¶ 61,196** (2026), Dockets **ER26-1323-000** and **-001**. An "as-available" service provided from transfer capability in excess of what is needed to serve firm load, with a **minimum term of one year and a maximum term of seven years** — the seven-year cap deliberately set to push the customer toward firm long-term service. CHILLS is curtailable on a non-discriminatory basis when the system is constrained or in an emergency; **all firm service has higher priority**, and CHILLS ranks equal to monthly non-firm point-to-point service. Separate telemetry and billing are required per CHILL, along with curtailment requirements keyed to the type of supporting resource.

**MISO.** ERAS — the Expedited Resource Addition Study — is a *generation* fast path tied to load growth, not a load interconnection process: a capped, temporary queue bypass. MISO subsequently sought to raise the number of requests studied per quarterly cycle. For load, MISO's tariff (per the Commission's June 2026 finding) still studies new load at its maximum demand regardless of the load's willingness to be curtailed, with the resulting network upgrades rolled into the host transmission owner's base zonal rates. MISO's answer is a Large Load Interconnection Reliability Requirements filing and a proposed **FLITS** — Flexible Large-load Interconnection and Transmission Service — offering a faster path in exchange for non-firm service, with its full show-cause response deferred to **16 November 2026** under the abeyance.

**PJM.** No large-load interconnection process as such; a large load reaches the system as network load of a transmission owner, and the federal instrument that touches it is the co-location order's three transmission service elections plus the BTMG materiality threshold, still in compliance and paper hearing as of this writing.

**NYISO, CAISO, ISO-NE.** Each is under its own section 206 order with the same five questions and the same clock. Nothing large-load-specific was in place before June 2026 that this analysis could verify from a primary source.

**ERCOT.** Outside all of it. SB 6 and the PUCT's implementing rule are the rulebook; the `oncor` guide teaches them clause by clause and this module does not repeat them.

## 8. The large-load tariff as a class — and what the federal layer reaches

The state instruments are the corpus's own material and are not re-derived here. What follows is a one-screen reference, drawn entirely from the six utility guides, placed beside the federal question so a reader can see which column is moving.

| Utility | Threshold | Minimum demand | Term | Exit / collateral | Guide section |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dominion (GS-5) | 25 MW contiguous site at 75% load factor | 85% of T&D, 60% of generation | 14 years (4-year ramp + 10) | Exit fees equal to remaining minimums; collateral up to 60% of minimum charges for weaker credits | `dominion-energy` → *where-dominion-slots-in* |
| AEP (eight jurisdictions) | 25 MW (OH) to 150 MW (KY) | 80% to 90% | 12 to 20 years | Minimum bills for the remaining term, capped at 3–5 years | `aep` → *eight-tariffs-one-argument* |
| Southern (Georgia Power) | Above 100 MW | In the filed contract, not the rule | 15 years | Termination payments and collateral per contract | `southern-company` → *the-large-load-rule* |
| Xcel (three drafts) | 50 MW (CO, the lowest) | 80% (CO, the highest) | Multi-year | Exit fee and credit support provisions | `xcel-energy` → *a-tariff-in-three-drafts* |
| Entergy | No class — a contract inside a certificate | A revenue-requirement match, not a percentage | 15 then 20 years | Unrecovered cost or net book value; parent guaranty | `entergy` → *anatomy-of-an-order-that-names-the-customer* |
| Oncor | No tariff — SB 6 at 75 MW | None | Not a tariffed term | $50,000/MW security; 80/20 forfeiture split | `oncor` → *sb-6-clause-by-clause* |

**Read the column that is not there.** Not one of these instruments is federal, and not one of these parameters is set at FERC. What the June 2026 orders reach is a different set of terms entirely — the study process, the transparency of network upgrade costs, the cost recovery agreement with its security posted by the host load-serving entity, the service election for co-located and flexible load, and the treatment of generation that is electrically proximate to a large load. A campus can therefore be simultaneously inside a fifteen-year state take-or-pay and inside a federal proceeding about whether it may be studied at its curtailable demand rather than its nameplate.

**The single structural point.** The state tariff answers the question *what does this customer pay if it does not show up*. The federal layer is now answering *whose queue is it in, at what assumed demand, and who carries the upgrade if it never arrives*. Those are different questions with different forums, and a campus schedule can be broken by either.

## 9. The flexibility bargain

Three separate forums arrived at the same trade within eighteen months, which is the strongest signal in this record:

| Forum | Instrument | What the load gives up | What it gets |
|---|---|---|---|
| FERC / PJM, Dec 2025 | Firm and Non-Firm Contract Demand service | Contracts for less than gross demand; non-firm is curtailable when usage exceeds the contracted level | Pays for transmission on net withdrawals rather than gross demand; avoids buildout that may not be necessary |
| FERC / SPP, Jun 2026 | CHILLS | Service behind all firm service in the curtailment order; a seven-year ceiling | Connection from surplus transfer capability without waiting for network upgrades |
| Texas, SB 6 (2025) | Statutory curtailment terms | Curtailment equipment and deploy-or-curtail authority as a condition of connecting | Connection at all — see `oncor` for the clauses |

**What it does not buy.** In every one of the three, firm service keeps priority and the flexible product is explicitly the lesser right. CHILLS's seven-year cap exists to stop a customer from living on it. None of the three reduces what the state tariff charges. And a load taking a contract-demand service in PJM is still assessed regulation and black start on gross demand.

**Where a storage or on-site generation product sits.** The bargain is only available to a load that can actually curtail or firm itself, which is a physical capability before it is a contractual one. That is the commercial opening these dockets create: a campus that wants the faster answer needs something behind the meter that lets it hold to a contract demand — and the ancillary-service treatment above says that something is not costless, which is a more honest pitch than "batteries make the interconnection problem go away".

## 10. What it means for the active engagement (grid-scale storage into AI data-center power)

Four usable consequences, none of which requires the reader to have an opinion about FERC.

1. **Stop saying "FERC's 2023 interconnection reform" to a load customer.** It is a generator rule and the customer's counsel will know it. The accurate version is more useful anyway: Order No. 2023 sets how fast *new supply* reaches the grid the campus wants to draw from, and the rulebook for the campus's own connection was empty until June 2026.
2. **Know which regulator owns the delay before proposing a product against it.** A study queue in one of the six jurisdictional RTOs is federal and is being rewritten this quarter, under dockets with dates; the same delay in ERCOT or behind a vertically integrated utility's own queue is a state matter on a different clock. A bridge-power or storage proposal aimed at the wrong clock is a proposal with no deadline in it.
3. **The flexibility bargain is the storage-adjacent opening, and it has a price.** All three forums are offering speed for curtailability. A product that lets a campus hold a contract demand — storage, on-site generation, load shaping — is the physical precondition for taking that bargain. The honest framing includes that firm service keeps priority, that non-firm has a term ceiling where SPP's rules apply, and that some ancillary charges stay on gross demand regardless.
4. **The first federal study numbers are now quotable.** A HILL study in SPP costs $10,000 non-refundable plus a $100,000 deposit, $200,000 more for an EMT study, $25,000 for a supplemental study — against Texas's statutory $100,000/$300,000 plus actual costs. Both are small next to the deposits at risk on the generation side, where the entry deposit alone runs to $250,000 and a late-stage withdrawal costs 20% of assigned network upgrade costs, uncapped. The asymmetry is worth naming: on the generation side FERC has priced speculation heavily; on the load side the pricing barely exists yet.

## 12. What the record does NOT say

1. **There is no federal large-load interconnection rule.** As of this writing there is a directed ANOPR, an open docket (RM26-4-000) whose next action FERC's own agenda records as undetermined, and six section 206 investigations. A NOPR has not issued; no *pro forma* large-load interconnection procedure exists. Anyone describing "FERC's large-load interconnection rule" is describing something that has not been written.
2. **The show-cause orders' own text was not read directly for this module.** FERC's website returned HTTP 403 from this environment for every path, and eLibrary redirects. The six orders are established from the Federal Register notices instituting each proceeding (which give docket, issue date, citation and refund effective date from a primary source) plus consistent law-firm and analyst summaries for their content. The five reform categories, the 50 MW / 69 kV suggested definition, and the cost-recovery-agreement directive are therefore **secondary** and are labelled so in the ledger.
3. **The 50 MW / above-69 kV threshold is FERC's suggestion, not a rule, and two summaries of the same orders disagree about whether any threshold is stated at all.** Two law-firm alerts report no MW or kV threshold in the orders; one analyst summary and RMI report the 50 MW / 69 kV figure as the Commission's suggested definition. This module states it as a suggestion under investigation and never as a binding threshold. The only thresholds it states as law are SPP's (10 MW / 50 MW, in an accepted tariff) and SB 6's 75 MW.
4. **The FERC citation for the PJM show-cause order is 195 FERC ¶ 61,211 on the primary source and 195 FERC ¶ 61,209 in one secondary summary.** The Federal Register notice for Docket EL26-67-000 ties ¶ 61,211 to that docket explicitly, so this module uses the Federal Register. The discrepancy is recorded rather than smoothed over.
5. **What each RTO actually filed on 17 August 2026 is not established here.** MISO and ISO-NE are reported to have taken the abeyance. The contents of the responses, and whether any RTO filed section 205 reforms instead, could not be verified from a primary source in this environment and are deliberately absent from the module.
6. **No dollar figure exists for a large-load study in PJM, NYISO, CAISO or ISO-NE.** SPP's HILL deposits are the only FERC-accepted load study fees this research could find. It would be wrong to generalise $10,000 + $100,000 to any other region.
7. **The outcome of the PJM paper hearing is not known.** The December 2025 order established the two new Contract Demand services and set a hearing to determine their rates, terms and conditions. The rates are not set. Any specific price for firm or non-firm contract demand service in PJM is, as of this writing, not a real number.
8. **The BTMG materiality threshold is a directive, not a number.** FERC told PJM to propose one; it did not set one. Any specific megawatt figure attributed to FERC for BTMG netting is unsourced.
9. **Nothing here says whether co-location is lawful in general.** The Commission acted on PJM's tariff and on the terms of transmission service taken on a co-located load's behalf. It expressly did not assert blanket jurisdiction over co-location arrangements, and the jurisdictional argument Talen made — that doing so would "improperly federalize the entire electric system" — remains on the record rather than resolved.
10. **This module does not forecast.** It states dockets, dates and the deadlines that follow from them. It does not predict what the six responses will produce, whether RM26-4 will become a rule, or when any campus's connection date will move.
