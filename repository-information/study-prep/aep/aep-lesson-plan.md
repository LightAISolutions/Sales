# AEP — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach how a utility holding company that spans three regional markets actually works — what changes when the same corporate decision produces a certification docket in one state, a capacity-auction cost in another and a private financing question in a third; what a large-load tariff is as an instrument rather than a price, and the four terms that make one; the single public measurement of what a tariff does to an interconnection queue; why a contracted gigawatt in one market is a different object from a contracted gigawatt in another; the three ways this company has to buy capacity; why transmission voltage is a strategic choice with a physics argument and a supply-chain constraint behind it; how one 765 kV capability gets authorized through four different planning processes with four different answers to who pays; and where a seller of turbines, transformers or batteries actually stands in each. Starting from high-school STEM and from the Dominion lesson plan, which this one does not repeat. No company trivia: founding dates, executives and share counts stay in the dossier. ## Module 1 — Two axes: the state and the market

**The single idea:** every question about this company has two answers, one set by a state commission and one set by a regional market, and collapsing them produces nonsense.

1. **The structure.** Seven regulated operating companies plus a transmission business, about 5.6 million customers across eleven states, roughly 38,000 circuit miles of transmission and 252,000 circuit miles of distribution, approximately 25,400 MW of owned generation of which about 10,700 MW is coal. The Entergy plan taught one parent with five operating companies under one grid operator; here the same structure runs under three.
2. **The retail axis — the state.** Eight commissions decide rates, certificates and the terms on which a large load connects. Two states are restructured, so AEP Ohio and AEP Texas own no generation and are wires-only utilities of exactly the kind the Oncor plan describes. Six states remain traditional, so Appalachian Power, Indiana Michigan Power, Kentucky Power, PSO and SWEPCO are vertically integrated and still certify plants. One company, two regulatory shapes.
3. **The wholesale axis — the market.** Four PJM companies, two SPP companies, one ERCOT company. PJM runs a capacity market clearing three years ahead. SPP places a capacity obligation on each member without a centralized auction. ERCOT has no capacity product at all. A sentence about "AEP's load growth" therefore describes at least three different commercial mechanisms, and a seller who learned one has learned a third of the company.
4. **What is common.** The wires. About 2,000 circuit miles at 765 kV — by the company's account nearly ninety percent of the national total, built starting in 1969 — earning under FERC formula rates that update annually and producing, per the company, more than half of operating earnings. Transmission is the same business in every RTO, which is what makes a company this regulatorily fragmented coherent as an investment.
5. **The row to memorize.** For any territory, ask two questions before anything else: does this operating company own generation, and which market is it in? The answers determine whether there is a resource plan to sell into, whether a merchant business case exists, and who the buyer is.

**Self-check:** a developer wants to sell a utility-scale battery into AEP Ohio and into Appalachian Power. Which conversation is possible and why? (Appalachian Power — it is vertically integrated and runs solicitations, including a planned Virginia standalone storage RFP. AEP Ohio owns no generation and runs none.)

## Module 2 — The tariff as an instrument, and what it does to a queue

**The single idea:** a large-load tariff is a risk-transfer document, not a price list, and it is built from four dials that every state sets differently.

1. **Why a utility wants one.** Without it, a load forecast is a collection of unpriced options. A utility that builds against unpriced options strands the cost on everyone else, which is the political failure that ends a growth story. The tariff converts a request the utility cannot verify into an obligation it can finance against.
2. **The four dials, plus the one that gets quoted.** *The term* — twelve to twenty years here — matters because it is compared against the depreciation life of what gets built; twelve years covers a substation comfortably and a combined-cycle plant badly, which is why the states underwriting plants wrote longer terms. *The ramp period* — four years in Ohio, up to five in Indiana — exists because a campus energizes in halls, not at once. *Credit support* is the collateral: cash, a letter of credit or a parent guaranty, usually waived only above a stated credit rating. *The exit fee* is what is owed for leaving early, commonly the remaining minimum charges capped at some years. And *the minimum demand charge* — the take-or-pay percentage — is the number everyone quotes and the one that means least in isolation.
3. **The eight-state comparison.** Ohio: 25 MW, 12 years including a four-year ramp, 85 percent, exit after year five capped at three years. Indiana: 70 MW site or 150 MW aggregated, 12–17 years, 80 percent. Michigan: 50 MW, 15–20 years, 90 percent. Virginia: 100 MW site or 150 MW aggregated, 14 years including a four-year ramp, 80 percent. West Virginia: same thresholds, 12–17 years, 80 percent. Kentucky: 150 MW, 20 years, 90 percent — the heaviest instrument in the set. Oklahoma and SWEPCO-Texas were pending as of mid-2026. And AEP Texas has no tariff at all: SB 6 governs and the customer posts statutory security. Read a row across, never a column down.
4. **The footnote that matters.** The company's own table notes that Indiana, Michigan, Texas, Virginia and West Virginia minimum-bill calculations include an energy component as well as a demand component. In five of eight jurisdictions the headline percentage understates the obligation.
5. **What a tariff does to a queue, measured once.** Ohio produced the only clean before-and-after in the corpus, because AEP Ohio had to report its contracting. Roughly 30,000 MW of original pre-tariff requests became 17,861 MW of contracted projects — 5,642 MW signed under the new tariff plus 12,219 MW of pre-tariff contracts — with 13,022.7 MW proceeding to engineering study, on a system whose peak is roughly 8,000 to 10,500 MW. About two fifths of the stated demand did not convert once conversion meant an 85 percent obligation for twelve years.
6. **Read the subtraction correctly.** This is not evidence that the requests were dishonest. It is evidence that a free request and a committed one are different objects. And notice what survived: contracted load is still roughly twice the system's peak, so the tariff filtered the queue without shrinking the problem. Its function is to make the remaining demand financeable, which is why the capital plan and the contracted-load ladder move together in every release.
7. **The customer's side.** A signed minimum-demand obligation is what converts a queue position into a construction schedule the utility will actually staff. From the developer's seat the tariff is the price of a date — which is why an eight-state franchise of these instruments is a competitive asset rather than a compliance burden.

**Self-check:** two tariffs, one at 85 percent for twelve years, one at 90 percent for twenty. Which is heavier, and what else do you need to know? (The twenty-year one, on those numbers — but you also need the ramp, the collateral and the exit terms, and whether the minimum bill includes an energy component.)

## Module 3 — Contracted versus pipeline, and three ways to buy capacity

**The single idea:** the headline gigawatt figure hides two distinctions — contracted against queued, and which instrument the contract actually is — and the company operates under three different capacity constructs at once.

1. **The two numbers.** Contracted load by 2030 stood at 69 GW backed by fully executed agreements as of July 2026; active projects in the interconnection queue stood at about 195 GW. Merging them overstates the committed position roughly threefold. The ladder is worth memorizing as the clearest public record of how fast this market moved: more than 20 GW in February 2025, 24 GW in July, 28 GW in October, 56 GW in February 2026, 63 GW in May, 69 GW in July.
2. **Then split the contracted number by instrument.** The company discloses contract form by market. In PJM: 100 percent letters of agreement and about 95 percent executed electric service agreements. In SPP: 100 percent letters and about 55 percent service agreements. In ERCOT: 100 percent letters of agreement only, because the market's contracting structure permits nothing else. And ERCOT is 45 of the 69 gigawatts.
3. **So the largest tranche rests on the weakest instrument and the strongest collateral.** A letter of agreement is a commitment to proceed, not a tariffed service obligation. What stands behind the ERCOT tranche is cash: approximately $2 billion in cash, corporate and parental guarantees and letters of credit, received in July 2026 for the 40 GW submitted into the grid operator's batch process. Whether that beats a filed tariff obligation is a real question; the honest answer is that it is different — money now against enforceable terms later.
4. **The event that tests it.** In August 2026 the Texas grid operator paused the batch process and began an audit of the projects in it, with a verification report due that December and the prior study timeline to be reassessed. Two thirds of the contracted book sits behind a process that was suspended at the time of writing, and it is not a risk the company controls.
5. **Three capacity constructs.** *PJM:* a capacity auction clearing three years ahead against a zonal reliability requirement, resources credited at their effective load carrying capability. Two consecutive auctions cleared at the cap, the later procuring thousands of megawatts below the reliability requirement — a price signal that is screaming and a queue that cannot answer it. *SPP:* a capacity obligation on each member, met with owned or contracted accredited capacity; no public clearing price, so cost is whatever the utility's own procurement costs and it is tested in a state prudence review. *ERCOT:* nothing at all — scarcity pricing to an offer cap, nobody obliged to build, no plant certified.
6. **Who the effective buyer is in each.** PJM: the market signals and the queue delivers, with utilities under the Fixed Resource Requirement carrying their own capacity and insulated from the clearing price in both directions. SPP: the state commission, through utility all-source RFPs and pre-approval. ERCOT: a private investor, which is why on-site generation and merchant storage carry the load-growth story there and why the utility is not the counterparty.

**Self-check:** you are told AEP has "69 gigawatts of contracted data-center load." Name three questions before you use the number. (Contracted or queued? Which instrument — service agreement or letter of agreement — and in which market? And what is the status of the process that has to study it?)

## Module 4 — Why voltage is a strategy, and four ways to authorize the same line

**The single idea:** 765 kV is a physics argument, a manufacturing constraint and a franchise — and the same line gets built through four different processes with four different answers to who pays.

1. **The physics, from first principles.** Power delivered is voltage times current. The heat wasted in a conductor rises with the *square* of the current. So carrying the same power at a higher voltage means proportionally less current and dramatically less loss, and more power through the same right-of-way. The company's own figures: a single 765 kV circuit carries up to six times the power of a 345 kV line, with about half the transmission losses.
2. **The costs.** Taller towers, wider corridors, greater clearances — and transformers, breakers and series compensation built by a very short list of manufacturers with multi-year backlogs. This is why the November 2025 agreement with Quanta Services pairs a 765 kV construction relationship with a development agreement to expand *domestic manufacturing capacity* for extra-high-voltage transformers and circuit breakers. The constraint is not the permit or the crew; it is the tank. A transmission award is not a delivery slot.
3. **The franchise.** The world's first 765 kV line was energized in 1969; today the company operates more than 2,100 miles and about 30 substations at that voltage across six states, by its account nearly ninety percent of the national total. Sixty years of design practice, spare parts, protection settings and trained crews is a genuine barrier, and the company monetizes it — describing itself as working with other investor-owned utilities to build their 765 kV capability, and entering joint ventures rather than bidding alone.
4. **Four planning processes, one capability.** *PJM's regional plan:* the operator identifies needs from its load forecast and opens competitive windows; the 2024 window produced a $6.7 billion plan and the 2025 window an $11.8 billion plan, with AEP's share taken through the Valley Link and Grid Growth Ventures joint ventures. *SPP's integrated transmission plan:* an annual portfolio against a regional benefit-cost test, whose 2025 slate ran to about $8.6 billion across 50 projects including 949 miles of new 765 kV. *MISO's plan:* long-range portfolios in tranches, one of which selected a nearly 200-mile 765 kV project into Wisconsin. *ERCOT:* no regional competitive window — the state commission chose 765 kV for the Permian Basin in April 2025 and assigned the import paths, with AEP Texas building roughly 300 miles.
5. **And a fifth route that avoids all of them.** At the Piketon campus in Ohio, the developer committed to pay the entire $4.2 billion of transmission directly. There is nothing to allocate, so there is no window, no benefit-cost test and no allocation fight — and the project sits outside the utility's capital plan entirely.
6. **Who pays, argued honestly.** *Spread it regionally:* the backbone is a network good, power does not respect state lines, and no single state could justify a corridor whose value is regional — but a state whose customers pay heavily for growth elsewhere has a political problem its own regulator cannot solve, one estimate putting West Virginia ratepayers at more than $440 million over 40 years for two lines, and the allocation formula is decided at FERC where a state commission has no standing to object. *Make the beneficiary pay:* politically survivable, it filters the plan, and it has been demonstrated at scale — but tracing benefit on a meshed network is genuinely hard, customer-funded builds only work for one very large load with one very large balance sheet, and front-loading cost pushes development to the next region or behind the meter where the planner cannot see it.

**Self-check:** a region announces it will build 765 kV rather than more 345 kV. What does that tell a transformer manufacturer, and on what timeline? (That the addressable market steps up discontinuously, and that the equipment order precedes construction by years — the lead time, not the award, is the binding date.)

## Module 5 — AEP against the chain: where the sale actually is

**The single idea:** three different desks buy three different things on three different clocks, and the one a storage seller wants is neither of the first two.

1. **The chain, rung by rung.** *The plan:* state resource plans in the five integrated states, the capital plan for the wires, and the regional transmission plans above all of them — different documents with different buyers, and none for generation in Ohio or Texas. *The customer contract:* a large-load tariff in seven jurisdictions, a letter of agreement under SB 6 in ERCOT; this is where the campus's own electrical design is settled. *The certificate:* plant certificates in the integrated states, a line certificate in Texas, RTO board approval for regional transmission — three authorizing bodies for what is physically the same construction. *The solicitation:* all-source and technology-specific RFPs at Appalachian Power, Indiana Michigan Power and PSO, roughly 12,100 MW outstanding — and none at the two wires-only companies. *The rate order:* eight commissions, with Ohio moving to triennial cases after 2028 and Texas using an annual tracker. *Interconnection:* generator queues in PJM and SPP, a large-load batch process in ERCOT, plus the utility's own load studies. *The regional layer:* the three capacity constructs from Module 3.
2. **Three desks.** *Turbines* are reserved at the parent, years ahead: approximately 13 GW secured through 2031 with options on up to 10 GW more through 2035, which trade press attributes to GE Vernova and Mitsubishi. That market is effectively closed for the decade. *Transmission equipment* is bought by the transmission business against regional awards, on multi-year manufacturer lead times, with a development agreement in place to expand the domestic supply. *Batteries* are bought through operating-company solicitations in the integrated states only — the smallest line in the plan, at 1,808 MW of approved-but-not-in-service renewables and storage containing a single 224 MW PSO project, with no supplier named anywhere.
3. **The fuel-cell exception.** Up to 1 GW contracted with Bloom Energy from November 2024; about 98 MW delivered as onsite generation for two Ohio data-center customers under contracts where the customers bear all costs; then approximately $2.65 billion committed in January 2026 for a Wyoming facility with a twenty-year offtake — held in an unregulated subsidiary because Ohio's HB 15 closed the regulated version of that channel prospectively. It was bought because it is firm and fast, not because it is storage.
4. **The financing governor.** The $78 billion plan sits against a funds-from-operations-to-debt metric the company reports at 13.9 percent versus a 13 percent downgrade threshold, with one operating company already on negative outlook. The answers so far — a $2.82 billion sale of 19.9 percent of two transmission companies to KKR and PSP, $3 billion of equity forwards, a $3.5 billion at-the-market programme and roughly $5 billion of federal loans — are why the plan is fundable, and also why reported earnings fell year over year: the minority interest is a permanent deduction. Read the capital plan as demand and the credit metrics as the governor on it.
5. **Where it fails, in one line each.** The date moves in three different ways, and two thirds of the contracted book sits behind a paused process. The instrument is weaker than the number sounds. The Ohio template is on appeal. The capacity price is set by an RTO and lands on the utility as an affordability fight in four states. The balance sheet, not the demand, is the binding constraint. The equipment, not the permit, sets the schedule. And the last one: sold to the wrong desk.

**Self-check:** a battery integrator asks you where to start with this company. Name the door and the two places that are closed. (Start with an all-source or standalone storage solicitation at a vertically integrated operating company — Appalachian Power's Virginia storage RFP or PSO's all-source RFP. Closed: the two wires-only companies, which own no generation, and the parent's turbine reservation programme, which is a different desk and a different product.)

## Whole-plan self-test

1. Name the two axes that determine what can be sold into an AEP territory. (Whether the operating company owns generation, set by the state; and which market it is in, set by the RTO.)
2. What are the four terms that make a large-load tariff an instrument, and which fifth number gets quoted instead? (Term, ramp period, credit support, exit fee; the minimum demand percentage.)
3. Why did tariff terms lengthen as utilities moved from building wires to building plants? (The term is measured against the depreciation life of the asset being underwritten.)
4. What did Ohio measure, and what does the ratio mean? (About 30,000 MW of pre-tariff requests became 17,861 MW of contracts — roughly two fifths did not convert once a take-or-pay obligation applied.)
5. Distinguish a letter of agreement from an executed service agreement, and say where each dominates. (A commitment to proceed versus a tariffed obligation; ERCOT permits only letters, PJM is about 95 percent service agreements.)
6. Give the three capacity constructs and the effective buyer of new supply in each. (PJM auction — the market signals, the queue delivers; SPP obligation — the state commission through utility RFPs; ERCOT energy-only — a private investor.)
7. State the physical case for 765 kV in one sentence. (Higher voltage means less current for the same power, and loss rises with the square of current — six times the throughput at about half the losses on the same right-of-way.)
8. What is the binding constraint on a 765 kV programme, and how do you know? (Extra-high-voltage transformers and breakers — the company signed a development agreement to expand domestic manufacturing capacity rather than simply ordering more.)
9. Name the four planning processes that can authorize the same line and the fifth route that avoids them all. (PJM's regional plan, SPP's integrated plan, MISO's plan, a state process in ERCOT — and a single customer funding it directly.)
10. Why can this company's reported earnings fall in a year its business grew? (A minority interest sale raises cash without issuing shares, and the outside investors' share of subsidiary earnings is permanently deducted.)
