# Albemarle — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** the chemistry and industrial economics of lithium and bromine, and where both land inside a battery-storage installation.
**Assumed starting point:** high-school STEM. No prior battery or chemicals knowledge.
**What this is not:** a company crib sheet. Founding dates, executives and share counts stay in the dossier.

**Suggested pacing.** Six modules. Modules 1–3 are the lithium chain and can be read in one sitting of about an hour. Module 4 is a hard stop — it is the commercial mechanics and rewards a second pass. Modules 5–6 are the bromine chain, which is genuinely separate chemistry and can be taken on a different day.

**The one sentence to hold onto:** Albemarle sells the material that stores the energy and the material that stops the box around it catching fire, and neither is a product it can price.

---

## Module 1 — What lithium does, and why nothing displaced it

**Concepts to acquire:** intercalation · energy density · specific capacity · why the periodic table decides this.

Start with the physical picture. A cell is two materials that both want lithium ions, separated so ions travel between them only through a liquid electrolyte while electrons must go the long way round through the load. That external electron journey is the current.

The property that makes it rechargeable is **intercalation** — ions slot into gaps in a host structure without destroying it. Emphasise the counterfactual: a reaction that consumed the host would give you a battery that works once. Primary cells are exactly that.

Why lithium: third element, lightest metal, so minimal dead mass per unit charge; and it releases its outer electron more readily than nearly anything, which sets the cell voltage. Light plus high voltage is what energy density rewards.

**The commercial consequence to draw out:** lithium is the ion, not the structure. Every chemistry needs it. So a lithium producer sells into total battery demand and does not need to bet on which chemistry wins — which is a comfortable position in growth and a brutal one when supply is built all at once, because the product is undifferentiated.

*Check for understanding:* why is a lithium producer less exposed to the LFP-versus-nickel argument than a cathode producer?

## Module 2 — Where lithium comes from, and why the source is the cost

**Concepts:** spodumene · brine · evaporation ponds · direct lithium extraction · LCE as a unit.

Two industries, not two grades. Teach them as separate businesses.

**Hard rock.** A mineral in granite-like rock. Mined conventionally, crushed, concentrated on site to a 6% product using density and surface-chemistry separations, then shipped — often across an ocean — to a chemical plant that converts it. Two plants, frequently two owners, two countries, two separately competed margins.

**Brine.** Salty groundwater under a salt flat, lithium at a few hundred parts per million. Pumped into shallow ponds where the sun removes the water over twelve to twenty-four months. Lowest cash cost in the industry because the energy is free.

**The three consequences worth deriving rather than stating:**
1. *Response time.* Brine is a pipeline eighteen months long. Today's output was decided long before today's price. Hard rock responds within a quarter. In a price spike these are completely different assets.
2. *Water.* Evaporation consumes the brine and, arguably, the aquifer. This is why extraction and local water rights become the same argument, and why most litigation about lithium is really about water.
3. *DLE.* Pull lithium out selectively in hours instead of months, recover more of it, return most of the water — by replacing free sunlight with a chemical plant you pay for. Have the learner articulate the trade before reading the answer.

Introduce **LCE** here as a unit of account: every lithium product restated as the equivalent quantity of lithium carbonate so that quantities can be added and compared. Note the conversion Albemarle publishes — 0.1878 tonnes of lithium metal per tonne of LCE — as an example of why a unit convention is necessary at all.

## Module 3 — Carbonate or hydroxide: the furnace decides

**Concepts:** lithium carbonate · lithium hydroxide · cathode · LFP · NMC and NCA.

This is the module that connects the company to this corpus, and it turns on one manufacturing detail.

A cell maker buys one of two compounds. Carbonate is the lithium source for **LFP**; hydroxide is the source for nickel-rich cathodes. The reason is temperature: nickel-rich cathode material is fired at a lower temperature, and at that temperature carbonate does not break down and react cleanly. Hydroxide does. It is not a preference — it is the furnace.

Now overlay the market. Stationary storage is overwhelmingly LFP: cheaper, more heat-tolerant, far better cycle life — and cycle life dominates for a battery cycling daily, in a way it does not for a car. **Therefore grid-storage demand is carbonate demand.**

**The reading skill to teach here:** watch which plants a producer *closes*. A company idling hydroxide conversion while running carbonate plants at record output has told you where it thinks demand is going, more reliably than any strategy slide. Have the learner find that pattern in the dossier's recent developments before being told it is there.

## Module 4 — Price-taking, the cost curve, and why guidance is a table

**Concepts:** cost curve · marginal tonne · index-referenced pricing · progressive royalty.

The hard stop. Everything here generalises to cells, modules and graphite.

Line producers up cheapest to most expensive; draw demand as a vertical line. Price settles near the cost of the last producer needed. Two consequences: a low-cost producer's margin is set by *someone else's* costs, and closures are the signal — idling a plant is a statement that it sits above the price.

Then work the published guidance. Albemarle guides in three price scenarios: about US$10/kg LCE gives US$0.9–1.0bn of adjusted EBITDA; about US$20/kg gives US$2.4–2.6bn; about US$30/kg gives US$4.2–4.4bn. Ask the learner what publishing a table rather than a number concedes. Answer: that the dominant variable is exogenous, and a point estimate would be false precision.

Then the sharp practical point: in September 2026 the market price sat essentially *on* the middle scenario. A producer guiding at a price the market has already reached has no cushion at all. **For a commodity producer, the price assumption is the real guidance.**

Finish with the least visible constraint: a **progressive royalty** on final sale price. Where a resource-holder takes a share that rises with price — reported to reach roughly 40% above US$10,000/t in the Chilean arrangement — the producer's participation in a recovery is capped in a way that never appears as its own line item. Teach the habit: always ask what the resource-holder takes, and how that share changes with price.

## Module 5 — Switching elements: what a flame retardant does to a flame

**Concepts:** bromine · brominated flame retardant · radical scavenging · UL 94.

Genuinely different chemistry, and worth teaching from first principles because the mechanism is counter-intuitive.

Burning is not "hot" — it is a self-sustaining chain reaction. Heat cracks molecules into highly reactive radicals, mostly hydrogen and hydroxyl; those attack more fuel and make more radicals and more heat. Remove the radicals and the chain breaks even though fuel and heat remain.

A brominated flame retardant is inert in the plastic at normal temperature. When the plastic starts decomposing, the additive releases bromine-containing species into the gas just above the surface, and those species scavenge the radicals. **It works in the flame, not in the plastic.**

Make the learner state what it is *not*: not insulation, not a substance that stops the plastic melting. The plastic still degrades under enough heat. What changes is that a small ignition — a spark at a connector, an overheating trace — fails to become self-sustaining.

Then **UL 94**, the standard that classifies how a plastic part behaves under an applied flame. This is the demand driver: electrical equipment must meet a rating, and for most engineering plastics meeting it means a retardant is present. This is the whole reason plastic is permitted near live electrical parts.

*Follow the demand:* this additive tracks electronics manufacturing, not battery manufacturing. Boards, cable, connectors, enclosures — which is data centres and storage installations alike.

## Module 6 — Both elements, one container; and where the reasoning fails

Walk a single containerised battery system and place each material: carbonate in the cells; graphite in the anode (a different supply chain — see the NOVONIX plan); bromine in the board laminate, the cable insulation, the connectors and the enclosure. Two unrelated chemistries arriving at different parts of the same box is the coherent reason this company belongs in a storage corpus at all.

**Then the failure modes, which are the most transferable content in the plan:**

- **A low-cost asset is not a low-cost company.** Mining and conversion are separate steps with separate economics. A cheap resource feeding an expensive conversion plant makes an expensive product — which is why conversion plants close while the mines feeding them keep running.
- **Volume growth and revenue growth differ by an order of magnitude in a commodity.** Read tonnes and prices separately; never revenue alone.
- **Ranking claims outlive the disclosure supporting them.** When a producer stops publishing tonnage, league tables become estimates presented as measurements. Check whether a number has a source or only a history.
- **Regulatory risk is asymmetric between the two legs.** The specific flame-retardant molecules have been under environmental review for years, and a restriction does not care how well the chemistry works. Nothing equivalent applies on the lithium side.
- **Accounting tells you what a divestiture was for.** A sale booked at a loss, preceded by impairments taken specifically to write the business down to its sale price, and expressly *not* treated as a discontinued operation, was done for the balance sheet rather than for value. Read the treatment, not the release.
