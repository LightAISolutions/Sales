# Amperesand — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Amperesand (private; NTU Singapore spin-off building silicon-carbide solid-state transformers for AI data centres, megawatt EV and port charging, and grid storage) · **Written:** 2026-09-19 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM physics, no electrical-engineering background.

**What this teaches.** The solid-state transformer as a *platform* rather than a single product: why the same stack of silicon-carbide converter cells can charge a 40-tonne port vehicle at a megawatt, hold a data hall's 800 V DC bus, and buffer a battery on the grid; what silicon carbide is and why the device's voltage class decides how many cells a vendor must build; what "five nines" actually asserts and why a prototype cannot prove it; how the Megawatt Charging System turned truck and port charging into a medium-voltage problem; and where a 34.5 kV-to-800 V DC unit slots into the AI hall NVIDIA has specified.

**Why this gap.** The Heron Power guide already teaches the SST as a machine — the transformer equation, the three-stage cascaded architecture, the SST-versus-TRU choice — and this guide does not repeat it. What the corpus does not yet teach is the *other* half of the SST story: the applications the technology was proven on before AI (megawatt charging, port electrification, storage), the silicon-carbide device physics underneath every cell, the reliability arithmetic behind an uptime claim, and what an Asia-headquartered, Temasek-backed vendor selling into US halls has to answer that a California one does not.

**Its place in the power-conversion set.** A sibling of the Heron Power guide at the hall edge. Heron owns the stage-by-stage physics and the DC fault ledger; this guide owns silicon carbide, the multi-application platform argument, availability and the megawatt-charging market. Read the Heron guide's Modules 2–4 first if the cascaded-cell architecture is new.

## Module 1 — The SST as a platform, not a product
A conventional transformer does one thing: it changes an AC voltage by a fixed ratio. A solid-state transformer is a converter system — rectify, chop to kilohertz, isolate through a small transformer, rectify again — and every one of those stages is controlled by software. That is why one hardware stack can be *configured* rather than *redesigned* for different jobs: the medium-voltage input is the same whether the load is a charger, a data hall or a battery; what changes is the output voltage the controller regulates, the direction it lets power flow, and the behaviour it shows the grid. The economic consequence is the module's core idea: a vendor that proves a cell design in one market (charging a port's vehicles, say) carries the same certified cell, the same magnetics and the same manufacturing line into the next (AI halls) with the controller and the enclosure as the delta. Contrast this with the Heron Power guide's single-application unit and ask what a platform vendor gives up — a design optimised for one duty cycle — and what it gains — field hours accumulating across every application at once.

## Module 2 — Silicon carbide: the material every cell is switched with
Every switch in an SST cell is a transistor, and the material it is made of sets three things at once: how much voltage one device can block, how fast it can switch, and how much energy it wastes doing so. Silicon carbide (SiC) is a *wide-bandgap* semiconductor — its electrons need about three times more energy than silicon's to be freed into conduction — so the same thickness of SiC blocks roughly ten times the electric field. A SiC MOSFET rated 1.7 kV or 3.3 kV can therefore be built thin, switch in tens of nanoseconds, and run hot, where a silicon IGBT of the same rating is slow and lossy. Two consequences drive the whole product category. First, *cell count*: the peak line-to-neutral voltage at 34.5 kV is about 28 kV, and after the customary derating a 1.7 kV device needs roughly ten cells per phase in series, a 3.3 kV device about five — so the device class a vendor buys sets its parts count, cost and failure points (see the Heron guide's Module 3 for the arithmetic). Second, *switching frequency*: the faster the switch, the higher the kilohertz the medium-frequency transformer can run at and the smaller it gets, but the faster edges stress insulation and radiate noise. Teach the trade-off with the two loss types — conduction loss, which rises with current squared, and switching loss, which rises with frequency — and why SiC lowers both. Close with the supply chain: SiC wafers are grown in a handful of fabs (Wolfspeed, Infineon, onsemi, ROHM, STMicro), 200 mm wafers only arrived recently, and a vendor's choice of supplier is a bankability and tariff question as much as an engineering one.

## Module 3 — Megawatt charging: how trucks and ports became a medium-voltage problem
A passenger-car fast charger delivers 150–350 kW through the Combined Charging System (CCS) connector at up to 1,000 V. A 40-tonne truck or a port prime mover carrying a 500–900 kWh battery cannot wait hours, so the industry wrote a new standard: the **Megawatt Charging System** (SAE J3271, published March 2025; IEC 63379), a liquid-cooled connector rated 1,250 V DC and 3,000 A — 3.75 MW through one plug. Work the arithmetic: a depot of ten such chargers is a 20–40 MW load, the size of a small factory, and utilities serve loads of that size at *medium voltage* (12–35 kV), not at the 480 V a garage is wired for. The conventional way to reach it is a step-down transformer, low-voltage switchgear, then a rectifier per charger — three pieces of kit, each with its own losses, footprint and lead time. An SST collapses them: medium voltage in, regulated DC out, and because every cell's output is a controllable DC source the same unit can feed several dispensers at different voltages and currents at once, sharing power dynamically as vehicles arrive and leave. Ports are the sharpest case: Singapore's Tuas terminal is converting its automated guided vehicles and prime movers to battery power with centrally scheduled charging, and ships at berth draw *shore power* — cold ironing — from the same grid connection. Teach why reliability expectations differ here: J3271 targets better than 99.99% first-attempt charge starts, because a port vehicle idle at a dead charger stops a crane.

## Module 4 — Five nines: what an availability claim asserts and how it is earned
Vendors quote 'five nines' — 99.999% availability, about five minutes of downtime a year. The module teaches what that number is made of and why a prototype cannot prove it. Availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR): mean time between failures divided by that plus mean time to repair. Both are *fleet statistics*: MTBF is estimated from parts counts and confirmed only by accumulated field hours across many units, and MTTR is a service-model question — is a spare cell on site, can a failed cell be bypassed while the unit keeps running? That second question is where the cascaded architecture earns its reliability: with N + 1 or N + 2 cells per phase, a cell failure is a maintenance event, not an outage, so the *unit's* availability can exceed any single cell's. Work an example with the hundreds of SiC devices in a 5 MW unit, a plausible device failure rate, and the redundancy that turns it into an acceptable unit MTBF. Then teach the honest reading: a five-nines claim on a product with a few dozen field units is a design target derived from a model, and the customers who care most — data-centre operators writing SLAs, ports whose cranes stop — will ask for the field-hour evidence behind it. This is also why platform vendors count hours across every application: a charger running 24/7 at a port is accumulating the reliability record the data hall will ask for.

## Module 5 — Storage and the grid: the bidirectional case
The dual-active-bridge stage inside every SST cell is symmetric — power flows whichever way the controller phase-shifts the two bridges — so an SST is bidirectional by construction. That property is a footnote in a data hall and the whole point in storage. A battery energy storage system needs a power conversion system (PCS) that charges from the grid and discharges into it, follows a frequency or voltage set-point, rides through faults and, increasingly, *forms* the grid when nothing else is there. Teach the conventional block — battery, DC-DC, low-voltage inverter, step-up transformer to medium voltage — and what the SST removes from it (the inverter's transformer and often the DC-DC stage, because the battery couples to the SST's DC side). Then vehicle-to-grid: the same symmetry lets a fleet of charging vehicles become a dispatchable battery, which matters most exactly where ports and depots sit — on constrained urban feeders. Close with the grid-code hooks a bidirectional MV converter has to satisfy (IEEE 2800 ride-through, anti-islanding, harmonic limits) and why grid-forming capability is becoming a purchase criterion rather than a feature.

## Module 6 — The AI hall: 34.5 kV to 800 V DC and what the unit displaces
Map the legacy chain onto the single block, function by function: the 34.5 kV-to-480 V line-frequency transformer (replaced by the cascaded SiC front end and the medium-frequency transformer), the switchgear room (folded into the enclosure, with arc-flash energy limited by settable electronic shutdown instead of by distance), the UPS (removed — a battery couples straight to the DC bus, which is why a DC-hall vendor pitches 'multi-sourced battery storage' rather than a UPS), the PDUs (replaced by DC distribution), and the rack power shelf (kept, but as a DC-DC stage from 800 V to the rack bus — the layer Delta, LITEON and Megmeet own). Then the staging from the Heron guide's Module 6 in one paragraph: NVIDIA rates the transformer-rectifier unit and the SST at the same 98.5%-plus and builds its first halls on the TRU, so a modular SST arriving in 2026–2027 sells on lead time and footprint against transformers with multi-year waits, and one arriving in 2029 sells against incumbents with field data. Teach the learner to ask of any hall-edge product: which wave is it built for, and what is the evidence it can ship into that wave.

## Module 7 — Where Amperesand's products slot in
Three configurations of one platform, each read against the public record. **Data hall:** a 5–10 MW modular block on 3.3 kV Wolfspeed SiC full-bridge modules, liquid-cooled to under 30°C module rise at 50°C-plus ambient, module swap in under twenty minutes; the 3.3 kV choice roughly halves the series cell count of a 1.7 kV design and buys margin against voltage overshoot at the price of a scarcer device class. Trade-show reporting, not a datasheet, supplies 10–34.5 kV in, 800 or 1,500 V DC out, 200 kW modules and a 22-week lead time. **Charging and ports:** containerised units with mixed AC/DC output and bidirectional operation, proven on a 1.5 MW grid-connected system and engaged at PSA Singapore's electric prime-mover charging — the MCS-class load of Module 3, and the field-hour proving ground of Module 4. **Grid:** a fifth generation 'in development for utility application' with on-grid/off-grid switching and constrained-grid load management — Module 5's concept map with no rated product yet. Close with the vendor-specification exercise: the company's own figures moved between November 2025 and August 2026 ('up to 10×' became '5×' faster time to power; 'greater than 80%' footprint reduction became '50%'), lifetime is stated as 15+, 20 and 20–30 years in three documents, and efficiency is the one number every source agrees on — and the one NVIDIA also gives the TRU. Sort each claim into 'a datasheet could settle this' and 'only field hours can settle this'.

## Module 8 — The industry map
Who buys: the campus owner through its design-build firm, against a GPU delivery schedule, with a battery supplier as the necessary third party once the UPS is gone. Who competes: the hedged incumbents (ABB, Eaton, GE Vernova, Siemens, Hitachi Energy, Schneider) selling the TRU bridge now and developing the SST for later; the Chinese and Taiwanese conversion manufacturers (Sungrow EnerNeo, Zhonhen, Delta, Megmeet); and the startups (Heron Power, DG Matrix, Amperesand), of which Heron alone is on NVIDIA's published facility-tier roster. In charging, the rival is the conventional depot chain and in-house SST efforts such as WattEV's. Then the platform argument as a business model: a single-market, single-factory vendor (Heron) versus a three-market vendor with Singapore engineering, a Reno line, Temasek on the cap table and a decade of university lineage. Teach what each buyer will ask the platform vendor — certification (UL 2877 and UL 1741 are targets, not listings), the supply chain behind a 'U.S.-built' unit whose silicon is Wolfspeed's and whose engineering is partly Singaporean, and which customer has actually energised a unit — and note that none had a public answer in September 2026. End on the economics of timing: field hours from ports and depots in 2026 are the platform vendor's route to a fleet record before the data-centre wave is decided.

## Pacing
Eight modules of roughly 30 minutes. Modules 2 and 4 are the technical core — silicon carbide and availability arithmetic — and are the ones to re-read before any conversation with a customer's reliability or electrical engineer. Module 3 can be read alone by anyone preparing for a port or fleet-charging conversation. Modules 6–8 are one argument about timing and the platform model and read best in a single sitting, with the Heron Power guide's Module 6 open beside them.
