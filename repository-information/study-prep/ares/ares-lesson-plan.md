# Ares — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Ares Management Corporation (NYSE: ARES), its Credit, Real Assets, Secondaries and Private Equity groups, its own data-centre platform Ada Infrastructure, and the funds that control Apex Clean Energy · **Written:** 2026-10-05 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1) · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no finance background.

**Purpose:** teach how a credit-first manager reaches AI and power infrastructure, and the one place it buys directly:
- the difference between a platform the manager owns and a company its funds own;
- how Ares's earnings split into fees, realized income and performance income, and why the last is lumpy;
- how a data-centre development fund works, from the investor's commitment to the platform's purchase order;
- controlled versus minority power positions, and why the operator keeps procurement;
- what joint control means, how merger control records it, and how to read a control record that has gone stale;
- preferred equity, dual-class minority stakes and loans — money without control — and the buying-authority test.

No company trivia: founding dates, executives, fund sizes and share prices stay in the dossier. The in-app guide (Profiler → Ares → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **Quinbrook** plan shows a manager that develops projects itself, which is Ada's model here. The **BlackRock** and **Blue Owl** plans teach buying another manager's business (GIP, IPI Partners); Module 1 applies the idea to GCP International. The **CPP Investments** plan sees Japan DC Partners from the investor's side, and the **Macquarie** plan sees Prime Data Centers from the departing co-owner's side.

## Module 1 — A platform the manager owns versus one its funds own

**The single idea:** Ada belongs to Ares itself and buys for Ares's data-centre funds; Apex belongs to Ares's funds and buys for itself.

1. **The usual model.** A manager reaches operating companies through funds that own them for a few years and then sell. The company's own management buys its equipment.
2. **Ada is different.** Ares's 10-K calls Ada Infrastructure 'our dedicated data center operating platform', combining 'in-house data center development and operational expertise'. It came with GCP International, bought in March 2025 for consideration valued at USD 3.9bn plus earnouts that pay only if the digital-infrastructure business meets revenue targets. Ada's design and energy-procurement teams specify and buy for the campuses Ares's funds own — over 1 GW in Northern Virginia, Tokyo, Osaka, London and Brazil.
3. **Apex is the usual model.** Ares funds have held a majority of Apex Clean Energy since 2021, with two Ares partners on its board. Apex's management develops, builds and operates its wind, solar and storage projects and signs for the equipment.
4. **Why it matters.** Both are real buyers. At Ada the manager sets strategy directly; at Apex a fund's board does, and the fund will one day sell.

**Self-check:** Ares launches a new Japanese data-centre fund. Who will place the switchgear orders for its campuses? *(Ada's teams, which develop and operate for Ares's data-centre funds.)*

## Module 2 — Fees, realized income and performance income

**The single idea:** fees are steady; performance income arrives only when funds sell at a profit.

1. **Fee-related earnings.** Management and transaction fees minus the cost of earning them: USD 1,775.3m in 2025.
2. **Realized income.** Ares's headline per-share measure adds the profits it actually received from fund sales and its own investments, after tax: USD 4.76 a share in 2025, against a consensus of USD 5.07.
3. **Performance income.** Ares's share of fund profits — carried interest — paid when an investment is realised. Sales cannot be scheduled: Ares expected over USD 350m for 2026 and had about USD 136m after three quarters on its own preliminary figures.
4. **Paper before cash.** Ares's Japan data-centre fund booked USD 54.3m of carried interest in the first half of 2026 on a revaluation of its campuses, before any sale.

**Self-check:** Which line would fall if no fund sold an asset this year, even though fees grew? *(Realized performance income, and with it realized income.)*

## Module 3 — How a data-centre development fund works

**The single idea:** investors commit money, the manager's platform builds, the fund owns — and the investor never sees the purchase order.

1. **The commitment.** Japan DC Partners I closed at about USD 2.4bn of equity commitments in June 2025 to develop three Greater Tokyo campuses of nearly 240 MW of IT load. CPP Investments was the largest investor, at about USD 1.3bn.
2. **Who decides.** A fund commitment buys a share of returns, not control. The limited partners commit; the general partner — Ares — decides what the fund buys.
3. **Who builds.** Ada develops and operates the fund's campuses, so the orders for a Tokyo campus come from Ada, paid with fund money.
4. **The same chain in the US.** Ares's 314-acre Spotsylvania, Virginia site has a first phase of two buildings and 200 MW.

**Self-check:** A pension fund commits USD 1bn to a data-centre fund. Can a switchgear seller sell to the pension fund? *(No — it neither designs nor buys; the platform that builds for the fund does.)*

## Module 4 — Controlled and minority power positions

**The single idea:** Ares's funds control two power buyers; most of the rest is passive capital beside an operator.

1. **Controlled.** Apex Clean Energy (majority since 2021) and, from September 2026, 80% of an EDPR California portfolio of 200 MW of solar and 184 MW of batteries; the release does not say who operates it.
2. **Minority beside an operator.** 49% of a 1,632 MW EDPR portfolio, with EDPR keeping 51%. In ENGIE North America's portfolios, ENGIE SA's 2025 annual report records Ares paying US$430m for a minority stake in 905 MW, and US$153m in 2025 with about US$280m scheduled for 2026 for 730 MW. ENGIE 'will retain a controlling share in the portfolio and will continue to operate and manage the assets'. No percentage is published.
3. **Why operators sell minorities.** A utility or developer sells part of its operating assets to recycle capital, keeps control and operations, and goes on buying the next project's equipment itself.

**Self-check:** A fund buys a minority of a utility's operating wind farms. Who buys the next project's turbines? *(The utility, which keeps control and operations.)*

## Module 5 — Joint control, merger control and a stale record

**The single idea:** control follows rights, not percentages — and a record of control is a snapshot.

1. **Joint control.** Two or more owners must agree on strategic decisions. Ares holds 26.24% of Eni's Plenitude after a contribution completed on 1 October 2026, and 'Ares and Eni will jointly control Plenitude': Ares appoints three of nine directors including the chairman, Eni five including the chief executive. Management, and with it procurement, sits under the chief executive.
2. **Merger control.** The European Commission must clear an acquisition of control, including joint control, before it closes; its decisions record who acquired control of what.
3. **A stale record.** In March 2025 the Commission cleared Ares joining the joint control of Prime Data Centers held by a Macquarie holding company and Data Realty Group. Since then Macquarie has sold its remaining stake without naming a buyer, and Grain Management's April 2026 release calls Data Realty Holding Corp. Prime's 'founding and controlling shareholder'. An Ares partner sits on Prime's board. No filing settles who controls Prime now.

**Self-check:** What do you write about Ares and Prime? *(Joint control as of the March 2025 clearance; current control unsettled on the record.)*

## Module 6 — Money without control, and the buying-authority test

**The single idea:** preferred equity, minority shares and loans earn returns; none of them signs a purchase order.

1. **Preferred equity.** Ares affiliates hold USD 800m of preferred equity and warrants in SB Energy's parent, with about USD 996m of liquidation preference at the end of 2025 — a senior claim paid before common shareholders, redeemed in full at the IPO.
2. **Dual-class minority.** After X-energy's IPO Ares affiliates held 4.9% of the Class A and 20.5% of the Class B shares. Class B shares vote but carry no economic rights, so by our calculation Ares holds about 10% of the combined vote. Two Ares partners are directors, without a contractual right to appoint them.
3. **A loan.** Ares funds arranged a USD 2.4bn facility for Vantage Data Centers, hold about USD 1.6bn of the commitment and funded about USD 330m at closing — three different numbers.
4. **The test.** Ada passes directly; Apex and the EDPR California portfolio pass through the platform; Plenitude is shared with Eni; Prime is unclear; the ENGIE and EDPR minorities, SB Energy, X-energy and Vantage fail.

**Self-check:** A lender 'provides USD 2.4bn' to a data-centre developer. How much has it lent? *(Only the drawn amount; and if it arranged the facility, it may hold only part of the commitment.)*
