# Arevon — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Arevon Energy (private; pension and sovereign capital; CAISO-centric storage owner) · **Written:** 2026-09-06 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no contract or tax background.

**What this teaches.** What an offtake contract actually obligates. Who buys storage in a contracted market and why a legal obligation — not a commercial appetite — creates the demand; the five contract forms and how each allocates risk; the difference between the megawatts a battery can produce and the megawatts a regulator will count; and why that counted number falls as more storage is built.

**Why this gap.** The corpus could explain what a battery is and could not explain what a battery is sold as. In California almost nothing is merchant, so a supply-side reader looking at CAISO sees fleets being built for reasons the technology alone does not explain. The explanation is the resource-adequacy obligation and the accreditation arithmetic underneath it.

**Its place in the buyer set.** Second of five. Apex Clean Energy covers the project lifecycle; this one covers the contract; Eolian the revenue stack; Jupiter Power the dispatch decision; Key Capture Energy the regulated purchase. Chemistry stays with REPT and Gotion.

## Module 1 — The buyer's buyer
Why every load-serving entity carries an enforced obligation to hold accredited capability covering its share of peak plus a reserve margin, and why that obligation converts reliably into procurement. The three kinds of California buyer — investor-owned utilities, community choice aggregators, municipal and joint-powers agencies — and why the same signed megawatts finance differently depending on which one signed them.

## Module 2 — Five ways to sell the same battery
Tolling, resource-adequacy contracts, energy storage service agreements, paired-plant power purchase agreements, and merchant operation. For each: who decides when it runs, what the owner is paid, who carries market risk. Two traps: a toll transfers upside as well as downside, and an availability obligation makes maintenance a contractual decision rather than an operational one.

## Module 3 — Counted megawatts
Effective load carrying capability and net qualifying capacity: what a system operator will let a buyer count, and why it is below nameplate. Duration as the dominant variable. Why four hours became the default — it is a fact about the length of the evening deficit, not about the technology. Exercise: given a deficit shape, work out what a two-hour resource should be credited.

## Module 4 — The saturation mechanism
The duck curve, and the reflexive effect that matters most in this market: storage flattens the very ramp it is credited against, so accreditation for a fixed duration declines as the fleet grows. Why duration creeps upward over time, and why a fleet built entirely at one duration has a structural rather than an operational problem.

## Module 5 — DC-coupled and AC-coupled
Where the battery sits relative to the inverter, and why a wiring choice is really a commercial one. Clipped energy recovery and one fewer conversion against grid-charging freedom, independent sizing and separate contracting. The solar peaker as the product that sits on the DC-coupled side of the choice.

## Module 6 — The credit as a supply-chain rule
The investment tax credit, why developers cannot usually use it themselves, and the two ways to monetise it: tax equity partnerships and outright credit transfer. Why the transfer buyer's disallowance risk makes equipment provenance a funding condition, and how the domestic-content and energy-community adders turn supply agreements into evidentiary documents.

## Module 7 — One supplier or several
The honest two-sided reading of a concentrated fleet: engineering done once, simpler operations and allocation priority against concentrated counterparty risk, lost price discovery and fleet-wide permitting exposure. Framed as a trade the buyer made deliberately, not an error.

## Module 8 — Safety as a specification
Thermal runaway as a self-sustaining reaction rather than an ordinary fire, and why design targets local containment instead of extinction. The institutional consequence: an incident anywhere raises the evidence burden everywhere, because permitting authorities, fire marshals and insurers generalise. Why buyers targeting dense load centres carry the heaviest burden and therefore the strictest supplier criteria.

## Pacing
Eight modules, roughly 30 minutes each. Modules 1–2 in one sitting, 3–4 in another — the accreditation pair is the conceptual core and should not be split. Modules 5–8 can be taken singly.
