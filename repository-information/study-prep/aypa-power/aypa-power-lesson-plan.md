# Aypa Power — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach **the standalone storage platform as a financed asset base** — what Brookfield is paying about USD 7 billion of enterprise value for when it buys a company that publishes no revenue, how a pipeline of options becomes an operating fleet, how the fleet is financed layer by layer, how the tax credit is turned into construction money two different ways, how a financial sponsor gets out, and where the whole structure fails. The plan assumes high-school STEM and no finance; every term is defined on first use and carried in the shared concepts registry.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **Classroom** owns how a battery earns money hour by hour — tolling revenue, ancillary-service stacking, duration and degradation. The **Spearmint plan** is this plan's mirror: the merchant developer that keeps the price risk. The **Intersect plan** is the third model: the captive developer bought by its customer. The **DNV and Sargent & Lundy guides** teach what an independent engineer checks at financial close; this plan stops at the door of that check. None of that is repeated here.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** A platform is worth the risks it has already retired, so a buyer pays for rungs, not megawatts. Capital is layered — corporate facility, warehouse, project loan, bridge, tax equity, back-leverage — and each layer has a different lender looking at different collateral. And the two things money cannot buy are the local permit and the grid connection date, which is where Aypa's record shows the losses.

**One boundary, held throughout.** Aypa is private and publishes no financial statements, no project list and no leadership page. Every figure in this plan is a facility size, a contract term or a transaction value stated by Aypa, its lenders, its counterparties or the buyer; the '~6.5 GW' is Brookfield's number and the operating-versus-contracted split is Brookfield Renewable's. The sale was signed on 22 July 2026 and had not closed by 5 September 2026.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min). Module 2 with the SRP Pediment releases open (~35 min) — it is the whole ladder in one project. Module 3 with the February 2026 warehouse release open (~40 min). Module 4 (~25 min). Module 5 (~25 min). Then flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — What a platform buyer pays for

**The single idea:** four things are inside the price — the contracted book, the pipeline, the team and the bank relationships — and the price is quoted two ways.

**The contracted book.** '95% contracted under long-term agreements with investment-grade customers for an average remaining contract life of 17 years.' Percent contracted tells a lender how much cash flow is fixed; average remaining contract life tells it for how long. Those two numbers set the debt a portfolio can carry.

**The pipeline.** '>20 GW development pipeline' — options at every stage from a land lease to a permitted site awaiting a contract. Worth the sum of many small probabilities; the place where buyer and seller disagree most.

**The team and the machine.** In-house development, transmission analytics, procurement, construction and operations — about 200 people. A platform sale buys the ability to make projects.

**Enterprise value against equity value.** USD 7 billion against USD 3 billion: the difference is about USD 4 billion of debt that stays with the assets. The seller receives equity value; the buyer's cost is enterprise value.

**Self-check:** the seller says 6.5 GW; the buyer's affiliate says 3,000 MW operating and under construction plus 3,500 MW contracted. Which number would a lender use to size debt today? *(The operating and under-construction figure, and only the contracted part of it — the rest is future.)*

## Module 2 — The conversion ladder

**The single idea:** every project climbs the same rungs — site control, queue position, permit, offtake, financing, notice to proceed, construction, commercial operation — and each rung retires one risk.

**Pediment as the worked example.** Originated by Eolian in 2018; jointly bid into SRP's 2023 all-source RFP; bought by Aypa as a late-stage project in 2024; SRP agreement announced July 2024; USD 398 million financed December 2024; online June 2026. Eight years from origination, twenty-three months from acquisition.

**The rungs money cannot buy.** Kingfisher Reserve was denied by a county board after a two-hour hearing; Dirac was refused by a city commission six months after the state commission approved its utility contract. PG&E's own evaluator flagged queue dates of 2031 and 2033 against 2027 contracted deliveries at Marici and Gabriel.

**Self-check:** a project has a signed 20-year toll, a CPUC approval and no local permit. Which rung is it on, and what is it worth? *(The permit rung — contracted but unbuildable until the hearing goes the other way; the contract is a liability if the permit never comes.)*

## Module 3 — The financing stack, layer by layer

**The single idea:** a multi-gigawatt private platform is financed in layers, and the layers are visible in Aypa's releases.

**Corporate facility.** Revolver, term loan and letter-of-credit facility at the holding company: USD 320 million (2023) to USD 1.55 billion (March 2026). The LC tranche — USD 550 million of the 2025 facility — is what posts interconnection deposits and contract security across the pipeline.

**Warehouse.** USD 1.5 billion revolving construction facility with a USD 500 million accordion (February 2026), 19 lenders, three years: several projects built at once, each leaving the warehouse when refinanced at COD.

**Project loans and bridges.** Construction-to-term loans (Wolf Tank, Vidal, Elora and Hedley), tax-equity bridge loans (Bypass, Kuna), a tax-credit-transfer bridge (Pediment), and back-leverage above the project.

**Self-check:** why does a warehouse exist if each project can get its own loan? *(Speed and scale — one facility funds the next batch without a separate closing per site; the project loans arrive at COD.)*

## Module 4 — Two ways to sell a tax credit

**The single idea:** the storage investment tax credit is worth roughly a third of a project's cost, and a developer with little taxable income must sell it.

**The partnership flip.** A bank becomes a partner, takes most of the tax benefits until a target return, then its share flips down. U.S. Bancorp at Cald–Borden County and Kuna; Morgan Stanley at Bypass in a 'hybrid' form.

**The transfer.** Since 2023 the credit can be sold for cash; Pediment used a 'tax credit transfer bridge loan'. Buyers are never named.

**The supply-chain link.** FEOC rules decide which batteries qualify. Aypa's only named supplier is Canadian Solar's e-STORAGE, with Sungrow units at one site — the concentration that makes the credit layer the exposed one.

**Self-check:** which financing layer loses first if a project's cells fail the FEOC test? *(Tax equity or the transfer — the credit is what they are buying.)*

## Module 5 — How a sponsor exits, and where it fails

**The single idea:** a sponsor sells the platform, not a project, and the gap between signing and closing is a period of real risk.

**The sale.** Teasers in February 2026 ('Project Alpha', Bank of America and Cantor Fitzgerald); a buyer through Brookfield's second transition-fund vintage with its listed affiliate co-investing; 'subject to customary regulatory approvals'; no closing by September 2026. Every facility was priced on Blackstone's sponsorship; change-of-control clauses are the buyer's first diligence item.

**Where it fails.** Permits; queue-versus-contract dates; numbers that drift (30 → 42 → 35 projects); one battery vendor; debt priced on the old sponsor; a deal that does not close.

**Self-check:** the site still says 'A Blackstone Portfolio Company' in September 2026. Is that a mistake? *(No — the seller owns the company until closing.)*
