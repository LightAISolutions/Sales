# Berkshire Hathaway Energy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach the fourth way a regulated utility serves a data center — the PPA-side model, in which the plan buys 5.4 GW of batteries and 4.4 GW of solar by contract and owns almost none of it; what a large-load service agreement is and why every commercial percentage in NV Energy's was left blank for the regulator; the difference between paying for wires (a line-extension agreement) and paying for generation (a customer-specific charge); why a utility would sue its own largest prospective customer over which forum decides; how wildfire liability, not load, became the binding constraint on a six-state utility; and what a grid without a capacity auction does instead — starting from high-school STEM. Companion: the in-app guide (Profiler → Berkshire Hathaway Energy → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** Dominion taught the rate class with minimum demand charges; DTE the special contract that makes the customer fund utility-owned batteries; WEC the subscription to named plants. This plan teaches a utility that contracts for its resources and writes a bilateral service agreement whose terms the commission fills in — and, through NV Energy and Tract, what happens when the customer and the utility disagree about who decides.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min — the PPA-side plan and where the equipment decision went), Module 2 in one sitting (~35 min — the LLESA, Rule 9 and the blanks), Module 3 in one sitting (~25 min — the Tract dispute and the executive order), Module 4 in one sitting (~30 min — wildfire liability and PacifiCorp's balance sheet), Module 5 last (~25 min — western markets, Iowa's contract, the group view), then the flashcard and self-test passes in the app.

## Module 1 — The PPA-side plan

**The single idea:** a resource plan can add 5,405 MW of batteries without the utility buying a single one — and when it does, the equipment decision moves to whoever won the request for proposals.

1. **The 2026 IRP.** NV Energy's plan (PUCN Docket 26-05007, filed May 7, 2026) asks for 23 power purchase agreements: about 4,370 MW of paired solar, 180 MW of geothermal and 5,405 MW of battery output capacity by 2031, plus 1,223 MW of gas turbines the utility would own at Fort Churchill (401 MW of aeroderivatives in 2030 and 2031, 421 MW of frame units in 2032). The fact sheet's phrase is the whole model: 'more than 5,400 MW of battery storage through power purchase agreements'.
2. **Where the equipment decision went.** A PPA buys output, not hardware. The developer who won the 2024 all-source RFP (198 bids, 21,019 MW, 12,634 MW of it storage) chose the cells, the integrator and the EPC at bid stage; the utility never places a purchase order. The corpus's owned exceptions are Reid Gardner (220 MW / 440 MWh, in service 2023, Energy Vault as EPC, BYD cells) and Sierra Solar (400 MW / 1,600 MWh, 2026, supplier undisclosed). Group-wide, battery storage is $0.2 billion of a $33.6 billion three-year plan.
3. **Why a utility chooses this.** Three reasons in the record: the Berkshire rule that hyperscalers must be served 'if there was no impact to the rates', which a fixed-price contract satisfies more visibly than a rate-base asset; the tax-credit and tariff risk after the 2025 federal changes, which a PPA leaves with the developer; and speed — the RFP shortlist already exists. The cost is a utility that earns nothing on the batteries and a plan whose timing depends on its counterparties.
4. **The gas exception.** The turbines are owned. Firm capacity that must run on the coldest morning is the one thing the plan keeps in rate base, and the earliest new turbine is 2029–2030 — the reason a developer built its own bridging plants (Module 3).

**Self-check:** a storage seller wants to supply NV Energy's 2026 batteries. Who is the buyer? (The developers behind the 23 confidential PPAs — Arevia, NextEra and the 2026 counterparties — at bid stage; NV Energy buys only the Sierra Solar and Reid Gardner units, both already contracted.)

## Module 2 — The large-load service agreement

**The single idea:** a line-extension agreement pays for wires; a large-load electric service agreement pays for generation — and NV Energy filed the second as a form with the commercial numbers left for the commission.

1. **Two instruments.** Under Rule 9, a large customer advances the cost of the distribution and transmission built to reach it and commits to minimum usage; about 6 GW of these agreements were signed against about 22 GW of inquiries. But Rule 9 'does not address generation'. The form Large Load Electric Service Agreement (LLESA), filed with the IRP for any accelerated load of 25 MW or more — the lowest threshold in this corpus — adds a maximum demand and ramp schedule, minimum billing for demand and energy, a customer-specific generation charge recovered before any plant enters rate base, a termination payment, and customer security under objective credit standards.
2. **The blanks.** The five customers who filed LLESAs in May and June 2026 — Amazon at Sparks, Fleet/Tract at McCarran, Vantage NV11, Novva in Las Vegas, Prologis — each reserved the minimum-demand and minimum-energy percentages, the generation charge, the rates, the termination payment and the security calculation for the PUCN to set (Exhibit G). Third-party reporting puts Fleet's minimum bill at 90 percent of contracted and Novva's term at 300 months with a ramp of up to 48. The utility asked for the form to be deemed approved by September 18, 2026; the IRP decision is due December 2.
3. **Compare the four models.** Dominion: a rate class with an 85 percent minimum demand charge on system service. DTE: a special contract in which the customer funds utility-owned batteries. WEC: a subscription to named plants at a fixed return. NV Energy: a bilateral contract at a 25 MW threshold with the generation cost charged to the customer up front and every percentage set by the regulator. The first three are tariffs or contracts the utility wrote; the fourth is a form the commission completes.
4. **The clean-service door.** The Clean Transition Tariff (approved 2025) lets a customer buy bundled service from a named new clean resource at its own charge — Google's Fervo geothermal, then Ormat's and Amazon's deals. It is the origin of the model Google exported to Minnesota.

**Self-check:** why does the LLESA charge for generation before the plant is in rate base? (So the customer, not the general body of ratepayers, pays for capacity built for it — the 'no impact to the rates' rule written as a contract term.)

## Module 3 — A utility sues its customer

**The single idea:** the NV Energy–Tract case is about which forum decides how the grid is built out, not about price.

1. **The sequence.** June 2024: Tract's Reno Power signs agreements with NV Energy for its Storey County parks. June 16, 2026: Tract demands private arbitration under them. July 24: NV Energy sues Tract Capital Management in Washoe County to keep supply adequacy, cost allocation and resource-plan treatment before the PUCN, saying Tract wants the utility to 'reserve and provide enormous amounts of power for Tract's private development while shifting the infrastructure and energy costs to Nevada families'. Tract: more than $127 million spent, about $1 billion of upgrades committed, 'a public relations blitz'.
2. **The arithmetic.** NV Energy says Tract plans 'at least five data centers with a combined load of nearly 5.5 GW' — against a combined 2025 system peak of about 8,200 MW. That is the scale that makes the question of who decides — a regulator with a duty to all customers or an arbitrator bound to two parties — the real dispute.
3. **The customer's answer.** Fleet's project companies applied in April 2026 for temporary gas plants — 218 MW at Peru Ridge, 144 MW at South Valley, about 90 LNG containerized engines and 136 CNG units with emissions controls — to run two to three years until the utility can serve. Storey County approved them in July; the PUCN conditionally approved them on September 17, 2026. The customer stopped waiting for the plan.
4. **The state steps in.** The same day, Governor Lombardo's Executive Order 2026-005 directed the PUCN to set an instrument ensuring data centers 'bear full service costs with no burden on other ratepayers' and load-reduction duties in grid emergencies, with a 'Responsible Speed to Power Plan' due December 31, 2026. Nevada has no large-load statute; the LLESA and this order are the framework.

**Self-check:** the PUCN approved Tract's bridging plants a week before the executive order. What does that tell a seller of engines and turbines? (Behind-the-meter bridging generation is a permitted, regulator-approved product in Nevada while utility service is years out — a buying door that opened because the utility's plan could not.)

## Module 4 — Wildfire liability as the constraint

**The single idea:** PacifiCorp spends more of its three-year plan on wildfire prevention ($1.5 billion) than on new generation ($0.1 billion of wind) because an unbounded liability decides what a utility can borrow to build.

1. **The number.** PacifiCorp has accrued $2.85 billion of probable wildfire losses through mid-2026 and paid $2.3 billion; the James class verdicts (about $1.2 billion for roughly 200 plaintiffs) were reversed and sent back in April 2026, and the Oregon Supreme Court hears the case on November 3, 2026. 'Reasonably possible' further material losses remain.
2. **What it costs.** S&P put the utility on watch for junk in March 2026 after two verdicts; a $2.55 billion letter-of-credit facility, a $575 million federal settlement and a $150 million plaintiff settlement followed. The Washington sale to Portland General ($1.9 billion, closing in 2027), the Oregon co-op sales and the Boardman-to-Hemingway sale-leaseback (about $1.1 billion) are modeled as liquidity for the damages case.
3. **What it does to the data-center book.** Oregon: more than 7,000 MW pursued, 784 MW under contract, negotiations 'stalled' under the 2025 POWER Act, and Amazon's complaint that the utility failed to serve its data centers. Utah: a first contract under SB 132 worth 'nearly $1 billion' of net benefits and about 2,000 MW of opportunity. The 2025 IRP Update added 2,223 MW of gas proxies and 4,677 MW of storage proxies by 2045 with no procurement door open in 2026.
4. **The state fixes.** Utah's 2024 caps on noneconomic damages, Wyoming's zero noneconomic liability, Idaho's statute, and Oregon's defeated punitive bills — a utility's ability to serve new load is now partly a function of its state's tort law.

**Self-check:** why does a wildfire verdict in Oregon affect whether a data center in Utah gets served? (One balance sheet, one credit rating and one liquidity plan serve six states; the rating decides the cost of every dollar borrowed to build.)

## Module 5 — Markets without an auction, Iowa's contract, and the group

**The single idea:** the West has no capacity market, so resource adequacy is a planning duty; Iowa's answer to large load is a ten-year contract; and the parent measures the whole group by one rule.

1. **No capacity auction.** BHE's western utilities buy and sell energy in CAISO's imbalance markets — PacifiCorp launched in the Extended Day-Ahead Market on May 1, 2026, NV Energy trades in the real-time market — but nobody pays them for capacity; the IRP's open-position target (no more than 500 MW short by 2031) is the reliability rule. Contrast PJM, where Exelon's customers pay an auction price.
2. **The Iowa contract.** MidAmerican's Load Commitment Agreement: a binding ramp, a minimum monthly charge payable for ten years regardless of usage, exit penalties, and customer funding of every substation and line. The forecast counts only signed contracts; data centers are about 8 percent of Iowa peak; there is no tariff docket and no litigation. Its supply is 800 MW of solar under a pre-approved return and a 520–700 MW peaker (three figures on the record, unresolved).
3. **The group number.** About 11,000 MW of contracted data-center load 'in service or in varying stages of construction', 91 percent with the highest-rated counterparties; a 2025 data-center peak just over 2,200 MW (about 8 percent of system peak); 4,500–5,700 MW by 2030. The parent's rule: serve the hyperscalers 'if there was no impact to the rates'.
4. **The optionality.** BHE Renewables, the nonregulated arm, is doing 'site diligence' on behind-the-meter data-center generation at sites 'capable of ... 1 GW or more' — the one part of the group that would buy turbines and batteries as a developer rather than a utility, and the one that satisfies the parent's rule by construction.

**Self-check:** a hyperscaler wants firm capacity in Nevada in 2028. What are its three routes? (NV Energy's owned Valmy peakers and Fort Churchill turbines through the LLESA from 2028–2030; a Clean Transition Tariff resource it pays for; or its own permitted bridging generation, as Tract did.)
