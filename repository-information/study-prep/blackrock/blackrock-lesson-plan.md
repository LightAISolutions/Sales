# BlackRock — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** BlackRock, Inc. (NYSE: BLK), with Global Infrastructure Partners (GIP), the AI Infrastructure Partnership (AIP) and HPS · **Written:** 2026-09-26 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1) · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no finance background.

**Purpose:** teach the machinery that decides whether an investor this large is ever a buyer of power equipment:
- what an index fund is, and why owning 9% of a utility passively is not controlling it;
- what a firm buys when it buys another manager, and why its infrastructure is counted three ways;
- how a consortium takes joint control, and why a partnership can be the headline without being the owner;
- what it takes to buy a utility — the shareholder vote, the federal and state approvals, the conditions;
- who actually signs the purchase order at a platform an investor owns;
- what a battery platform under a fund buys, and how a fund's ownership shapes that;
- how a hyperscaler moves a data-centre campus off its balance sheet with an investor;
- the ladder a deal climbs from rumour to closing, and how to read each rung.

No company trivia: founding dates, executives, fund sizes and share counts stay in the dossier. The in-app guide (Profiler → BlackRock → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **Blackstone** plan teaches how a listed alternative manager earns (fees, carried interest, the three numbers called AUM); this plan assumes it. The **MGX** plan teaches the sovereign fund and the fund-versus-platform distinction from the Gulf side of the same partnership. The **KKR** plan, written the same day, teaches the insurer-backed manager and the co-owner on the other side of CyrusOne.

**Suggested pacing (before 7 October 2026):** Module 1 (~15 min), Module 2 (~15 min), Module 3 (~15 min), Module 4 (~20 min — the densest), Module 5 (~15 min — the most important for a seller), Module 6 (~10 min), Module 7 (~10 min), Module 8 (~10 min), then the flashcard and self-test passes in the app.

## Module 1 — Index funds, and why a big stake is not control

**The single idea:** an index fund buys everything in an index in proportion, so its manager ends up holding a few per cent of thousands of companies without choosing or running any of them.

1. **What an index is.** A list of companies with a weight for each, usually by market value — the S&P 500 is about 500 large US listed companies weighted by market value. An index fund buys each company in proportion to its weight and rebalances when the list changes.
2. **Why the stakes are large.** BlackRock's ETFs held USD 6.25 trillion at 30 June 2026 and its institutional index mandates another USD 3.95 trillion. At that scale, following the index means owning 8–11% of big US utilities — Exelon 11.02%, PSEG 10.41%, NextEra 8.58% in the PJM market monitor's list.
3. **Passive in law, not just in habit.** In the US anyone holding more than 5% of a listed company must file. A holder who certifies it will not try to influence control files the short **Schedule 13G**; a holder who can or will influence control files the longer **Schedule 13D**. BlackRock's index stakes are on 13Gs. Its Clearway Energy stake, held through GIP, is on a 13D. Same parent, two different kinds of owner.
4. **Why a regulator still cares.** The US energy regulator (FERC) must approve large voting stakes in power companies. BlackRock holds its index stakes under a **blanket authorization** — a standing approval on condition the holdings stay passive. In April 2025 FERC renewed it while noting 'some concern' about 'a novel degree of influence'.
5. **The tension.** A July 2026 protest argues that a firm cannot be the passive owner of competing utilities and the controlling owner of others at once, and asks FERC to force the two businesses apart.

**Self-check:** BlackRock holds 9% of a generator through index funds and files a 13G. Can it tell that generator which batteries to buy? *(No — a 13G certifies a passive holding; the stake confers no board seat and no say over purchasing.)*

## Module 2 — Buying a manager, not a pile of assets

**The single idea:** when BlackRock bought Global Infrastructure Partners it bought a team and its contracts to manage other people's money — the power plants and data centres still belong to GIP's funds and their investors.

1. **What was bought.** On 1 October 2024 BlackRock completed the purchase of GIP for USD 3bn in cash and about 12 million BlackRock shares, part of them deferred for up to five years and tied to later performance. It combined its own infrastructure businesses under the GIP name.
2. **Where the money comes from.** GIP's funds are raised from pension funds, sovereign funds and insurers (limited partners). GIP decides what to buy and charges fees; the investors own the assets. So 'BlackRock owns CyrusOne' is shorthand for 'funds managed by BlackRock's GIP co-own CyrusOne'.
3. **Three ways to count it.** BlackRock reports USD 113.5bn of infrastructure *fee-paying* AUM, USD 197bn of infrastructure *client assets*, and GIP describes itself as managing 'over $200 billion'. Fee-paying excludes money committed but not yet invested; client assets include it. The gap is money waiting to be deployed.
4. **Why the deferred shares matter.** Part of the price was paid years later and only if targets were met — **deferred consideration**. It keeps the acquired team working to the buyer's plan.
5. **The ranking consequence.** Infrastructure Investor's 2026 table puts BlackRock first in five-year infrastructure fundraising. It counts funds closed from 2021 to 2025, a window in which the GIP team raised GIP V (USD 25.2bn), so the rank is largely acquired.

**Self-check:** GIP V closes at USD 25bn but BlackRock's infrastructure fee revenue barely moves that quarter. Why? *(Committed capital often earns little until it is invested; fee-paying AUM rises as the money is deployed into deals.)*

## Module 3 — Consortiums and joint control: who actually bought Aligned

**The single idea:** a press release can name three buyers while a regulator names two controllers — and the regulator's answer is the one that decides who governs the company.

1. **The deal.** On 21 July 2026 'AIP, MGX, and BlackRock's GIP' completed the purchase of 100% of Aligned Data Centers — about USD 40bn of enterprise value, 51 campuses, more than 6.4 GW — the AI Infrastructure Partnership's first investment.
2. **What a consortium is.** Several investors buy together through a shared vehicle, each with a share and with rights set in a shareholders' agreement: board seats, vetoes over budgets and large contracts.
3. **What the regulator said.** European merger law asks who gets *control*, meaning the power to decide strategy. In Case M.12259 the European Commission named two acquirers of **joint control**: Global Infrastructure Management, LLC (ultimately BlackRock) and MGX Fund Management Limited. AIP was not a notifying party.
4. **So what is AIP?** A partnership launched in September 2024 by BlackRock, GIP, Microsoft and MGX, with NVIDIA and xAI joining in March 2025, targeting USD 30bn of equity and up to USD 100bn with debt. No first-party source gives its legal form. It works as a pool of capital that invests through GIP and MGX.
5. **Why the membership matters to a seller.** AIP's members include the demand side — Microsoft, NVIDIA and xAI — so the tenants' own money sits among the landlord's owners. That shapes which specifications the landlord's engineers treat as fixed.

**Self-check:** a release says 'AIP, MGX and GIP acquired X'. Which document tells you who controls X? *(The merger-control decision — here the European Commission's — which names the parties acquiring control.)*

## Module 4 — Taking a utility private

**The single idea:** buying a regulated utility needs a shareholder vote, a federal antitrust clearance, a foreign-investment review, federal energy approval and every relevant state commission — and each can attach conditions.

1. **The AES deal.** GIP and the EQT Infrastructure VI fund, with CalPERS and the Qatar Investment Authority as co-underwriters, signed on 1 March 2026 to buy The AES Corporation at USD 15.00 a share — about USD 10.7bn of equity and USD 33.4bn including debt. After closing, GIP-managed vehicles would hold 56.625%: control.
2. **The approval ladder, in order.** Shareholders approved on 26 June 2026. The US antitrust waiting period (HSR) expired. CFIUS, which screens foreign investment for national security (QIA is a foreign state fund), cleared on 27 August. Ohio's commission approved AES Ohio on 17 September. FERC (**Section 203**) and New York's commission were still outstanding on 26 September.
3. **What commissions ask for.** Minnesota approved GIP and CPP Investments' purchase of ALLETE in October 2025 with about USD 200m of customer benefits, including a one-year base-rate freeze and USD 50m of rate credits. Ohio's conditions for AES reportedly include no merger-driven rate changes and recusal of directors with data-centre interests.
4. **The clock.** Signed deals carry an **outside date** — AES's is 1 June 2027, extendable twice by three months — after which either side may walk away. Regulators that ask for hearings can push a deal toward it.
5. **Why it matters to an equipment seller.** A utility under new owners keeps its engineers and procurement rules, but its capital plan — how many batteries and substations it can fund — is reset by the new owner's business plan and the commission's conditions.

**Self-check:** list, in order, the approvals the AES deal needed, and name the two still open on 26 September 2026. *(Shareholders, HSR, CFIUS, Ohio commission, New York commission, FERC; FERC and New York were open.)*

## Module 5 — Who signs the purchase order

**The single idea:** an investor passes the buying-authority test only through a platform it controls, and even there the order is written by the platform's engineers, not the investor.

1. **The test.** Does the company, or a platform it controls alone or jointly, sign for batteries, medium-voltage switchgear, generation or solid-state transformers? BlackRock, Inc. itself signs for none.
2. **Where it passes.** Through platforms its GIP funds control or co-control: Aligned (with MGX), CyrusOne (with KKR), Coravel (a 50:50 data-centre developer with the builder ACS), ALLETE (GIP 60%, governance shared equally with CPP Investments), Clearway Energy Group (50:50 with TotalEnergies), and the battery owners Eolian and Jupiter Power. AES joins on closing.
3. **Where it fails.** A minority preferred stake (20% of Recurrent Energy), a loan (GIP as a lender on EdgeCore's construction debt; HPS lending to Intersect Power), an index stake, a memorandum of understanding (NVIDIA's compute-financing MOUs) or reported talks (STACK Asia-Pacific) give no purchasing say.
4. **Co-control cuts it further.** At Aligned the other controller is MGX; at CyrusOne, KKR; at Coravel, ACS. Large contracts typically need both owners' consent, and the platform's management proposes them.
5. **Owner versus builder.** At the El Paso campus BlackRock funds are to own 80%, but Meta provides construction management and is the only tenant, so in practice the design and equipment choices run through Meta.

**Self-check:** you sell solid-state transformers. BlackRock's funds co-own a data-centre landlord. Whom do you call first? *(The landlord's engineering and procurement leads; BlackRock's value is the list of landlords, not the purchase order.)*

## Module 6 — A battery platform under a fund

**The single idea:** a merchant battery owner buys cells, containers, inverters and transformers itself, but the fund above it sets how much it can build and what risks it may take.

1. **What these platforms own.** Eolian 'operates a growing portfolio of wholly-owned utility-scale battery energy storage projects'; Jupiter Power raised USD 1.4bn in September 2026 for ten projects of 1,500 MW / 3,600 MWh. Both sell into wholesale markets rather than on long utility contracts — the **merchant** model.
2. **What they buy.** Battery containers (cells, racks, cooling, fire protection), power conversion systems that turn DC to AC, medium-voltage transformers and switchgear, and a substation to the grid. Each buy is a platform decision.
3. **What the fund controls.** The equity cheque, the leverage limit, the hold period and the risk policy — for example whether foreign-sourced equipment is acceptable for tax-credit eligibility.
4. **Why the owner has gone quiet.** Jupiter's and Akaysha's 2026 releases name no owner, and Akaysha was reported in January 2026 to be weighing a minority sale. The record cannot yet say whether these stay long-term holdings.
5. **A minority preferred stake is different.** BlackRock's USD 500m in Recurrent Energy bought 20% on an as-converted basis, with a dividend, a liquidation preference and an exit right, but no control. It pays first and decides nothing.

**Self-check:** why might two battery platforms owned by the same sponsor adopt similar supplier rules? *(The sponsor sets risk and compliance policy at fund level, and both platforms report to the same investment committee.)*

## Module 7 — Moving a campus off a hyperscaler's balance sheet

**The single idea:** a tech company can build a data centre with an investor's money, keep a minority, lease the whole campus back and guarantee its value, so the asset and most of the debt sit outside its accounts.

1. **The El Paso numbers.** A roughly USD 14bn, 1 GW campus: BlackRock funds 80%, Meta 20%. Meta contributes about USD 2.3bn of land and work in progress, BlackRock about USD 4.9bn of cash, and a USD 12.5bn debt financing funds much of the rest.
2. **The lease.** Meta is the only tenant on a four-year lease extendable to twenty years. The investor earns rent; lenders look to that rent for repayment.
3. **The guarantee.** Meta gives a **residual value** guarantee of about USD 13bn: if the campus is worth less than that when the lease ends, Meta covers the shortfall. That is why lenders accept the structure.
4. **Why a hyperscaler does it.** Capital spending on AI campuses is running in the tens of billions a year. Shifting part of it to investors preserves cash and borrowing capacity, at the cost of paying rent and keeping the value risk through the guarantee.
5. **The status caveat.** Meta's own quarterly filing, two days after the announcement, called it an 'exclusivity agreement' subject to definitive agreements; no closing notice had been found by 26 September 2026.

**Self-check:** who bears the risk that the campus is worth little in 2030 — the owner or the tenant? *(Largely the tenant, through the residual value guarantee — which is what makes the investor's 80% financeable.)*

## Module 8 — The deal ladder

**The single idea:** every deal climbs the same rungs, and a dossier must record which rung it has reached, never the rung the headline implies.

1. **Report.** A news outlet says talks are happening. It binds nobody. *Example:* Bloomberg's 24 September 2026 report of AIP and IFM Investors talking to Blue Owl about STACK's Asia-Pacific data centres.
2. **Exclusive talks.** The seller agrees to negotiate with one bidder for a period. It signals intent but can collapse — the report itself said 'talks could be prolonged or fail'.
3. **Memorandum of understanding.** A written statement of intended terms, usually not binding. *Example:* NVIDIA's 10 August 2026 compute-financing MOUs with six financiers, 'subject to execution of the final agreements'.
4. **Signing.** A **definitive agreement** binds both sides, subject to conditions. *Example:* AES, signed 1 March 2026.
5. **Approvals.** Votes and regulators, each with power to impose conditions or delay. *Example:* AES's FERC and New York approvals, pending on 26 September 2026.
6. **Closing.** Money changes hands and control passes. *Example:* Aligned, 21 July 2026; ALLETE, 15 December 2025.

**Self-check:** place each on the ladder as of 26 September 2026 — STACK Asia-Pacific, NVIDIA financing, AES, El Paso, Aligned. *(Exclusive talks reported; MOU; signed awaiting approvals; announced with exclusivity, closing unconfirmed; closed.)*

## Where it fails — the risks in one list

- A FERC order that conditions BlackRock's control of utilities on limits to its passive stakes, or a hearing that runs AES past its outside date.
- A co-owner that disagrees: joint control means either side can block a large contract.
- A partnership with no disclosed legal form (AIP) whose members' commitments are unknown, so its buying power cannot be sized.
- Battery platforms held without public ownership statements, which may be sold before a supplier relationship pays back.
