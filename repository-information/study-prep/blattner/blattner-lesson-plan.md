# Blattner — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Blattner (a unit of a listed contractor; the largest and least visible renewables construction force in the US market) · **Written:** 2026-09-06 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no construction-contracting or scheduling background.

**What this teaches.** What an engineering, procurement and construction contractor actually sells, which is a date and a price rather than a building — and what follows from having sold them. A fixed price as the purchase of certainty rather than of labour, with the contractor absorbing the tail of a cost distribution and the premium being what that transfer costs. The three ways the same plant can be bought — a full wrap, a set of direct contracts, or owner-bought equipment with an installation contract — and why grid storage overwhelmingly uses the third. Scope seams as the actual failure mode of construction projects, with their three characteristic breakages. The schedule as a commercial instrument: critical path, float and who consumes it, the baseline as the only thing that makes delay measurable, and concurrent delay. Procurement lead times that start before the design is finished. The seasonal grain of a construction calendar. And the choice every late project faces between extending the date and paying to compress the work.

**Why this gap.** Three courses in this corpus describe everything around a power project and nothing about building one. The project-lifecycle guide compresses construction and commissioning into a single bar of roughly fourteen months and observes, correctly from the outside, that a slipped completion date is usually a queue problem rather than a construction one. Nothing in the corpus went inside that bar — so the party that carries a fixed date with money payable per day of lateness had no account of its own economics, and the reader had no way to understand why a contractor prices ambiguity rather than difficulty.

**Its place in the construction set.** First of four, and the course entry point. It establishes the claim the other three elaborate: an EPC converts a financed design into an energised plant by selling a date and a price. It hands the acceleration cost curve to the MasTec guide, which explains why time cannot simply be bought back; it hands the site-conditions and commissioning seams to the SOLV guide; and it hands liquidated damages and their caps to the Samsung C&T guide, which asks what stands behind them.

## Module 1 — What a fixed price transfers
Cost plus margin as the intuitive and misleading reading. A contractor pricing a distribution rather than a cost, and a lump-sum price as the conversion of that distribution into a number. Why ambiguity is more expensive than difficulty, and why an uncapped obligation cannot be priced at expected value at all. Contingency, and why whose it is changes behaviour. A fixed price being only as fixed as the scope, with the change-order count as the honest measure of design completeness at award. The lender's technical adviser as the one party with an unambiguous preference, and why.

## Module 2 — Three ways to buy the same plant
The wrap, the multi-contract, owner-furnished equipment with an installation contract, and construction management converting to a guaranteed maximum. What each keeps with the owner. Why storage projects almost always separate the battery purchase from the construction contract, and that the reason is commercial — supply agreement, capacity guarantee, service agreement and tax position — rather than technical.

## Module 3 — How the work is actually won
Standing agreements and repeat-owner relationships rather than sealed bids at the top of this market. Three consequences: market share becomes invisible and must be inferred from customers and parent disclosure; capacity is allocated before it is priced, because the buyer is purchasing a place in a crew calendar; and the contractor's incentive shifts from winning the next job to keeping the relationship. Limited notice to proceed as the mechanism that starts the ordering clock before financial close. Where the pattern reverses, in public and international procurement.

## Module 4 — Seams
Every scope split as a decision to create a boundary. The three failure modes — gap, overlap, timing mismatch. The five recurring seams on a storage project: equipment delivery, the utility's side of the connection point, inherited civil work, the controls boundary between supplier and plant controller, and documentation. Why liquidated damages work poorly across a split scope. The boundary against the integrator course: a supplier gets a product listed, a contractor gets an installation accepted. The interface register as the highest-return document on a multi-party project.

## Module 5 — The schedule as a commercial instrument
Activities, durations, logic and the critical path. Float, and the fact that it is consumed by whoever uses it first. Near-critical paths and why the distribution of float matters more than the finish date. The baseline as what makes delay measurable rather than assertable. Concurrent delay and its usual compromise. The pairing of extension of time with compression, which the next module takes apart.

## Module 6 — The ordering clock and the calendar
Why the procurement lane starts before the site lane and finishes inside it. Submittal approval rather than the purchase order as what releases a factory slot, so an engineering review queue governs the delivery of steel a year out. Why the main equipment is usually not the longest lead. Weather priced into the programme as a normal allowance, with only the abnormal part excusable — and the non-linearity that follows, where a two-month slip across a seasonal boundary can cost six.

## Module 7 — Accept or accelerate
The binary every late project faces. Extension of time as the cheapest way to do the work and the most expensive way to earn revenue. Acceleration as the reverse, with a cost curve that is not linear in time recovered, quality risk concentrated in the least visible operations, and a safety exposure the schedule does not show. Why the answer depends on the owner's daily loss rather than on fault, and why the worst outcome is drifting into neither.

## Pacing
Seven modules, roughly 20 minutes each. Modules 1 and 4 are the conceptual core and should be read together; a reader short of time who reads only those two will still understand why construction projects fail. Module 3 is short and explains a market structure that is otherwise puzzling. Module 6 is best read immediately before the SOLV guide, whose civil scope is where its seasonal argument lands first.
