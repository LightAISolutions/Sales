# Blue Owl — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Blue Owl Capital Inc. (NYSE: OWL), its Credit, Real Assets and GP Strategic Capital platforms, and the funds that own STACK and hold most of Hyperion · **Written:** 2026-10-04 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1) · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no finance background.

**Purpose:** teach how an alternative asset manager reaches AI campuses without buying any equipment itself:
- what a listed manager owns and earns, and why AUM, fee-earning AUM and three kinds of earnings all differ;
- owning a piece of other managers (GP stakes), and what that does and does not decide;
- funds that let investors withdraw each quarter, and why withdrawals get rationed;
- what a manager buys when it buys another manager's business;
- how a hyperscaler moves a campus off its balance sheet with an investor, layer by layer;
- why loans against GPUs are short, expensive and drawn in stages;
- the buying-authority test across Blue Owl's positions, how to read a reported dispute, and the deal ladder.

No company trivia: founding dates, executives, fund sizes and share prices stay in the dossier. The in-app guide (Profiler → Blue Owl → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

The **Blackstone** plan teaches how a listed alternative manager earns, which Module 1 assumes. The **Meta** dossier and the BlackRock plan's El Paso section show the hyperscaler side of off-balance-sheet campuses.

## Module 1 — What a listed manager owns and earns

**The single idea:** shareholders own the management company and its fees; fund investors own the assets.

1. **Three sizes.** Blue Owl reported USD 319.0bn of assets under management (AUM) at 30 June 2026 but only USD 190.6bn of fee-earning AUM. The difference is money committed but not yet invested and capital that pays no fee.
2. **Permanent capital.** Money with no return date — listed and non-traded funds that keep raising. Managers prize it because fees do not stop when a fund's life ends.
3. **Three earnings.** Fee-related earnings (management fees alone, about USD 1.5bn in 2025), distributable earnings (what dividends are paid from, about USD 1.3bn) and net income under accounting rules (about USD 79m, after items the manager's own measures leave out, such as amortisation of intangible assets bought with other managers).
4. **Three platforms.** Credit (USD 158.1bn of AUM), Real Assets (USD 89.4bn, including data-centre Digital Infrastructure at USD 18.4bn) and GP Strategic Capital (USD 71.5bn).

**Self-check:** A manager's AUM rises 20% but its fees barely move. How? *(The new money is committed but not yet invested, or sits in vehicles that pay no fee.)*

## Module 2 — Owning a piece of other managers

**The single idea:** a GP stake earns from another manager's fees and carried interest, not from its deals — and gives no say over its portfolio.

1. **What it is.** A minority share in another asset manager's own business. GP Strategic Capital, formerly Dyal Capital, does this at scale and claims an 87% share of minority-stake deals above USD 600m.
2. **Why buyers sell them.** The selling manager gets permanent capital for its own balance sheet — to seed funds or buy out founders — without giving up control.
3. **The web it creates.** A GP-stakes owner can hold part of a rival: Public Citizen put Blue Owl's holding at 19.3% of Energy Capital Partners, the generation-focused investor, and FERC's first order on ECP's combination with Bridgepoint cited an undisclosed affiliation.

**Self-check:** Blue Owl holds a GP stake in a manager whose fund owns a battery developer. Does Blue Owl decide that developer's purchases? *(No — the stake is in the manager's fee business; the developer's own management, under its fund's governance, decides.)*

## Module 3 — Funds that let investors leave, up to a point

**The single idea:** a fund that lends for years but lets investors withdraw each quarter must ration the exits.

1. **Business development companies (BDCs).** US funds that lend to mid-sized private companies. Listed BDCs trade on an exchange; non-traded BDCs are sold to wealthy individuals.
2. **Tender offers.** A non-traded fund's only exit: an offer to buy back shares, usually up to 5% a quarter. When requests exceed the offer, each investor is paid pro rata and the rest stays in — the redemption gate.
3. **What happened in 2026.** At OCIC, Blue Owl's largest non-traded credit fund, requests were 21.9%, 18.8% and 16.8% of shares in the first three quarters, against a 5% quarterly offer.
4. **Why a seller should care.** Heavy withdrawals slow the new money a manager can lend — including the loans that fund data centres and GPU purchases.

**Self-check:** Why can't a BDC simply pay every withdrawal in full? *(Its assets are multi-year loans that cannot be sold quickly at full value; paying everyone at once would force fire sales that hurt the investors who stay.)*

## Module 4 — Buying a manager's business

**The single idea:** Blue Owl bought IPI Partners' contracts and team, not the data centres IPI's funds own.

1. **What passed.** On 3 January 2025, 'the rights to investment management agreements, investor relationships, related assets and personnel', for USD 1.306bn of booked consideration.
2. **What did not.** STACK Infrastructure and the other platforms stayed with the funds and their limited partners. Blue Owl's own annual report never names STACK; a Blue Owl-managed REIT's report calls it 'a portfolio company owned by certain Other Blue Owl Accounts'.
3. **A wealth-channel REIT.** In December 2025 Blue Owl opened a non-traded REIT for wealthy individuals that owns finished, leased data centres — 12 buildings, 235 MW, 99.7% leased at 30 June 2026. Finished buildings rarely buy new power equipment.

**Self-check:** After the IPI purchase, whose money owns STACK? *(The Blue Owl-managed funds' limited partners and co-investors — Blue Owl the company owns the manager.)*

## Module 5 — Moving a campus off a hyperscaler's balance sheet

**The single idea:** at Hyperion the investor owns most of the venture, the hyperscaler builds and leases it, and the bond market supplies part of the money.

1. **The contributions.** In October 2025 Meta put in USD 4.3bn, largely assets; an affiliate of Blue Owl-managed funds put in USD 7.0bn of cash. Meta took a one-time USD 2.6bn distribution and kept 20%; the Blue Owl funds hold 80%.
2. **The lease.** From 2029, an initial four-year term renewable up to 20 years; Meta manages construction.
3. **Why it stays off Meta's books.** Meta does not direct the activities that most affect the venture's results, so the venture is an unconsolidated variable interest entity and its debt is not Meta's.
4. **The residual value guarantee.** For 16 years Meta pays the shortfall if the campus is worth less than a threshold — which starts at USD 28bn and declines — when it stops leasing. This is what makes a four-year initial lease acceptable to investors and lenders.
5. **Bonds.** Meta's release says part of Blue Owl's capital is 'debt issued to PIMCO and select other bond investors'.
6. **Two numbers, two meanings.** About USD 27bn is the campus's total development cost; USD 7.0bn is the Blue Owl funds' cash in. Never state one as the other.

**Self-check:** At Hyperion, who decides which transformers are installed? *(Meta, as construction manager — the Blue Owl funds are investors in the venture.)*

## Module 6 — Lending against chips

**The single idea:** collateral that loses value fast supports only short, expensive debt, drawn as the collateral arrives.

1. **The IREN example.** About USD 2.4bn for GPUs at IREN's Mackenzie, British Columbia campus: a USD 1.2bn term loan and USD 1.2bn of senior secured notes, both 9.0% fixed, with a Blue Owl BDC as agent and lender and PIMCO advising some note buyers.
2. **Short.** About 30 months, because a GPU's resale value falls quickly as newer generations ship.
3. **Expensive for secured debt.** The rate prices the collateral risk.
4. **Drawn in stages.** Funds are released as chips are delivered, so the lender is never owed more than the equipment in place.
5. **No say over the site.** The lender holds the chips as security; the borrower decides the campus's power equipment.

**Self-check:** Why would a lender accept a ten-year loan on a substation but not on GPUs? *(A substation keeps most of its value for decades; a GPU loses most of it within a few years.)*

## Module 7 — Who signs, how to read a dispute, and the deal ladder

**The single idea:** Blue Owl is a directory — the orders are signed at STACK, at Meta, at Crusoe and at the borrowers.

1. **The buying-authority test.** STACK (fund-owned) passes through its own management; Hyperion fails at Blue Owl because Meta specifies; Abilene fails because Crusoe develops; the Chirisa campuses for CoreWeave pass through their developer; IREN and Nscale fail because Blue Owl is a lender; the REIT's finished buildings rarely buy.
2. **A reported dispute.** On 24 September 2026 Bloomberg reported that Oracle had sent STACK a force-majeure notice on Project Jupiter. Oracle said such notices 'are common on developments of this scale' and that Jupiter 'remains on our planned schedule'. A notice preserves a right to be excused; it is not a cancellation, and its terms are unpublished.
3. **The ladder.** Report (a sale of STACK's Asia-Pacific portfolio) → exclusive talks (the same report) → signing (IREN, 25 August 2026) → closing (IPI Partners; Hyperion; the Lancaster joint venture with Chirisa) → terminated (the merger of two Blue Owl BDCs, November 2025).

**Self-check:** A report says Blue Owl is 'selling STACK Asia for USD 25bn'. What do you record? *(Reported exclusive talks, not a sale — until a party's own release confirms a signing.)*
