## Module 1 — Three clocks nested inside each other

**The single idea:** a data-center outage is a relay race between things that act on different timescales, and every backup layer exists to cover the next layer's start-up gap.

1. **Milliseconds — the power supply.** A server's power supply holds a little charge in capacitors; that reserve is its **hold-up time**, roughly 10–20 ms. Think of it as the coasting distance of a bicycle after you stop pedalling: enough to cross a pothole, not enough to climb a hill. Anything upstream that finishes inside that window is invisible to the server. Nothing with a crankshaft does.
2. **Minutes — the UPS.** The uninterruptible power supply (UPS) is the first machine to act: its inverter picks the hall up from a battery (or a flywheel) inside the hold-up window and can hold it for minutes. It is the referee's stopwatch for everything below.
3. **About ten seconds — the engine.** The automatic transfer switch (ATS) watches the utility, waits a second or two so a flicker does not launch a multi-megawatt engine for nothing, commands a start, and moves the load once the alternator's voltage and frequency are inside limits. The US standard for emergency and standby systems, NFPA 110, classes systems by that **transfer time**; the usual data-center class must be carrying load within ten seconds of losing the supply.

**Worked walk-through (memorize the order, not the numbers):** 0 ms the utility collapses → 0–20 ms the servers coast → the UPS inverter takes the hall → ~1–3 s the ATS times out its start delay and signals → ~2–8 s the starter cranks, fuel fires, the engine climbs to rated speed and the alternator builds voltage → ~8–10 s the ATS opens the utility side and closes the generator side → the whole hall lands on the engine in one step (the **block load**), speed and voltage dip and recover → minutes later the UPS is recharging and chillers are being added back in order → hours later the fleet is running on stored fuel → when the utility returns, a return delay, a transfer back, and a cool-down before the engines stop.

**Two transfer styles, one decision.** *Open transition* breaks before it makes: for a fraction of a second the load is fed by nothing, so the UPS must carry every transfer — including the monthly test. It is simple and can never back-feed the utility. *Closed transition* makes before it breaks: the engine is first **synchronized** to the utility (voltage, frequency, phase angle and rotation matched), both sources feed the load together for under a tenth of a second, and the hall sees no gap. The price is a sync-check relay, protection that separates instantly if the grid is lost, and the utility's permission — because for that instant the campus is a generator on its network. Keep the engines paralleled for minutes instead of milliseconds and you can *soft-load* them, or run them for peak shaving and demand response — which, as Module 4 shows, changes what class of machine you are allowed to own.

**Self-check:** a hall browned out for 40 ms during a test and one row rebooted. Which clock failed? (Longer than hold-up, shorter than any engine start: an open-transition gap with nothing bridging it — the UPS was on bypass, tired, or under-sized. The engines were never the problem.)

## Module 2 — Ratings and block load: what the kilowatt number promises

**One engine, three numbers.** A generator set (**gen-set** — engine, alternator, controls, cooling and fuel system on one frame) carries different kilowatt ratings depending on how it will be used. The rating is a promise about *hours and average load*, not a description of the hardware. **ISO 8528** is the rulebook that makes one maker's kilowatt comparable with another's:

| Rating | Hours a year | Load allowed | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standby** (emergency standby power, ESP) | Limited — a couple of hundred, only while the normal supply is out | Varying, averaged well below the rating; no overload | The backup fleet — the biggest number the engine will ever wear |
| **Limited-time running** (LTP) | Capped at a few hundred | Up to the rating for those hours | Long-but-bounded outages; standby fleets that test under real load |
| **Prime** (PRP) | Unlimited | Varying, averaged around 70 %; short overload allowance | The site's main supply — a bridge plant, a mine, an island utility |
| **Continuous** (COP) | Unlimited | Constant, up to the full rating | A machine run like a power station |

Read *down* the table and the number shrinks: the same engine is smaller prime than standby and smaller again continuous, because the maker is promising it will survive the duty. **Consequence:** a backup fleet cannot be re-declared as the campus's main supply when the grid date slips. The rating, the maintenance interval and — Module 4 — the emission class all move together. Manufacturers also publish data-center-specific ratings that relax the average-load rule for critical facilities; treat those as brand vocabulary on top of the same standard.

**Block load — why a data hall is a hard load to catch.** When the transfer closes, the whole hall arrives on the alternator at once. Speed dips (the engine was carrying nothing an instant ago); voltage dips (the alternator's field takes time to rebuild under current). The governor opens the fuel and the regulator raises excitation; within a second or two both recover *if the step was inside what the set can take*. A turbocharged diesel can typically accept its full rated load in one step, and the rating standard's companion part grades how far speed and voltage may dip and how fast they must recover — critical-load specs call for its tightest common class.

**Why gas engines cannot.** A **lean-burn** gas engine burns with excess air to keep the flame cool and nitrogen oxides (NOx) low. The same excess air leaves less fuel in the cylinder to answer a sudden call for torque, and the turbocharger has to spool before the mixture can be held — so gas sets take load in stages, a fraction of the rating at a time. Fine for a plant that runs all day and sees load creep; wrong for the transfer event. **That is why a campus running on gas still keeps a diesel standby fleet.**

**Derating — ratings assume an atmosphere.** Every machine is rated at a reference temperature and altitude. Hotter or thinner air carries less oxygen per stroke, so output falls above them. A turbocharged engine compresses its intake and shrugs off a fair amount; a gas turbine is a mass-flow machine and loses output steeply on a hot afternoon — exactly when the chillers are working hardest. A fleet sized from nameplate rather than site-rated numbers is one of the quiet ways a backup plant ends up smaller than the hall it protects.

**The load nobody designed for.** Thousands of accelerators starting, stopping or checkpointing together swing a hall's demand by tens of megawatts inside milliseconds. No governor answers in milliseconds. The industry's answer is to put something electronic between the engines and the load — the UPS on a backup site; on a prime-power site a battery on a grid-forming inverter, or synchronous condensers supplying inertia — so the engines see the average and the electronics see the swing. From the engine maker's chair, that buffer is a product line, not a competitor.

**Worked example (arithmetic, not a spec):** a 60 MW hall at design load, N+1 with 3 MW standby-rated sets → 20 sets carry it, 21 installed. Site-rate them at 40 °C and 600 m and you might lose several percent per set → the 20 no longer cover 60 MW, and the "+1" has quietly been spent on the weather. Now suppose the campus must run on those engines for two years: prime rating is smaller still, the annual-hours cap of the standby class is blown in the first month, and the diesels are no longer emergency-class machines. Three different fleets, one building.

## Module 3 — Paralleling, load shed, black start: a room of engines behaving as one plant

**Synchronization.** Two alternators connected out of step push against each other with the full force of the bus behind them: the one ahead in phase throws its load onto the one behind, currents surge, shafts take a torque shock. Synchronizing means matching four things before a breaker closes — voltage, frequency, phase angle and phase rotation — and the sync-check relay physically refuses to close until they match. The first set onto a *dead* bus needs no partner; every set after it must catch up with a bus that is already alive. (Analogy: joining a spinning merry-go-round. You match its speed running alongside, then step on; step on cold and it throws you.)

**Paralleling switchgear** does this automatically and is the most complex electrical gear in the building. Its controller starts the whole fleet on the ATS signal, lets the fastest set claim the dead bus, synchronizes the rest one by one, and transfers the load — so the ten-second clock is met by whichever engine wins the race while the others arrive seconds later.

**Load sharing — droop and isochronous.** Several sets on one bus must agree how to split the load. The usual method is **droop**: each governor lets its speed sag slightly as its load rises, so the machines settle into shares proportional to their size instead of fighting. One machine — or the controller acting for all — runs **isochronous**, holding speed exactly constant, so the bus frequency stays pinned. (Two people carrying a couch: if both insist on setting the pace, they fight; if one sets it and the other yields a little, the load shares.)

**Fleet sizing in data-hall grammar.** N+1 means one more engine than the load needs; the cautious carry more. While the plant runs, the spare is not idle — it is **spinning reserve**, headroom already synchronized and available in seconds, which is what lets the bus absorb a unit trip or a chiller start without a frequency excursion. An outage plan says how many engines must be *running*, not just how many exist.

**Load-shed priority.** If generation runs short, the plant does not go dark — it sheds. Pre-ranked non-critical circuits drop in order (offices, then lower-priority mechanical plant, then whole halls by contract tier) until the remaining engines can carry what is left. The list is written in advance, agreed with tenants, and executed by the controller in a fraction of a second. The add-back runs the list in reverse as engines join. Chillers are the awkward case: a hall rides only minutes on thermal mass, and a big chiller motor starting is itself a block load, so the mechanical plant is staged deliberately — a site's real *thermal* ride-through is often the tighter clock in a long outage.

**The return trip.** The ATS does not trust a returning utility at once: a return delay lets it prove stable, the transfer back is open or closed like the outbound one (a closed return spares the hall a second break), then the engines run unloaded to cool turbochargers and exhaust systems, and stop. Everything is logged — start time, time to load, hours run — because those numbers feed both the maintenance schedule and, in Module 4, a legal limit.

**Black start and the island.** Take the grid away and two things change. First, **black start**: with no outside power the plant boots itself — batteries or small units energize controls and starters, the first engine closes onto the dead bus, the rest synchronize, load returns in stages. Second, the plant *becomes the grid*: connected, an engine's frequency is set for it by a continent of spinning machines; islanded, frequency is whatever the balance between generation and load makes it, and there is no larger system to lean on. Spinning reserve becomes arithmetic; the AI load swing becomes the defining hazard; and the machines that hold frequency through it — synchronous condensers or a grid-forming battery — are specified at the same table as the engines. The **microgrid controller** is the software that runs all of it: which engines run, bus frequency, battery dispatch, shed and restore, and the seams between grid-connected and islanded modes. Engine makers moved into this layer because whoever controls the dispatch controls how many engines are sold and how they are run.

**Where Module 3 fails (the two rows every equipment lesson pins):** *open transition with no stored energy* — the monthly test drops the hall because the UPS was on bypass; *generators that do not synchronize* — a droop setting, a tightened voltage window, a swapped engine, and the fleet starts on time while the bus stays empty; *load not shed in priority* — the list was written for a building that has since changed. All three present as electrical faults and are settings faults, proven only under real load.

## Module 4 — Diesel, gas, and what the air permit constrains

**The shift, stated honestly.** For decades the data-center engine question had one answer: diesel, standby-rated, tested monthly, run a few hours a year. AI campuses that need power for years before the grid arrives changed the question — not *from backup to something else*, but to backup *plus* a second, different plant. Compare the two on what a campus actually buys:

| | Diesel gen-set | Natural-gas gen-set |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | Full block load, start-to-load inside the ten-second class, fuel on site, highest power density, permitted quickly as an emergency generator | Duration nearly free (the fuel lives in a pipeline), lean-burn cuts NOx at the source and cuts CO₂ per unit energy by roughly a quarter, modular fleets run near full load, months not years to deliver |
| Weakness | Emergency class caps non-emergency hours; prime or demand-response use forces Tier 4 with SCR and a particulate filter; highest NOx and particulates per hour; fuel logistics do not scale to months; idling fouls the engine (wet stacking) | Takes load in steps, so it is a poor transfer machine and keeps a diesel fleet or a battery beside it; the pipeline is a single point of failure no engine redundancy fixes; lower output per set; prime operation moves the plant into a permit written around hours, tons per year and aggregation |

Two further machine families compete for the prime role and are taught in the campus-power lessons: the gas turbine (tens of MW per unit, fast to start, derates in hot air, dislikes part load) and the fuel cell (no flame, near-zero NOx, prefers steady load). An engine maker with a turbine subsidiary sells across that boundary — engines where the campus wants granularity and permits by the block, turbines where it wants megawatts per machine — while its diesel line keeps the standby duty in both cases.

**The permit, from the engine's side.** Air law regulates what leaves the stack, and the pollutant it mostly cares about is **NOx** — nitrogen oxides make ground-level ozone downwind, and every metro region with a data-center cluster is watching its ozone number.

1. **It decides what class of machine you own.** An **emergency generator** may run when the supply fails, plus up to a hundred hours a year for testing, maintenance and the like; in exchange it is held to an earlier emission stage and permitted with little review. Run the same engine for prime power, peak shaving or a **demand-response** payment and it is a *non-emergency* engine: it must meet **Tier 4**, which in practice means **selective catalytic reduction** (urea or ammonia injected across a catalyst to turn NOx into nitrogen and water) plus a particulate filter — a larger, hotter after-treatment package on the skid. The run-hour meter on the controller is a legal instrument.
2. **It decides how big the plant may be before the review changes character.** Potential emissions are counted for the whole site. Above the thresholds the plant is a **major source**: modelling, public comment, best-available-control analysis, years. Below them it is a **minor source**, and a plant can stay below deliberately by accepting enforceable limits on hours or fuel — a *synthetic minor*. That is why on-site power is packaged in blocks sized to the permit rather than to the engineering, why a campus in a **non-attainment area** pays a premium for the lowest-emitting machine, and why the unresolved question of whether dozens of nominally separate or mobile units running together count as one source is the live legal risk over the whole category.
3. **It rewrites the product.** The diesel that sells into the emergency class is standby-rated, certified to the earlier stage, and optimized for block load and cost per kilowatt of a machine that seldom runs. The gas engine that sells into the non-emergency class is prime- or continuous-rated, certified as a spark-ignition stationary engine with its own NOx limits, and optimized for efficiency and emissions over thousands of hours. The turbine that sells into the bridge-plant class carries a low-NOx combustor and, above a certain size, SCR of its own. Three engineering targets, one campus — and the permit, not the purchasing department, sorts a project into them.

**Where a gen-set ends and a bridge-power plant begins** — the same word covers two products:

| Dimension | Backup gen-set fleet | Bridge-power plant |
|---|---|---|
| Rating | Standby | Prime or continuous |
| Machine | Diesel, 1–4 MW per set, full block load | Gas engines or turbines; diesel only as the backup layer |
| Fuel | Tanks for a day or two, refilled by truck | Pipeline with firm supply contracted; storage or virtual pipeline as the hedge |
| Hours | A few a year | Thousands, until the grid arrives |
| Permit | Emergency class, earlier stage, light review | Non-emergency: Tier 4 or spark-ignition limits, minor-source packaging or major-source review, tons-per-year caps |
| Controls | ATS + paralleling switchgear running an outage sequence | Microgrid controller dispatching engines, storage and the grid tie, with black start designed in |
| Stability | UPS carries the transfer and hides the swing | The plant is the grid: reserve, inertia or a grid-forming battery holds frequency through every transient |
| Who runs it | The operator via a dealer service agreement and a test calendar | Often the supplier or a power partner under a long-term service or power-delivery contract with availability guarantees |
| Where it ends | Never | When the interconnection energizes — the engines drop back to backup and the permit is amended |

The seam is the moment a campus decides it cannot wait for the grid. From then on the buyer is procuring a power station with data-hall redundancy grammar, and the conversation moves from kilowatts per skid to fuel contracts, permit strategy and who is accountable when the plant does not behave as a plant.

**The ladder.** Rated megawatts per machine, lowest to highest: D1500 1.5 → G3520K 2.55 (continuous) → 3516E 3.0 → G3520 Fast Response 3.12 → C175-20 4.0 → CG260-16 4.5 → medium-speed 10 → SMT130 16 → Titan 130 16.5 → Titan 250 23.1 → Titan 350 38. Above the top rung sit the heavy frames at hundreds of megawatts each. A campus that needs a gigawatt in eighteen months either stacks many rungs or waits years for a machine that is not on the ladder — and no single Cat machine does every job: the diesel wins the transfer, the gas engine wins the years, the turbine wins megawatts per unit, the battery wins the milliseconds. The company's bet is that owning all four, plus the switchgear and the controller, is worth more than being best at one.

**Who buys (and through whom).** Standby fleets are bought by the data-center operator or its electrical contractor through a Cat dealer, usually under design-assist with the switchgear and a service agreement attached. Prime and bridge plants are increasingly bought by *power partners* — developers and power-as-a-service operators (the Joule, Monarch, Hunt and PROPWR agreements are all of that shape) — with the dealer delivering and Cat Financial funding, and the hyperscaler or AI lab as the end user rather than the purchaser. Turbines are the exception: Solar sells them direct.

**Who competes, by machine family.** *Diesel standby:* Cummins (the ~16 % second share), Rolls-Royce Power Systems mtu, Rehlko (Kohler), Mitsubishi. *High- and medium-speed gas engines:* Wärtsilä (medium-speed, more than 1.6 GW across four US data-center projects), INNIO Jenbacher (VoltaGrid's supplier), Cummins; the linear-generator and fuel-cell vendors (Mainspring, Bloom) compete on emissions rather than on the same physics. *Mid-size turbines:* Solar's 5–40 MW band has few direct rivals; the heavy-frame majors — GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi Power — bound it from above and co-supply the same sites. *Storage and controls:* the storage integrators on block size; the electrical incumbents on ATS and switchgear.

**Why the transition matters economically.** The standby franchise was a fleet that ran a few hours a year and earned mostly on the sale and a light service calendar. A prime-rated gas fleet runs thousands of hours, consumes fuel and parts continuously, and needs the dealer every month — which is why the company's own arithmetic says a continuous gas set is worth an order of magnitude more in services over its life than a standby diesel. That is also why the permit (Module 4) is a commercial event for the OEM: it is the boundary between the small-annuity product and the large-annuity product.
