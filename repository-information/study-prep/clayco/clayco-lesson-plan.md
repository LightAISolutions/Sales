## Module 1 — Who holds the risk: design-build, CM at risk, construction management

**The single idea:** the contract form decides whether the general contractor designs, buys and builds, or merely coordinates.

1. **Design-build.** One contract for design and construction. The GC's own or contracted designer writes the basis of design; the GC prices the job as a lump sum or a guaranteed maximum price and usually procures the major equipment inside it. Fastest, because there is no redesign cycle between an owner's engineer and a builder; riskiest for the GC, which carries design and cost.
2. **CM at risk.** The owner's architect and engineer design; the GC joins early, prices a GMP and builds. Long-lead gear — generators, UPS, switchgear, chillers — is often owner-furnished; the GC buys the rest.
3. **Construction management (agency).** The owner's team designs and buys; the GC coordinates subcontractors for a fee. This is how most hyperscale campuses run, and it is how this company's flagship AI job ran — 'a construction management structure' with more than a hundred subcontractors on site.
4. **Turnkey EPC.** Engineer, procure, construct for a fixed scope — the form used for solar and storage plants, now offered by this GC's energy unit.
5. **For a seller.** Under design-build the GC's procurement team runs your bid; under agency CM the owner does and the GC installs. Ask which form the campus uses before you pitch.

**Self-check:** a hyperscaler's own engineers design the hall and the hyperscaler buys the generators. What does the GC decide about your product? *(Installation sequence and fit — not the purchase.)*

## Module 2 — Self-perform: owning the trades that set the schedule

**The single idea:** a GC that employs its own craft for the critical-path trades can promise sequence instead of negotiating it — until it runs out of crews.

1. **Concrete first.** Foundations, slabs and tilt-up or precast walls are the first months of every data hall; a GC with its own concrete company (here one pouring close to a million cubic yards a year) controls the hand-over to the trades that follow.
2. **Enclosure second.** A watertight shell releases electrical and mechanical rough-in; an owned curtain-wall and enclosure firm closes it on the GC's clock.
3. **MEP self-perform.** The hardest to build and the most valuable: mechanical and electrical are where hyperscale schedules slip. A GC that fields its own MEP crews — here a 'systems and equipment' arm doing preconstruction, purchasing and installation — removes a layer of negotiation on the critical path.
4. **The ceiling.** Craft must be hired, trained and kept busy. When a client doubled a follow-on phase on the same timeline, this company lost it to a rival, with the client citing 'project commitments' and 'the necessary labor and workforces'. The dossier treats that as the defining fact of 2026.
5. **For a seller.** A self-performing GC's own electricians and pipefitters set your equipment; its preconstruction team may also specify it.

**Self-check:** what did the lost follow-on phase reveal that the ranking did not? *(That labour capacity, not demand or rank, was the binding constraint.)*

## Module 3 — Speed to power

**The single idea:** owners pay for time to energised capacity, and three levers compress it.

1. **The sequence.** Site and civil → foundations and shell → equipment yard (generators, switchgear, transformers) in parallel with interior rough-in → equipment set → commissioning Level 1 through the integrated systems test → energisation → tenant fit-out.
2. **Lever one: offsite fabrication.** Power assemblies, chiller skids and piping racks built in a shop and set as units move hours off the critical path and arrive tested.
3. **Lever two: design-build.** No redesign cycle; the designer and the builder answer to the same manager.
4. **Lever three: concurrent engineering.** Foundations pour while the electrical design closes; the yard is graded before the generator order is final.
5. **The worked example.** The dossier's flagship: 'more than 100 integrated power assemblies and more than 90 chillers' with 'significant off-site assembly' and an on-site batch plant — all three levers on one liquid-cooled site.

**Self-check:** why does an on-site concrete batch plant speed a remote campus? *(It removes truck-cycle and supply constraints on the largest early-phase material.)*

## Module 4 — Converting a bitcoin mine into an AI data centre

**The single idea:** the mine has the interconnection; everything downstream is rebuilt for density and resilience.

1. **What transfers.** The utility interconnection and the transformers — the scarcest, slowest assets in the industry.
2. **What does not.** Bitcoin mines run cheap ASICs with air cooling, no UPS and no redundancy; an AI tenant needs UPS, 2N or N+1 distribution, liquid cooling to 100 kW racks and heavier floors.
3. **The mechanical rebuild.** Chillers and coolant distribution units replace air handlers; piping reaches every rack; heat rejection per square foot rises several-fold.
4. **The GC's craft.** Sequencing a live site: new foundations and steel beside operating infrastructure, chillers set as they arrive, hand-over in tranches so the tenant takes capacity progressively.

**Self-check:** name the one asset a bitcoin site brings that a greenfield campus lacks on day one. *(The energised grid interconnection.)*

## Module 5 — What a data-centre contractor ranking measures

**The single idea:** rankings are self-reported revenue by building type; the basis matters more than the rank.

1. **BD+C's data-centre table.** Firms report the prior year's revenue they attribute to data-centre facilities; the publisher ranks the numbers. It says nothing about megawatts, self-perform share or whether the firm was GC or CM. A USD 10m gap on USD 3.6bn is a tie.
2. **ENR Top 400.** Construction revenue only, by firm and by market sector; a GC with a development arm reports less here than its own boilerplate says, because development revenue is excluded.
3. **Design-build lists.** Revenue delivered under design-build contracts; they do not say who designed.
4. **Company claims.** A 'top three' on a company page is a claim until matched to the publisher's table; this dossier could not match one.
5. **For a seller.** Use the ranking to size the book and name the peer set; use the dossier's project record to learn what the GC actually controls.

**Self-check:** two GCs report USD 3.65bn and USD 3.64bn of data-centre revenue. Which builds more megawatts? *(Unknown — the table does not measure that.)*

## Module 6 — Powered land and the develop-design-build model

**The single idea:** a developer arm lets a GC control the timeline from land to lease — and exposes it to the politics of entitlement.

1. **Powered shell and powered land.** A shell has grid power and cooling ready for racks; powered land has the interconnection, water and zoning secured but nothing built. Both are sold or leased to hyperscalers and colocation operators who want to skip years of entitlement.
2. **Develop-design-build.** Assemble the land, win rezoning and the utility agreement, design and build the shell, lease it, repeat.
3. **The exposure.** Rezoning hundreds of acres for a gigawatt campus is a public act. The record: a USD 1bn campus withdrawn under a petition and a moratorium in one city; a USD 6bn campus approved 6–2 in another, followed within a week by the defeat of four council incumbents and a twelve-count open-meetings suit.
4. **For a seller.** The developer arm is a second door: it signs for substations and sometimes for generation and storage before a tenant is named — and it is where a GC's local reputation is made or lost.

**Self-check:** why is the developer affiliate more exposed than the construction business? *(Entitlement is decided in council chambers and elections; a builder hired afterwards avoids that stage.)*

## Module 7 — When the owner stops paying

**The single idea:** a design-builder on a stalled plant is lending the owner working capital; the lien measures only the unpaid tail.

1. **Progress billing.** The builder pays subcontractors monthly and bills the owner for work performed.
2. **Suspension.** An owner that halts a battery plant at three-quarters complete — citing policy and market uncertainty — stops paying while the builder's obligations to subcontractors continue.
3. **The mechanic's lien.** A claim against the property for unpaid work, enforced by a foreclosure suit. Here USD 26m sued for against more than USD 1bn performed.
4. **The lesson.** The exposure is the receivable the builder carried while paying 'every subcontractor in full… using our own funds' — a private balance sheet absorbing an owner's policy risk.

**Self-check:** what does the lien amount understate? *(The working capital advanced to the owner over the months before suspension.)*

## Module 8 — A GC's solar-and-storage unit

**The single idea:** a new EPC unit inside a data-centre GC is a new procurement channel for modules, inverters and batteries — once it names an award.

1. **Turnkey EPC.** Engineer the plant, procure modules, inverters and batteries, build and commission for a utility, an IPP or a financial sponsor.
2. **What a GC brings.** Craft labour, project controls and, here, 'data center operators' on its own customer list — which suggests behind-the-meter storage at campuses it already builds.
3. **What it lacks.** Supplier relationships; the first named projects reveal the battery and inverter makers it chooses.
4. **For a seller.** Track the unit's first named award, its MW and its supplier; a USD 300m-by-2027 target is a plan, not a purchase order.

**Self-check:** what single event would turn this unit from a watch item into a customer? *(A named solar-plus-storage award with a stated battery supplier.)*

## Risks to keep in view (from the dossier)

- Labour capacity: a marquee follow-on phase lost to a rival on crews and commitments.
- Revenue opacity: two company figures for the same year; no margin or backlog disclosure.
- Developer politics: moratoria, a council wipe-out and litigation in Missouri.
- Owner default: more than USD 1bn performed on a suspended plant.
