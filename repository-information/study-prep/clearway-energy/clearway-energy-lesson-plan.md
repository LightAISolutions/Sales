# Clearway Energy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach **how contracted American renewables are actually financed, and why the accounts of a company that owns them look broken when they are not**. Specifically: what a yieldco is and what it deliberately is not; why cash available for distribution and net income can point in opposite directions in the same year; how tax equity and hypothetical-liquidation-at-book-value accounting move hundreds of millions of loss onto someone else's line; what a gas plant sells when it runs 1% of the year; why a fully contracted portfolio still misses guidance when the wind drops; and how to read a battery fleet quoted at two different capacity numbers.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **ENGIE** plan teaches what a merchant battery sells inside an hour; the **AES** plan teaches building a gigawatt; the **RWE** plan teaches what an interconnection is worth. This is the **ownership and financing** plan: what happens to a renewable asset after it is built, who holds it, and how the cash reaches a shareholder. The **Recurrent** plan (same session) is its mirror image — the developer's side of the same transaction.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** A yieldco is a distribution vehicle wearing the clothes of an operating company, and almost every oddity in its accounts follows from that. The GAAP bottom line of a tax-equity-financed renewables owner is close to uninformative, and the number that constrains behaviour is a non-GAAP one with no analyst consensus. And a contract removes price risk while leaving volume risk exactly where it was.

**One boundary, held throughout.** **Clearway Energy, Inc. (NYSE: CWEN)** and **Clearway Energy Group** are different legal persons. The listed company owns operating assets and has no employees. The private sponsor — owned 50/50 by Global Infrastructure Partners, now part of BlackRock, and TotalEnergies — employs everyone, develops everything, and holds all the Class B and Class D shares. Most published confusion about this company is a failure to keep them apart; at least one trade outlet has described the listed company as the sponsor's parent, which is backwards. Also: **CWEN.A no longer exists** — every Class A share converted into Class C on 1 May 2026 — and **there is no ROFO agreement**; the term appears zero times in the FY2025 10-K.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 with the FY2025 10-K income statement open (~30 min). Module 2 with the tax-equity note beside it (~35 min). Module 3 with the Item 2 fleet table (~25 min). Module 4 with the 16 July 2026 operational update (~20 min). Module 5 with the 1 May 2026 8-K (~25 min). Then flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — Two income statements in one filing

**The single idea:** the loss and the dividend are both real, and they measure different things.

**The worked case.** FY2025: total operating revenues $1,429m, depreciation $(682)m, net loss $(231)m — and a dividend of $1.77 a share, up from $1.65. Cash available for distribution was $430m, at the top of the original guidance range.

**Where the gap comes from.** Two places. Depreciation is a real expense and no cash. And $(400)m of the consolidated loss was allocated to non-controlling and redeemable non-controlling interests, leaving net income *attributable to Clearway Energy, Inc.* at a positive $169m.

**The metric nobody benchmarks.** CAFD is company-defined. No published analyst consensus exists for it in any period — sell-side models cover EPS and revenue. So a CAFD "beat" is measured against the company's own guidance and nothing else. Note the asymmetry: the market has a consensus for the number that does not drive the dividend, and none for the number that does.

**Exercise:** take FY2024 (revenue $1,371m, net loss $(63)m, Adjusted EBITDA $1,146m, CAFD $425m) and FY2025. Compute the change in each line. Then explain in one sentence why net loss got worse by $168m while CAFD improved by $5m.

## Module 2 — Tax equity, and the accounting that hides in it

**The single idea:** American renewables are part-financed by selling tax benefits, and the method used to split the resulting partnership's profit distorts the income statement by design.

**The mechanism.** An investor with a large tax bill puts cash into a project and takes most of the credits and most of the taxable loss for a period; the developer keeps the cash and the asset. The partnership's profit share changes over time, which ordinary consolidation cannot express.

**HLBV.** Hypothetical liquidation at book value asks, each period: if we liquidated at book value today, what would each partner receive? The change in that answer is the partner's income or loss. That is what produced the $(400)m line — not a real loss of $400m, but a fall in a hypothetical claim.

**Why it is not merely academic here.** In January 2026 Clearway disclosed a **material weakness** in exactly this control and PwC issued an **adverse opinion** on internal control for FY2025. The consequent revisions were immaterial and changed neither net income nor operating cash flow. It was unremediated at the year end. Weigh it as a diligence item in the mechanism that carries the tax-credit economics — not as a restatement.

**The direction of travel.** Credits became directly transferable under the 2022 law, so a developer can now sell a credit for cash rather than assemble a partnership. Simpler and cheaper — but existing partnerships run for years, so HLBV will distort these statements for a long time.

**Exercise:** explain to someone who has never seen a tax-equity partnership why a company can report a large net loss, a positive net income attributable to its own shareholders, and a record distributable cash figure, all in the same year, without any of the three being misleading.

## Module 3 — What the fleet is, and what "megawatts" means twice

**The single idea:** rated capacity is the plant; net capacity is the ownership. Confusing them roughly doubles the answer.

**The fleet at 30 June 2026:** 13,872 MW rated / 10,665 MW net across 27 states. Wind 4,234 MW, utility solar 4,730 MW, storage 1,439 MW, distributed solar 699 MW, gas 2,770 MW.

**The storage detail that matters.** 844 MW of California batteries is 391 MW net, because most are held at 25–50% in partnerships. Honeycomb's 320 MW in Utah is 320 MW net — wholly owned. So a rated-to-rated comparison against a wholly-owned fleet is not a comparison.

**What a peaker sells.** 2,770 MW of California gas generated 323 GWh in FY2025, down from 847 GWh, and produced $210m of Adjusted EBITDA. It is selling resource adequacy — availability at peak, priced in megawatts — not energy. This is the same product a four-hour battery sells, which is why ELCC accreditation is the exchange rate between them.

**The undisclosed variable.** Marsh Landing (820 MW, contracts to 2026–2030) and Walnut Creek (501 MW, 2026–2027) recontracted in Q2 2026 on terms that were not published. That is the largest unknown in the company.

**Exercise:** given 844 MW rated and 391 MW net across seven California projects at stated ownership of 25%, 25%, 40%, 40%, 50%, 50% and 100%, reconstruct which projects carry which stake. Then say what the exercise tells you about how much of the fleet's growth is capital and how much is consolidation.

## Module 4 — Why a contracted portfolio still misses

**The single idea:** a contract fixes price, not volume.

**What happened.** On 5 August 2026 Clearway cut 2026 CAFD guidance from $470–510m to $430–470m — its first cut in three years. The 16 July operational update gives the reason in the company's own words: an El Niño pattern left measured CAISO wind at **87% of P-50** and ERCOT at 98%, with Q2 renewable output about 5% below the guidance mid-point requirement.

**P-50.** The output a plant is expected to exceed half the time — the central case a financing is built on. Falling short is not a failure of the asset or the contract; it is weather.

**The defences, and their limits.** Geographic diversity helps and Clearway has it. Contracts written per megawatt of capacity rather than per megawatt-hour remove the exposure entirely — which is precisely what the gas resource-adequacy agreements and the battery tolls are. That is a real argument for a mixed fleet.

**Read the market reaction.** The same quarter beat on adjusted EPS ($1.00 against $0.38) and revenue, and the shares fell 1.73%. The market priced the guidance.

**Exercise:** a 4,234 MW wind fleet runs at a 30% capacity factor in a P-50 year. Compute annual MWh. Then recompute at 87% of P-50 and, at $30/MWh, state the revenue difference. Compare it to the $40m guidance cut and say what else must be in the bridge.

## Module 5 — The structure: sponsor, drop-downs and the share-class collapse

**The single idea:** control was preserved through a change that looked like it gave control away.

**The chain.** GIP III Zephyr Acquisition Partners is the sole member of Clearway Energy Group; TotalEnergies Renewables USA holds 50% of Zephyr's general partner. The 2026 proxy states it plainly: "GIP and TotalEnergies each own 50% of CEG through intermediate holding companies." BlackRock's absorption of GIP changed the filer name, not the chain.

**The drop-down process.** No ROFO. The sponsor *offers*; the listed company's **independent directors** review and approve. Contractual scaffolding: a Master Services Agreement (about $23.8m paid in FY2025) and a Master Development Services Agreement with Clearway Renew signed 22 December 2025.

**The conversion.** Stockholders approved on 29 April 2026; all Class A became Class C at 12:01 a.m. on 1 May; CWEN.A was suspended and a Form 25-NSE filed. Simultaneously the sponsor put **41,678,637 Class B shares** into a Wilmington Trust voting trust whose trustee must vote **in the same proportion as all stockholders**. Post-conversion voting power: Class B 39.48%, Class C 45.12%, Class D 15.40% — reproducing the pre-conversion 54.88/45.12 split exactly.

**Read that carefully.** The public got one deeper, more liquid class. The sponsor gave up no relative voting power. Both statements are true and the transaction was designed so they would be.

**Exercise:** state the conflict of interest in a drop-down in one sentence, name the three mitigations present here, and then say which of the three you would rely on if you were a minority holder — and why the absence of a ROFO makes that question harder rather than easier.
