# CPP Investments — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Canada Pension Plan Investment Board (CPP Investments), with Pattern Energy, ALLETE/Minnesota Power, atNorth and the Equinix xScale venture · **Written:** 2026-10-03 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1) · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no finance background.

**Purpose:** teach how a pension fund with no customers and no fund clock comes to control several of the companies that buy power and data-centre equipment, and where in that structure the purchase orders are actually written:
- why pension capital holds where fund capital must sell, and what a benchmark is;
- the control ladder — what 12%, 37.5%, 40%, 51% and 69% each let an owner do;
- why a percentage at signing is not the percentage at closing;
- how a regulated utility earns, and why its capital plan is the order book;
- how a developer finances one project at a time, and who signs its contracts;
- the four routes into data centres and who runs each;
- when the pension fund is the lender rather than the owner;
- the politics of a public fund.

No company trivia: founding dates, executives and net-asset figures stay in the dossier. The in-app guide (Profiler → CPP Investments → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **BlackRock** plan teaches the co-owner's side of ALLETE and the GIP machinery; the **KKR** plan teaches the consortium that CPP joined for Sempra Infrastructure Partners; the **Blackstone** plan teaches the majority owner's side of AirTrunk. The **Pattern Energy** dossier carries SunZia's project-finance detail.

**Suggested pacing (before the mid-November 2026 results):** Module 1 (~15 min), Module 2 (~15 min), Module 3 (~10 min), Module 4 (~15 min), Module 5 (~15 min), Module 6 (~10 min), Module 7 (~10 min), Module 8 (~10 min), then the flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — Pension capital has no clock

**The single idea:** a pension fund invests its members' money for decades and is judged against a simple public-market benchmark, so it can hold an asset for as long as it earns its place — a fund manager cannot.

1. **Where the money comes from.** Contributions from Canadian workers and employers flow into the Fund; benefits flow out over decades. Net transfers in were C$22bn in the year to March 2026 — the Fund is still growing from contributions, not only from returns.
2. **What it is measured against.** A benchmark portfolio of public stocks and bonds the Fund could have bought instead. In fiscal 2026 the Fund earned 7.8% against a benchmark of 13.2%, because the benchmark was full of large technology stocks. The department holding the platforms, Real Assets, earned about 12%.
3. **Why there is no sale date.** A closed-end fund must return money after about ten years. CPP has no such promise; its stated objective is maximum return without undue risk over the long term. Pattern Energy, bought in 2020, was enlarged in 2026 rather than sold.
4. **What that means for a seller.** A platform owned by a pension fund has an owner who is not leaving. Investment in a platform's next build depends on returns, not on a fund's exit timetable.
5. **The limit.** Scale and politics. A C$860bn fund cannot write small cheques, and a public fund can be told where to invest (Module 8).

**Self-check:** a fund manager and a pension fund each own a wind developer. Which is more likely to merge another renewables company into it rather than sell? *(The pension fund — it has no exit obligation and can consolidate holdings; the manager must realise cash by its fund's end.)*

## Module 2 — The control ladder

**The single idea:** the percentage owned tells you who decides, but only together with the shareholders' agreement that sets the reserved matters.

1. **Minority with consent rights (12%).** At AirTrunk CPP holds 12% beside Blackstone's majority: a board seat, information rights and a veto on a short list of reserved matters — a sale, new debt, the annual budget. Strategy and procurement belong to the operator under the majority owner.
2. **Joint control in a venture (37.5%).** In the Equinix xScale US venture CPP and GIC each hold 37.5% and Equinix 25%. Nothing large happens without both 37.5% partners agreeing, but Equinix designs, builds and operates the halls. CPP's own words: it 'will control a 37.5% equity interest'.
3. **Co-ownership of a utility (40%).** At ALLETE CPP holds 40% beside GIP's 60%; the corpus's BlackRock dossier records governance as shared equally, from ALLETE's proxy. Either way the utility remains regulated and its engineers run it.
4. **Majority of a platform (51%).** At atNorth CPP appoints the board majority and sets the plan; Equinix (34%) and Partners Group (10%) hold minority protections.
5. **Majority of a developer (69.1% with others).** At Pattern CPP is 'the majority shareholder', in a group that includes two other pension investors; management runs the projects.

**Self-check:** why might a 37.5% holder have more say than a 40% holder? *(Because 37.5% in a venture where the other large partner also has 37.5% means neither can act alone — joint control — whereas 40% beside a 60% partner is a minority with agreed protections.)*

## Module 3 — Signing is not closing

**The single idea:** an agreement states intent and price; a completion states ownership — and rollover by sellers or managers can change the split in between.

1. **atNorth.** February 2026: CPP to pay about USD 1.6bn for about 60%, Equinix about 40%. September 2026: CPP about 51% for USD 1.3bn, Equinix about 34%, the seller Partners Group re-invested for about 10%, managers rolled their shares. The enterprise value (USD 4bn) did not move; the buyers' percentages did.
2. **Sempra Infrastructure Partners.** Agreed September 2025 (CPP about 13% indirectly for about USD 3.0bn beside KKR); Sempra expected closing in the third quarter of 2026 and attached a ticking fee and a twelve-month long-stop. No completion filing had appeared by late August. Until it does, the stake is a contract, not a holding.
3. **Why rollover happens.** Sellers who believe in the asset keep a slice; managers are given equity to stay. Both dilute the incoming owners.
4. **For a seller.** Address the owner the completion names. A supplier who read the February release would have expected a 60/40 board.

**Self-check:** a release says a fund 'will own approximately 60%'. What single document do you wait for before relying on that number? *(The completion release or filing — the only statement of ownership as it actually is.)*

## Module 4 — Owning a regulated utility

**The single idea:** the regulator sets the utility's prices and allowed return; the owner funds the capital plan and appoints directors, and the capital plan is the order book.

1. **The bargain.** A regulated utility has a monopoly service territory and an obligation to serve; in exchange a state commission sets rates to recover prudent costs plus an allowed return on the rate base — the invested capital in the grid.
2. **What the ALLETE approval attached.** A one-year freeze on base rates; the allowed return cut from 9.78% to 9.65% with a cap to 2030; USD 50m of customer credits; a commitment to fund a USD 5bn five-year capital plan; USD 50m toward clean firm power; Minnesota-based independent directors. An administrative law judge had recommended rejection; the commission approved unanimously with these terms.
3. **Where the orders are.** Transformers, switchgear, storage and generation in the five-year plan are bought by Minnesota Power's engineers; the commission later decides what was prudent. The first rate case after the freeze (2027) is where that is tested.
4. **Why a pension fund wants this.** Regulated returns matched to long liabilities — the same logic as an insurer's. The price is a public process every time the owner wants something.

**Self-check:** the new owners want the utility to add 400 MW of storage not in the approved plan. Who must agree first? *(The state commission — spending outside the approved plan may not be recovered in rates.)*

## Module 5 — Owning a developer: project finance

**The single idea:** a developer's projects are financed one at a time on non-recourse debt, so each project company is its own borrower and its own buyer.

1. **The structure.** Pattern Energy develops wind, solar and transmission; each project sits in its own company that borrows against its own revenue contract. SunZia — 3,650 MW of wind and a 550-mile HVDC line — carries about USD 11bn of such debt.
2. **Non-recourse.** The lenders can claim only that project's assets and revenue, so they scrutinise everything about it: the power contract, the equipment, the contractor, the schedule. Their independent engineer reviews the turbine, inverter and battery choices before money is released.
3. **Where CPP sits.** Above the projects: it funds Pattern's corporate equity, folded its other renewables company into Pattern for shares in 2026, and has participated directly in a holding-company facility. It does not choose suppliers.
4. **The ownership number.** CPP's stake 'together with other institutional investors' is 69.1%; Pattern calls CPP the majority shareholder; two other pension investors hold the balance beside management. Nobody has published the split.

**Self-check:** a project company's lenders reject a battery supplier. Can the developer's majority owner override them? *(Not in practice — the lenders control the money for that project; the owner would have to fund the project without them.)*

## Module 6 — Four routes into data centres

**The single idea:** a pension fund can reach the same industry four ways — control an operator, co-own a venture, back a manager's platform, or commit to a developer — and only the first puts its people near a purchase order.

1. **Control an operator.** atNorth (Nordic colocation, about 1 GW secured): c. 51% at completion on 2 September 2026, beside Equinix (c. 34%) and Partners Group (c. 10%). The owner appoints the board; atNorth's engineers still specify the switchgear and chillers.
2. **Co-own a venture.** The Equinix xScale US venture: more than USD 15bn of capital with GIC, CPP's initial equity up to USD 2.4bn for a 37.5% controlling interest. Equinix develops and operates; the partners approve budgets and reserved matters.
3. **Back a manager's platform.** USD 1.75bn behind EQT's AI-infrastructure strategy led by EdgeConneX (closed July 2026); a 50/50 A$14bn European partnership with Goodman (435 MW across Paris, Frankfurt and Amsterdam in the first tranche); 8.2% of CtrlS plus 48% of a campus joint venture in India. The manager or developer makes every operating decision.
4. **Commit to a developer.** USD 310m to Vantage Data Centers and USD 200m across Vantage and Yondr; 12% of AirTrunk beside Blackstone. Here CPP is one name on a capitalisation table.
5. **The seller's map.** Routes 1 and 2 have a CPP director in the room; routes 3 and 4 do not. The equipment conversation is always with the operator or developer, whichever route the money took.

**Self-check:** CPP commits USD 310m to a data-centre developer with no stake percentage stated. Who do you ask about transformer specifications? *(The developer's engineering team — the commitment makes CPP a funder, not a decision-maker on equipment.)*

## Module 7 — When the fund is the lender, not the owner

**The single idea:** a lender is repaid first and controls nothing day to day; it takes security over assets and sets covenants, but it does not choose what the borrower buys.

1. **The GPU loan.** CPP's credit department lent USD 250m to CoreWeave secured on GPU servers (fiscal 2025) and USD 150m through a delayed-draw term loan for four CoreWeave data centres in the US and Canada (first quarter of fiscal 2027). CPP holds no CoreWeave equity.
2. **Construction lending.** C$225m, half of a construction loan arranged with Deutsche Bank, for a 54 MW hyperscale expansion in Cambridge, Ontario pre-leased to an AI cloud provider — the lender's engineer signs off on each drawdown against progress.
3. **Lending through others.** A USD 1.5bn separately managed account and a USD 1bn financing with Blackstone's private-credit platform: CPP's money, Blackstone's underwriting.
4. **Why it matters here.** A delayed-draw facility pays for data-centre fit-out as it happens — the borrower's purchases are the lender's collateral, so the loan documents describe the equipment without the lender ever choosing it.
5. **Reading a release.** 'Loan facility', 'term loan', 'construction financing' and 'SMA' mean debt. 'Stake', 'interest' and 'equity' mean ownership. The same release often carries both.

**Self-check:** a fund announces a USD 150m delayed-draw term loan to a cloud provider's four data centres. Does the fund own those data centres? *(No — it is a secured lender; the borrower owns them and chooses the equipment.)*

## Module 8 — The politics of a public fund

**The single idea:** a fund that invests Canadians' contributions answers to Parliament and the provinces, so where it invests is a political question even when its mandate says it need not be.

1. **The mandate.** The CPP Investments Act requires investing for maximum return without undue risk; it does not require investing in Canada. About 12% of the Fund is Canadian.
2. **The pressure.** In May 2026 the Senate Finance committee chair said pension funds 'need to invest more in Canada' and favoured a legislated mandate; CPP answered with more than C$115bn already invested domestically.
3. **The voluntary answer.** On 15 September 2026 CPP and Brookfield launched the Maple Fund — up to C$50bn, 50/50, for Canadian infrastructure and strategic industries — while CPP's chief executive repeated that the Fund is 'not required to invest in Canada'.
4. **The contribution side.** Bill C-30 cuts the base contribution rate from 2027; the Chief Actuary finds the plan sustainable at legislated rates. Lower inflows change how much new capital the Fund has to place each year.
5. **Who gets the letters.** The 2025–2026 US Senate letters on utility and data-centre ownership went to BlackRock, KKR, Brookfield and Blackstone; CPP — co-owner of ALLETE, AirTrunk and atNorth — was not a recipient. Low profile is part of the model.

**Self-check:** a change to CPP's investment mandate needs more than an Act of Parliament. What else? *(Agreement of two-thirds of the provinces representing two-thirds of the population — the amendment threshold in the CPP legislation.)*

## Where it fails — the risks in one list

- Headline percentages move between signing and completion (atNorth 60% → c. 51%); a dossier that trusts the signing release overstates control.
- Sempra Infrastructure Partners is signed but not closed; a termination after the long-stop date would reverse the ~13% position.
- A legislated domestic-investment mandate would redirect capital away from the US platforms this corpus sells to.
- Regulated utilities can have returns cut and plans disallowed; ALLETE's 2027 rate case is the first test.
- CPP's control positions sit beside partners (GIP, Equinix, Blackstone) whose consent is needed for large decisions.
