# DG Matrix — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** DG Matrix (private; Morrisville, North Carolina; multi-port solid-state transformers — the six-port low-voltage Interport Flex shipping today, the Cell-LV sidecar for GPU pulse loads, and a medium-voltage Cell-MV skid on the roadmap) · **Written:** 2026-09-19 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM physics, no electrical-engineering background.

**What this teaches.** The solid-state transformer as a *power router*: how one converter can expose several galvanically isolated AC and DC ports on one magnetic core and steer power between them under software control; why a unit that connects at 480 V is a different product from one that connects at 34.5 kV even when both are called SSTs; what a GPU pulse load is, how large and how fast, and why a capacitive sidecar next to the rack is the physical answer; how to read a converter datasheet against a marketing page; what 'designed for compliance to UL 1741' means, what UL 2877 is for, and what a listing costs in time and money; and how a behind-the-meter block lets a campus run more load than its interconnection allows.

**Why this gap.** The Heron Power guide teaches the SST as a machine — the transformer equation, the cascaded-cell architecture, the SST-versus-TRU choice at the hall edge — and the Amperesand guide teaches silicon carbide, five-nines availability, megawatt charging and the bidirectional storage case. Neither teaches the *topology* question a multi-port vendor raises (ports versus windings), the *input-voltage* question that separates a low-voltage router from a medium-voltage transformer replacement, the *pulse-load* problem the sidecar exists for, or the *certification* path every startup in the tier is walking. Those are what DG Matrix's record uniquely teaches.

**Its place in the power-conversion set.** The third guide at the hall edge. Heron owns the stage-by-stage physics and the DC fault ledger; Amperesand owns silicon carbide, the platform argument, availability and charging; this guide owns the multi-port topology, low- versus medium-voltage input, pulse loads, datasheet literacy, certification and the behind-the-meter block. Read the Heron guide's Modules 2–4 first if the cascaded-cell architecture is new, and the Amperesand guide's Module 2 if silicon carbide is.

## Module 1 — Ports, not windings
A 60 Hz transformer has two windings and one fixed ratio. Inside every SST cell sits a dual-active bridge: two full-bridge switch stages facing each other across a small medium-frequency transformer, with power flowing from the bridge that leads in switching phase to the one that lags. Put a third, fourth or sixth winding on the same core, each with its own bridge, and you have a multi-active bridge — all windings see the same flux, so their voltages track automatically, and shifting any bridge's phase steers power into or out of its winding. Each port is galvanically isolated from every other. Teach why a port's *function* is the stage populated in front of its bridge — a rectifier/inverter stage makes an AC port, a bare DC bridge a DC port — which is why a datasheet can offer 'three AC ports, three DC ports' as one configuration and 'field-reconfigurable' as a feature, and why a DC port's 800 V can be a set-point 'reprogrammed' toward 1,500 V within the switches' rating. Close with the cost: leakage inductances fixed by winding geometry limit how much each port can move, per-path efficiency is a little lower than a single-path design, and the control problem is harder. The reward is what iron cannot do — blend a discharging battery, a producing solar array and the grid into one bus while serving AC and DC loads at once.

## Module 2 — Power routing arithmetic: what the box replaces
Take the company's own example — 600 kW of solar plus 600 kW of batteries serving twelve 100 kW racks with the grid as third source — and count the equipment. Conventionally: switchgear and a rectifier for the grid feed, a solar inverter whose output is then rectified back to DC, a battery PCS with its own transformer, a UPS with its own rectifier-battery-inverter, a DC distribution panel, and a separate feed for any AC load. With a multi-port unit: one AC port for the grid, one DC port each for solar and battery (bidirectional by construction — see the Amperesand guide's storage module), one or more DC ports at 800 V for the racks, an AC port for the AC load, and the battery port holding the bus through a grid dip in under a millisecond, which is the 'built-in UPS' claim in physical terms. Teach the learner to count conversions removed (each was two to three percent of losses) and to notice what is *not* removed: the rack power shelf that still converts 800 V to the rack bus, and the switchgear the datasheet footnote excludes from the footprint.

## Module 3 — 480 V in versus 34.5 kV in
Utilities deliver at medium voltage, 12–34.5 kV in the United States. A conventional hall steps it down with a big line-frequency transformer to 480 V, then switchgear, UPS and distribution. Heron's and Amperesand's SSTs connect at 34.5 kV: they *are* the transformer, rectifier and UPS in one enclosure. DG Matrix's shipping units connect at 400–480 V AC: they replace everything downstream of the transformer and leave the transformer standing. Work the consequences: the medium-voltage transformer is the item with the two-to-three-year lead time that the whole 'speed to power' argument concerns, so a 480 V-input unit shortens the electrical room, not the transformer queue, unless the site already has transformer capacity or a source that arrives at low voltage; and certification changes — UL 1741 with IEEE 1547 for a low-voltage converter, UL 2877 for anything above 1,000 V. Then read the vendor: SemiAnalysis calls DG Matrix the 'LV-input SST in place of the conventional rectifier-plus-PSU stack'; the company's medium-voltage skid is on its own roadmap for 2028 with no datasheet. The first question to any 'SST' vendor is the input voltage.

## Module 4 — Pulse loads: why the rack needs a buffer, and how big
A CPU hall's load is flat; a GPU training cluster's is not. Thousands of accelerators compute, wait for one another to exchange results, and compute again in lockstep, so the cluster's draw steps between a low and a high level several times a second — hundreds of kilowatts at the rack, tens or hundreds of megawatts at the campus. A transformer and the grid behind it see a flicker source; battery chemistry cannot follow microseconds; a UPS is built to bridge an outage, not to follow a waveform. Work the arithmetic: energy is power times time, so a 500 kW step lasting 20 ms is 10 kJ — little energy, but it must arrive in 20 ms, which is a large power. Capacitors and supercapacitors store little and release it instantly; batteries store much and ramp in milliseconds to seconds. Hence the architecture: a capacitive stage at the rack for the microseconds, fast storage in the hall for the seconds, the grid seeing only the average. That is what a sidecar is: DG Matrix's Cell-LV is a three-port unit next to the IT load with 'a dedicated capacitive-module input for AI pulse loading', ±400 or 800 V DC out, 'sub-millisecond surge response', rated 1.6 MW 'with up to 2x surge' — buffer vocabulary, not transformer vocabulary. Teach the three questions for any sidecar: how many joules, how fast, and does it also isolate the rack from a fault on the 800 V bus.

## Module 5 — Reading a converter datasheet against the marketing page
Two one-page specification sheets exist for the shipping product; every row is a question. 'Interport Flex 400 kVA' is apparent power — kVA times power factor is real power, so at the sheet's 0.85 PF about 340 kW — and it is not the same quantity as the whitepaper's '1.2 MW UPS power'. 'Efficiency (peak) 96–97%' is the best point on the curve near full load, not the daily average of a part-loaded unit with its cooling running, and it is two to three points below the 'up to 98.5%' platform claim — ask which product and which measurement the headline describes. 'DC port 300–850 VDC (800 nominal), 530 ADC' is a regulated window whose ceiling is about 450 kW; 'AC port 400–480 VAC, 520 A' is a low-voltage input with no medium-voltage port on the product. 'Current THD <5%' and 'PF 0.85 leading–lagging' are the IEEE 1547 quantities a utility will check. 'Designed for compliance to* UL 1741 — *certification information can be provided upon request' is design intent, not a listing. 'Liquid cooled (sealed), NEMA 3R, 100 dBA at 1 m, −25 to 40 °C' says outdoor, closed loop, loud, and derated above 40 °C. '4,000 kg; footprint does not include the AC and DC switchgear' says four tonnes on a pad plus the switchgear the unit does not replace. The exercise: sort every figure the company publishes into datasheet fact, platform claim and roadmap, and check the roadmap against the calendar.

## Module 6 — From 'pre-certification units' to a listed product
Two things get certified. Safety is evaluated by a Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory (UL, Intertek, CSA, TÜV) against a product standard — UL 1741 for converters up to 1,000 V, UL 2877 for power-conversion equipment above 1,000 V up to 38 kV. Grid behaviour — ride-through, anti-islanding, harmonic limits — is tested to IEEE 1547 and its test procedure, packaged for the utility by the UL 1741 SA/SB supplements. A product with both is 'listed' and 'conforms'; one with neither is a prototype however many are in the field. Teach the cost from mgrid's April 2026 walk-through: six to eighteen months and a mid-six-figure sum per variant, more than USD 5 million and two to three years for a line across voltages and geographies — and every size and port mix is a variant. That is why target dates matter: DG Matrix said 'UL certification targeted for the end of Q2 2026' while shipping 'pre-certification units', and the quarter passed without an announced listing; Amperesand names UL 2877 and UL 1741 as targets with no listing; no vendor had completed data-center SST certification as of May 2026. Close with what a buyer does with an unlisted unit — a per-installation NRTL field evaluation, a willing authority having jurisdiction, and a contract that says who pays if the listing never comes — and why a hyperscaler's design-build firm will not do that at scale: 'first commercial shipment' and 'first listed product' are different milestones, and the second opens volume.

## Module 7 — The behind-the-meter block: 35 MW of AI on a 30 MW interconnection
A developer's binding constraint is often the size of its utility interconnection and the years in the queue to enlarge it. Behind-the-meter generation and storage relieve it if something can blend them with the grid feed continuously — and a multi-port converter is that something. Work DG Matrix's own example: a 30 MW interconnection, a 35 MWh battery and a 10 MW fuel cell support 'approximately 35 MW of AI capacity' — the grid covers the average, the battery the peaks, the fuel cell the baseload the grid cannot, and the ports move power between them as the load moves. The same ports carry the grid functions the company's California filing lists — black start, islanding, grid-forming operation, Volt-VAR control, vehicle-to-grid — because a multi-port converter is a microgrid controller with power stages attached. Teach why the first commercial relationships are a microgrid integrator (PowerSecure), a behind-the-meter generation startup (Exowatt) and a battery maker (Ampace) rather than a hyperscaler: the unit's value rises with the number of sources and loads to blend, and it is lowest at a plain grid-fed hall. Then the policy backdrop — FERC's 2026 large-load orders and Texas SB 6 both ask new loads to be flexible or self-supplied — and the two halves of the time-to-power question: how fast can the site energise at all, and how much load can it run before the utility upgrade arrives.

## Module 8 — Where DG Matrix's products slot in, and the industry map
Three tiers of one platform. **Interport Flex (shipping):** six-port, low-voltage, 200 and 400 kVA, 96–97% peak efficiency on the datasheet, 'designed for compliance to UL 1741', paralleled into 'Interport 360' (eight units for 3.2 MVA in the published example) — the unit behind every deployment statement, a router for a low-voltage site rather than a transformer replacement. **Interport Cell-LV (next):** the pulse-load sidecar of Module 4, tied to the NVIDIA storage-response testing programme and the MGX rack, 'in development (Q3 2026)' with no datasheet. **Interport Cell-MV (2028+):** the three-port 12–34.5 kV skid that would compete with Heron Link and Amperesand's platform, described in the present tense by partners and dated '2028+' on the company's own roadmap slide; the CTO rules out 10 kV devices, so expect a cascaded-cell front end. Who buys now: microgrid integrators, behind-the-meter developers, fleet depots, and builders who need gray-space power blocks quickly. Who buys later: the campus owner through its design-build firm, who needs a listed medium-voltage single-path unit with field hours. Who competes: at the low-voltage block, conventional microgrid packages and grid-forming PCS vendors; at the sidecar, the rack-power and DC-storage layer on NVIDIA's rosters (Delta, LITEON, Megmeet, Vicor); at the hall edge in 2028, Heron Power, Amperesand, Delta, and the hedged incumbents — GE Vernova, Eaton via Resilient Power, ABB (which also holds a stake in DG Matrix), Hitachi Energy, Siemens, Schneider. End on the port-count economics: a single-path SST wins on efficiency at one operating point, lead time and field hours; a multi-port unit's value rises with the sources and loads on the site — which is why 'first company shipping a multi-port SST' is true of a 480 V unit and says nothing yet about the medium-voltage race. What a buyer asks in September 2026: which unit and at what input voltage; a listing, from which laboratory; a named energised site with unit counts; where the power stages are built; and what ABB's dual position as investor and roster incumbent means.

## Pacing
Eight modules of roughly 30 minutes. Modules 1 and 4 are the technical core — the multi-port topology and pulse-load physics — and are the ones to re-read before a conversation with a customer's electrical engineer. Module 5 can be read alone with the two datasheets open. Modules 6 and 7 are the commercial core — certification and the behind-the-meter block — and read best together with the Heron guide's Module 6 on the two 800 V DC waves.
