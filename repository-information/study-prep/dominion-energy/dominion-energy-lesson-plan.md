## Module 1 — The monopoly bargain: who the utility answers to, and the one equation

**The single idea:** a regulated utility is a legal monopoly that has traded pricing freedom for supervised, guaranteed cost recovery — and every purchase it makes is shaped by the arithmetic of that bargain and by which of four bodies has to approve it.

1. **The bargain.** A **vertically integrated utility** — one that owns generation, transmission and distribution and sells to the end customer — holds an exclusive franchise for a territory. In exchange, a state **public utility commission** (in Virginia the three-judge **State Corporation Commission**, the SCC) sets what it may charge, certifies what it may build, reviews its long-term plan and decides after the fact whether its spending was prudent. The utility proposes; the commission disposes. Nothing it buys at scale is its own decision alone, and a seller who forgets this sells to the wrong room.
2. **The equation.** The **revenue requirement** — what the utility may collect in a year — is operating costs + depreciation + taxes + (**rate base** × **allowed return**). Rate base is the depreciated value of the plant in service; the allowed return (usually quoted as an authorized return on equity, nine to ten percent in most recent US orders) is what the commission lets the utility earn on the equity share of that plant. The first three terms are pass-throughs. Only the last term is profit, which means a utility's earnings grow when its rate base grows — when it builds and owns things.
3. **Do the arithmetic once.** A $1 billion program of substations and lines, financed half with equity at a 9.5 percent authorized return, earns about $47 million in its first year ($500 million × 9.5 percent), declining slowly over a thirty-to-forty-year depreciation life. A $1 billion power-purchase agreement for the same capacity from somebody else's plant earns nothing: it is recovered as a cost, without a return. This is the reason a storage mandate in a regulated state produces company-owned batteries as well as contracted ones, the reason a utility argues for a self-build against a PPA in front of its commission, and the reason "we would rather own the plant" is not greed but incentive. The seller who knows which purchases enter rate base knows which ones the utility will fight for.
4. **How the requirement is divided — cost of service.** A **cost-of-service study** allocates each category of cost (generation, transmission, distribution, customer service) to the **rate classes** that cause it, using allocators such as each class's share of the system's **coincident peak** (its demand at the moment the whole system peaks), its energy, its number of meters. The output is the tariff book: residential, small and large commercial, industrial — and, since 2024, a very-large-load schedule. This dry study is where the data-center fight is actually fought, because a flat load that draws the same at 3 a.m. as at the system peak looks like no other customer, and where its costs land decides whether everyone else's bill rises (the **cross-subsidy** question).
5. **Two more words.** A **rider** — a *rate adjustment clause* in Virginia — recovers one program outside base rates, so a new plant or a transmission program earns from the day it enters service rather than waiting for the next rate case; regulators guard riders because they move cost onto customers without the full rate-case test. A **prudence review** is the after-the-fact check: a cost found imprudent when the decision was made is disallowed and falls on shareholders. That review, years later, is why a regulated buyer cares so much about a supplier's **bankability** and documentation — every purchase must still look reasonable to a hearing examiner in the next decade.
6. **Who decides what.** Five bodies, five clocks. The **state commission**: retail rates, the rate case, the plan, every certificate, riders, the prudence review — a **docket** with an order at the end, months to a year each. The **regional grid operator** (an **RTO**; PJM for Virginia): wholesale and capacity prices, resource accreditation, the generation queue, the regional transmission plan — auction cycles three years ahead, planning horizons of five to fifteen. **FERC**, the federal regulator: the transmission tariff, market rules, the interconnection procedures, co-located load. The **legislature**: mandates and fences — Virginia's Clean Economy Act, data-center tax treatment, cost-allocation directives. And the utility itself: what to file, what to self-build, the annual solicitation calendar. None of the five buys equipment; the utility's engineers do, but only what the other four have authorized.

**Self-check:** a utility's plan proposes a company-owned 500 MW battery instead of a PPA for the same capacity, and the consumer advocate objects. Explain both sides in two sentences each. (Utility: owned plant enters rate base and earns the allowed return for its life, and the commission keeps control over a prudently built asset; advocate: a PPA at a competitive price is a pass-through with no return on top, so customers pay less if the market price is below the utility's cost of capital plus construction risk. The commission's job is to decide which is true this time.)

## Module 2 — From plan to plant: the three documents that turn a need into a purchase

**The single idea:** an IRP, a CPCN and an RFP answer three different questions, are decided by different people, and open different doors — and none of them is a purchase order.

1. **The integrated resource plan (IRP).** The utility's commission-filed roadmap: a **load forecast** fifteen to twenty-five years out and a preferred portfolio of generation, storage, transmission and demand-side resources to meet it, modelled under scenarios and against the state's mandates. The commission reviews it and finds it reasonable or not — it approves *nothing*. Read the IRP for the ceiling of the market: its storage and **dispatchable** totals are the addressable demand for the next decade, and two assumptions buried in it decide the size of the storage slice — the cost curve it assumes for batteries, and the capacity credit (**ELCC**) it gives a four-hour battery toward the reserve requirement.
2. **The certificate (CPCN).** A **certificate of public convenience and necessity** is the commission's permission to build one specific plant or line and put it in rate base. The application proves need, prudence and the choice among alternatives — cost, site, schedule, the winning bids or the self-build case — in a contested **docket** with intervenors; nine to fifteen months later an order grants it, conditions it (a cost cap, a schedule) or denies it. This is the document that turns a plan into a purchase. The vendor of the chosen technology rarely appears in it by name, but the cost, performance and schedule evidence the utility files is the vendor's.
3. **The solicitation (RFP).** A **request for proposals** — storage-specific, solar, or all-source — is how the utility finds who will supply the capacity the plan calls for, utility-owned or contracted through a **PPA**. Bids come in, an evaluator (often an **independent engineer**) scores them, the utility takes the results to the commission for approval, and the awards become certificates and contracts. For an equipment vendor the RFP has two lanes and two buyers: the *developer* bidding a project chooses its OEM at bid stage, when the bill of materials locks; the *utility's own self-build bids* run through its **EPC** and approved-vendor list. Arriving after the bid date is arriving after the decision.
4. **The sequence, and where it loops.** Plan filed → order on the plan → solicitation → certificate application → certificate → contracts and construction → commissioning and **energization** → rider or rate-case recovery → prudence review. Every arrow can loop back: a forecast revision reopens the plan, a rejected certificate restarts the solicitation, a rate case can reprice everything. Draw it once with the clocks on it — plan review six to twelve months, solicitation to approval about a year, certificate nine to fifteen months, award to purchase order twelve to thirty-six months more — and the shape of a regulated sale is visible: the two steps a seller can see are the short ones.
5. **Where leverage sits.** Highest between the order on the plan and the solicitation's bid date — the plan says what is wanted, nobody has yet chosen how, and the bidding developer is pricing its bill of materials. Lowest after the certificate, when the buyer is a contracting officer executing an approved plan and the hardware was chosen months earlier. The most common seller's error in this market follows directly: treating plan megawatts as a pipeline. They are the ceiling. The orders are the certificates and the awards, on a one-to-three-year fuse, and in most of them the developer, not the utility, picks the battery.

**Self-check:** a utility's IRP shows 1,500 MW of new storage by 2032; its most recent solicitation awarded 300 MW, of which 100 MW is company-owned and 200 MW is under PPAs with two developers. Name the buyer for each of the three tranches. (The 100 MW: the utility's EPC and approved-vendor list, on the self-build lane. The 200 MW: the two developers, who chose their OEMs when they bid. The remaining 1,200 MW: nobody yet — it is the ceiling, and the next solicitation's bid date is the seller's deadline.)

## Module 3 — The rate case, the docket and the large-load tariff

**The single idea:** a data center's terms of service are written in the same proceeding that sets everyone else's rates — and every term of the tariff that comes out of it is a translation of one fear, that plant gets built for a load that never fully arrives.

1. **The rate case.** The proceeding that resets base rates. The utility files a test year — its costs, its rate base, the return it asks for; intervenors (the consumer counsel, industrial customers, environmental groups, the data-center industry itself, the commission's own staff) test every number through discovery, testimony, rebuttal and a hearing; the order sets the **revenue requirement**, the **allowed return**, the depreciation schedules and the tariffs by rate class, each effective on a stated date. Virginia runs Dominion Energy Virginia's as a **biennial review** on a fixed two-year cycle, so the calendar is known years ahead.
2. **A new class is born inside it.** When the commission concludes that very large loads cost the system something existing tariffs do not capture — a dedicated substation, a share of new generation, a risk that the load never arrives — it can create a **large-load tariff** class inside the rate order, with a threshold in megawatts, a contract term, a **minimum demand charge**, collateral and exit provisions. Dominion's GS-5 class came out of its biennial review this way: the data-center terms were decided in the same order that set the residential rate.
3. **Reading the docket.** Every proceeding has a case number, and under it sit the application, the exhibits, every witness's testimony, the discovery responses, the hearing examiner's report and the final order — all public. A press release says what the utility asked for; the docket records what it was granted, what it conceded, what staff recommended and what the commissioners wrote. Two dates matter on every order — the order date and the effective date — and the gap between them (fourteen months, in the GS-5 case) is where a developer decides whether to sign before or after the new terms apply. That decision, multiplied across a cluster, moves a utility's contracted pipeline from one quarter to the next.
4. **Anatomy of a large-load tariff.** Read the terms as a list of the ways commissions have been burned. *Threshold* (25 MW in Virginia and AEP Ohio; 100 MW in Georgia's rules): keeps hospital-sized customers off the class. *Contract term* (twelve to fifteen years): guards against **stranded cost** — plant built for a customer that leaves after the hype cycle; the developer's lease with its tenant has to be at least as long. *Minimum demand* (85 percent of transmission-and-distribution demand is the norm; a separate, often lower, generation minimum): converts paper megawatts into demand the utility can finance against — the utility's own **take-or-pay**. *Ramp*: the first years at a lower share, so a real project is not punished for the time it takes to fill a building. *Collateral*: a deposit, a letter of credit or a parent guarantee — a shell company cannot post it, a hyperscaler can, which is one reason the tenant's name decides the interconnection. *Exit fee*: the same fear priced for the case where the customer does leave. *Flexibility*: whether accepting **curtailment** or **demand response** buys an earlier connection — in Texas **SB 6** makes it a condition of connecting at all, and it is the socket for on-site storage.
5. **The arithmetic, by hand.** A developer contracts 300 MW of wires demand for a campus it will fill over four years, on a class with an 85 percent transmission-and-distribution minimum and a 60 percent generation minimum. Assume — for the arithmetic only, these are not any utility's rates — $12 per kilowatt-month on the wires and $8 on generation. In year one, at 30 MW of actual draw, the campus is billed for 255 MW of wires demand (85 percent of 300 = 255,000 kW × $12 ≈ $3.1 million a month) and 180 MW of generation demand (60 percent of 300 = 180,000 kW × $8 ≈ $1.4 million a month): roughly $54 million a year for a building that is one-tenth full, before it has paid for a kilowatt-hour. The developer's choices: contract in phases and accept that each phase joins the queue on its own date, or sign the full 300 MW to hold the queue position and carry the minimum until the tenants arrive. Neither is free, and the choice is the most important number in a data-center pro forma after the lease rate.
6. **What the tariff does for the utility.** It turns 300 MW of intent into 255 MW of bankable revenue — demand the utility can take to a certificate docket and finance a substation against. The minimum charge is not a penalty; it is the regulated version of the contract structure the rest of this ecosystem runs on, and it is the instrument that made AEP Ohio's 30 GW of requests collapse to a few gigawatts of signed load once real money was required — the proof that a tariff is a filter as much as a price.

**Self-check:** a commission order creating a large-load class is dated November and effective fourteen months later. What does a developer with a 200 MW request already in the queue do in the gap, and why? (It races to sign a connection agreement under the old terms if the old terms are better for its phasing — no minimum, or a shorter term — or waits if the new class buys it something, such as a ramp. Either way the utility's contracted pipeline jumps in the quarters before the effective date, and a seller reading the pipeline number needs to know which.)

## Module 4 — Interconnecting a load, and the layer above the utility

**The single idea:** a load's connection date is the output of a study that models a grid that keeps changing, and the two largest numbers on the resulting bill — the capacity price and the big lines — are set one level above the utility, at PJM.

1. **Generators queue; loads connect.** A generator joins the RTO's **interconnection queue** under rules FERC and the grid operator set; a large load connects under the utility's own process, and the two are constantly confused. For a data center: a request stating site and megawatts → a feasibility screen → a **system impact study** (what the load does to the network; which lines, **transformers** and **substations** it overloads) → a facilities study (what the upgrades cost) → a construction agreement → substation design and build → **energization**, the date every schedule upstream is measured against.
2. **Why the date moves.** The study models the grid as it will be on the connection date, and the grid keeps changing: every other request in the corridor, every retirement, every transmission line that slips changes the answer. A study valid in March is stale in September; a utility with a hundred requests in one county cannot study them serially — hence batch and cluster studies, re-runs, and pauses while a corridor's transmission is rebuilt. This is row one of the corpus's failure map (`CLASSROOM-CURRICULUM-PLAN.md` §5), and it is not a failure of competence: the requester funds upgrades it does not control, on a network it shares with everyone.
3. **Who pays for the upgrade — cost allocation.** A dedicated substation is usually the customer's cost, as a *contribution in aid of construction*, often refundable if others later use it; a network upgrade that strengthens the whole system is socialized into rate base; and the fight over which is which is the **cross-subsidy** fight. Both principles are defensible. *Cost-causation* protects other classes and filters the queue but struggles to attribute shared assets and pushes development behind the meter or to the next utility over. *Socialization* gets corridor lines built and treats transmission as the network good it is, but raises bills for load residents do not use and plans for paper megawatts. Most tariffs are a hybrid: dedicated facilities on the customer, network on the system, and a minimum charge that makes the customer fund its share through time.
4. **PJM: the market above the utility.** A vertically integrated utility inside an **RTO** keeps its wires and plants, but PJM runs the wholesale energy market, the generation queue, the **capacity market** and the regional transmission plan (**RTEP**). The capacity market pays a resource *for existing*: in the Base Residual Auction PJM buys, three years ahead, enough accredited capacity to meet each zone's forecast peak plus a reserve, and the clearing price in dollars per megawatt-day is what every load in the zone pays through its utility. Data-center growth, retirements and a slow queue moved the region from surplus to scarcity, the price hit the administrative cap in consecutive auctions, and the cost flowed through regulated utilities into residential bills — which is how a wholesale auction became state politics.
5. **Accreditation sizes the storage market.** **ELCC** — effective load carrying capability — is the share of a resource's nameplate PJM counts toward the peak: a gas plant near its nameplate, a solar farm a fraction, a four-hour battery in between and declining as more storage joins. A utility that needs 1,000 accredited megawatts from batteries must build more than 1,000 nameplate megawatts, and a plan that assumes a generous credit buys less storage than one that assumes a strict one. The IRP's accreditation assumptions are, quietly, a sizing decision on the whole storage slice.
6. **The regional plan is where the lines come from.** PJM's planners identify reliability violations from the load forecast, open competitive windows and assign projects — 500 kV loops, an **HVDC** link, a 765 kV backbone — to transmission owners and developers, with a cost-allocation rule deciding what each zone pays. A multi-billion-dollar award is a decade of construction and a decade of rate-base growth, decided in a planning window rather than in any customer's request; for a data-center corridor it is the answer to the question the load study keeps asking — when will the wires exist? Two more PJM facts sit under the story: the queue reforms (batch studies replacing serial ones) decide how fast relief arrives, and the rules on co-located load — a data center behind an existing plant's meter — are decided at FERC on PJM's filings, not by any state.
7. **Why the clock is years.** Put the durations on one scale: a load study one to two years; a rate case or a certificate docket about a year; a battery site eighteen months from award; a gas peaker three years and a combined-cycle plant four or five from certificate; a 500 kV line five to eight years from planning window to energization; offshore wind seven to ten from federal permit; a small modular reactor a decade from early site work. The two steps a seller can see are the shortest, and everything the utility must build to serve the load takes longer than the data center that needs it — which is the origin of the behind-the-meter bridge-power market, and the reason it ends when the wires arrive.

**Self-check:** a plan revision cuts the four-hour battery's ELCC from 60 to 45 percent and moves a 500 kV line's in-service date out two years. Name the effect of each on a storage seller. (The ELCC cut raises the nameplate storage the plan must buy for the same accredited need — more market. The line slip pushes the corridor's loads toward behind-the-meter bridge power and pauses connections — a different buyer, on a different clock, for two more years.)

## Module 5 — Dominion against the chain: the programs, the ladder, who buys, who competes

**The single idea:** every instrument in Modules 1–4 is running at Dominion Energy Virginia at once, at the largest scale in the country, and the dossier's figures can be read as a worked example of each — including the one instrument the earlier modules could not teach, a change of owner.

1. **The scale that makes it the worked example.** The DOM Zone's all-time peak is 24,678 MW (January 23, 2025). The company's contracted data-center capacity was about 53.8 GW in July 2026 — more than twice the peak — of which 12.0 GW sits under electric service agreements that carry a revenue requirement whether the customer takes service or not, 9.4 GW under construction authorizations with full walk-away reimbursement, and 32.4 GW at the substation-engineering stage. The February 2026 SCC filing counted roughly 70,000 MW of pending requests, 45,000 MW of them without a connection date. Against all of that, connections ran at 11 data centers (744 MW) in 2025 and 13 are expected in 2026. Read the three numbers together — requests, contracted, connected — and you have Module 2's lesson (plan megawatts are a ceiling) in one utility's own disclosure.
2. **The rate base and the plan.** Dominion Energy Virginia's utility rate base was $48.2 billion in 2025 with a planned compound growth of 11.9 percent a year through 2030; the consolidated five-year plan is $64.7 billion, 25 percent transmission, 20 percent distribution, 18 percent new gas generation, 13 percent solar and storage, and 64 percent rider-eligible. Apply Module 1's arithmetic: an authorized return of 9.8 percent on roughly half of $48 billion is the earnings engine, and the 64 percent rider share is why the plan earns as it is built rather than at the next rate case. The 2025 biennial review (SCC Case PUR-2025-00058, order November 25, 2025) set that return, cut the requested base-rate increases from $822 million and $345 million to $565.7 million and $209.9 million, and — in the same order — created the GS-5 class.
3. **The tariff, with Dominion's numbers.** GS-5: 25 MW or more of measured or contracted demand on a contiguous site with a load factor of at least 75 percent; minimums of 85 percent of contracted capacity for transmission and distribution and 60 percent for generation, in place as long as service is provided and applying to existing customers too (those who began service before 2016 exempt); for new electric service agreements from January 1, 2027, a 14-year term made of a four-year ramp plus ten years, exit fees equal to the remaining minimum obligations, cash deposits for equipment and enhanced collateral — up to 60 percent of minimum charges over the term for customers without sufficient credit, per the SCC's fact sheet. Redo Module 3's arithmetic with the real percentages: a 300 MW campus at one-tenth occupancy is billed for 255 MW of wires demand and 180 MW of generation demand. The fourteen months between the order (November 2025) and the effective date (January 2027) are Module 3's gap, and the ESA tranche's growth from 10.2 GW to 12.0 GW between December 2025 and July 2026 is what to watch inside it.
4. **The certificate and the plan, side by side.** The Chesterfield Energy Reliability Center — four simple-cycle turbines of up to 250 MW, 944 MW, about $1.5 billion, operations in 2029 — was filed March 3, 2025 and certified November 25, 2025: nine months, Module 2's fastest link. Then the order was suspended on reconsideration, reinstated in February 2026 and appealed to the Supreme Court of Virginia in May 2026: the failure map's "certificate that is denied, delayed or conditioned" in real time. Above it sits the 2025 IRP Update's preferred plan through 2045 — 17,534 MW of solar, 3,460 MW of wind, 2,000 MW of storage, six SMRs (1,944 MW), 8,510 MW of gas — and beside it an April 2026 statute requiring 4.0 GW of short-duration storage by 2030. The plan and the law disagree, and the next comprehensive IRP is where they will be reconciled; that reconciliation is the largest single storage signal in the mid-Atlantic.
5. **The solicitation, two lanes.** The company issues a PPA RFP each autumn (the 2026 edition on October 1, 2026, for Virginia and North Carolina) and an Acquisition RFP for assets it will own; the October 2025 clean-energy petition sought 845 MW of solar, 155 MW of owned storage and 439 MW of solar PPAs, and the SCC approved 532 MW of solar and 80 MW of storage for about $1.5 billion in April 2026. The 10-K's $2.0 billion of storage capital over 2026–2030 is 3 percent of the plan. Module 2's two lanes with the volumes attached: the PPA lane, where the developer bidding the RFP chooses the battery, is the larger one; the self-build lane runs through the company's EPC and no integrator or cell maker is named on any of its pages. A third lane opened on July 1, 2026 — a Dispatchable Generation RFP with bids due December 18, 2026.
6. **The study and the wires, dated.** The 2022 eastern-Loudoun pause reduced allocations through January 2026. The Loudoun Reliability Loop — Mars–Wishing Star (summer 2026), Aspen–Golden (June 2028, affirmed by the Supreme Court of Virginia in February 2026), Golden–Mars (summer 2028) — is the fix; Morrisville–Wishing Star (36.5 miles, 2030) and Line 514 (2031) follow; PJM's February 2026 plan added the Heritage–Mosby ±525 kV HVDC link, 185 miles underground, 3,000 MW, about $3.8 billion, in service June 2032, and the 765 kV backbone with AEP and FirstEnergy has no firm date. Put those on Module 4's scale and the corridor's connection dates explain themselves — and so does the existence of the behind-the-meter bridge market that the Caterpillar and Piller guides teach from the equipment side.
7. **The regional layer, priced.** PJM's capacity auctions cleared at the cap two years running — $329.17/MW-day for 2026/2027 and $333.44 for 2027/2028, the latter with a 14.4 percent reserve margin against a 20 percent target and forecast load up 5,249.9 MW "largely due to additional Large Loads". The company's IRP calls the first the second-highest price ever for its zone; its certificate filing cites it; and the SCC's July 31, 2026 order requiring direct assignment of defined transmission facilities to data centers is the cost-allocation answer to the same pressure. PJM's 2026 forecast, meanwhile, cut the zone's ten-year growth from 6.3 to 5.4 percent a year by vetting large loads as firm or non-firm — the load forecast being argued about in Module 4, point 2.
8. **The instrument the earlier modules could not teach: a change of owner.** On May 15, 2026 Dominion agreed to be acquired by NextEra Energy — 0.8138 NextEra shares plus a pro rata share of $360 million in cash per share, NextEra 74.5 percent and Dominion 25.5 percent, a combined $138 billion of rate base — with closing expected in the second half of 2027 after approvals from the SCC (hearing November 17, 2026), the Carolinas' commissions, FERC and the NRC, and $2.25 billion of bill credits pledged. Dominion Energy Virginia keeps its president; the Virginia docket calendar does not change; capital allocation and supplier qualification move to the acquirer. For a seller: the regulatory clock in Modules 2–4 is unaffected, and the buyer's platform is.
9. **Who buys, who competes, why the transition matters.** Who buys from Dominion: every large load on its system, on its terms. Who Dominion buys from: developers bidding its PPA RFP, project sellers in its Acquisition RFP, EPCs and turbine and turbine-generator OEMs for its self-build (turbines "secured" for 6.5 GW of gas), Siemens Gamesa for CVOW's 176 turbines, transmission contractors for the loop and the HVDC link. Who competes with Dominion for the load's dollars: the behind-the-meter power partners and their gen-set, turbine, fuel-cell and rotary-UPS suppliers that exist because the wires arrive after the load — and, after 2027, the acquirer's own merchant development arm. Why the transition matters economically: a utility that can turn 53.8 GW of intent into a decade of rate-base growth at an authorized return is the most valuable franchise in the AI buildout, and the whole regulated-procurement chain — tariff, certificate, solicitation, study, auction — is the machinery by which that value is either captured by ratepayers, shareholders or the data centers themselves.

## Whole-plan self-test

1. A utility's IRP shows 2,000 MW of storage through 2045 and the legislature mandates 4,000 MW by 2030. Who reconciles them, in which document, and what does the answer do to a seller's addressable market? (The utility, in its next comprehensive IRP filed with the commission; a higher storage total raises the ceiling, and whether it comes as owned plant or PPAs decides which lane the volume lands in.)
2. Why does a regulated utility prefer a $1 billion owned battery to a $1 billion PPA, and what would a consumer advocate say back? (Rate base earns the allowed return for the asset's life; a PPA is a pass-through. The advocate says the PPA is cheaper for customers if the market price beats the utility's cost of capital plus construction risk.)
3. A campus signs 300 MW under a class with an 85 percent transmission-and-distribution minimum and a 60 percent generation minimum. At 30 MW of draw, what is billed? (255 MW of wires demand and 180 MW of generation demand — the utility's take-or-pay.)
4. Order date November 2025, effective date January 2027. What happens to the utility's contracted pipeline in between, and why? (Customers race to sign under existing terms if those suit their phasing; the firm tranche jumps before the effective date — the ESA figure is the tell.)
5. A study's connection date moves eighteen months. List three causes that are nobody's fault. (A neighbouring request re-priced the corridor; a transmission line slipped; a retirement changed the flows — the study models a grid that keeps changing.)
6. PJM's auction clears at the cap. Trace the dollar to a residential bill. (Capacity price per MW-day → the utility's purchased-power and capacity costs → recovered through riders and base rates → the residential class's share by the cost-of-service allocators.)
