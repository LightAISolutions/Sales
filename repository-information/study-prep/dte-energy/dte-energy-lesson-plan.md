## Module 1 — The customer pays for the battery

**The single idea:** under DTE's energy storage agreement the data-center customer pays the full cost of utility-owned batteries matched one-for-one to its contracted load, while the utility owns them in rate base — a purchase triggered by a signature rather than by a plan.

1. **The agreement.** Two special contracts with Oracle's subsidiary, approved December 18, 2025: a 19-year supply agreement at 80 percent minimum billing demand, and a 15-year **energy storage agreement** under which DTE builds 1,383 MW of company-owned storage — one megawatt per contracted megawatt — that the customer pays for over fifteen years while receiving the wholesale market revenues. The commission's words: the customer "must foot the bill to develop [storage] to match the data center's contracted demand", and DTE bears any cost it cannot recover from the customer.
2. **Why it is different from a PPA or a toll.** In a PPA or tolling agreement a developer owns the battery and the utility pays for output or availability. Here the roles are reversed: the utility owns, the customer pays. DTE gets a rate-base asset earning its allowed return; the customer gets accredited capacity for its load and a hedge on its energy; other ratepayers, on the order's conditions, get none of the bill.
3. **What follows.** Purchase orders arrive fast — 332 MW of named projects in March 2026, 660 MW in April, 300 MW of third-party tolls in July, a 1.5 GW / 6 GWh supply agreement with one manufacturer in May. The load is grid-served ("existing resources augmented by a new battery storage investment, financed entirely by the project"), so the AI campus is a grid customer with a battery portfolio behind it, not a self-generator. And the argument moves from "should the utility build this?" to "who bears the risk if the campus never fills?" — which is the question now at the Michigan Court of Appeals.
4. **The tail.** Fifteen years is shorter than a battery's rate-base life, so the customer pays faster than the asset depreciates; termination payments of up to ten years of minimum charges and collateral that steps up on a downgrade cover the case where the campus stops paying; the 2026 rate case carries an "ESA rate base reimbursement" line so the customer's revenue reduces what other ratepayers fund.

**Self-check:** a developer wins a 15-year tolling agreement with DTE under the Oracle energy storage agreement. Who pays for the developer's battery? (Oracle, through the utility — the toll counts against the customer-funded obligation.)

## Module 2 — Special contract or tariff; ex parte or contested

**The single idea:** the procedure a regulator uses to approve a customer decides how fast the customer gets served and how long the approval can be attacked.

1. **Tariff versus special contract.** A tariff is a published rate any qualifying customer can take; its terms are decided for a class in a case with intervenors. A special contract is a negotiated deal for one customer that the commission approves on its own, with conditions written for that deal. DTE used special contracts for its first two data centers and, on the commission's order, filed a general large-load revision of its Rate D11 within 90 days (Case U-22061, still pending on EEI's September 2026 list). The standard D11 gives an ordinary large customer a five-year term and 50–60 percent minimum billing; the data-center contracts carry 80 percent for 19–20 years.
2. **Ex parte.** Approval on the filing alone, no hearing. Oracle's contracts: filed October 31, approved December 18, 2025, 3–0, with six conditions. Fast for a customer that had paid a $40 million deposit and ordered $2 billion of equipment; thin as a record. The Attorney General, unheard, petitioned for rehearing, moved to reopen, was denied standing in March 2026 and appealed; briefs were filed in August. The batteries are being built while the approval that funds them is under review.
3. **Contested.** For Google, DTE chose a contested case — testimony, cross-examination, a record. The Attorney General used it to argue for a 90 percent minimum bill and a full-term exit fee. Slower: the requested decision date (September 10, 2026) passed with no order reported. But an order that survives a hearing survives appeal.
4. **Conditions as tariff by other means.** The six conditions on the Oracle order — cost tracking so the customer pays everything, an amended renewable plan, with-and-without-load resource scenarios, emergency procedures that curtail the data center first, six cost-of-service studies in the next rate case, a general tariff within 90 days — are what a commission writes when it approves a deal it did not design. Each is a docket a seller can read.

**Self-check:** why might the second customer's terms become the tariff's floor rather than the first's? (The contested case produces a tested record — the 80-versus-90 percent argument — that the general tariff must answer; the ex parte order produced only conditions.)

## Module 3 — MISO accreditation, and the free 300 MW

**The single idea:** in a self-supplied MISO utility the value of a battery is set by accreditation — how much of it counts at the hour of need — not by the auction price, and a new gigawatt of load raises the utility's obligation before the resources built for it count.

1. **The market.** MISO's capacity construct is thin: most load-serving entities self-supply, and the annual planning resource auction clears the residual. Each utility must demonstrate, for each season, enough accredited capacity — **zonal resource credits**, one per megawatt counted on at the hour of need — to cover its share of the reserve requirement.
2. **Accreditation.** A 100 MW four-hour battery does not earn 100 credits; it earns what MISO's method says it delivers when the system is short. From planning year 2028/29 the method is "direct loss of load": credit based on how much the resource helps in the actual shortfall hours. As more four-hour batteries shift the evening shortfall later, each additional one helps less — which is why DTE's 2026 plan reaches for 1.7 GW of long-duration storage (compressed air, iron-air) from 2037.
3. **The free credits.** The Google contract has Google supplying DTE with about 300 MW of Zone 7 resource credits at no cost. A new gigawatt of load raises DTE's obligation immediately; the batteries and renewables built for it are accredited later. The customer bridging the gap with credits it holds is the same idea as Duke's letter agreements or WEC's bespoke resources — the load brings capacity with it — in MISO's currency. The Oracle order requires DTE's next capacity demonstration to show the data center's effect for the same reason.
4. **The price.** Zone 7's 2026/27 auction cleared summer at $424/MW-day, down from $667 the year before, after a ten-to-twenty-fold jump the year before that. Volatile enough that a self-supplied utility plans to physical accreditation, not price. Do the reading: a battery seller pricing to the auction is pricing to the wrong number.

**Self-check:** DTE buys more batteries than ever in a year when the capacity price falls 36 percent. Why is that not a contradiction? (A contract obliges the customer-funded storage, and the utility plans to accreditation and its own demonstration; the auction is a residual signal.)

## Module 4 — Four ways to buy a battery, and the supplier that failed

**The single idea:** a utility can buy the same megawatt through four channels, the buyer of the hardware differs in each, and a regulated self-build makes a supplier's failure a docket.

1. **Self-build with a supply agreement.** Trenton Channel (220 MW / 880 MWh); Fish Creek, Cold Creek, Pine River; Monroe I; Fermi. The utility signs the battery supply agreement itself, files it, and the commission approves it — so the OEM sells to the buyer directly and the price can be second-guessed.
2. **Build-transfer.** Isabella (190 MW) on the wind site Apex built and sold to DTE; Meridian (295 MW). The developer carries interconnection, permitting and construction to commercial operation and hands over an operating asset; the developer chose the OEM at bid stage.
3. **Tolling with a merchant developer.** Big Mitten (450 MW, 20 years); Coldwater River and Tuscola II; KCE MI 2 (100 MW) and Redstart (200 MW) at a blended $15.30 per kilowatt-month, sourced by the 2025 RFP. No capital on the utility's balance sheet; the utility earns no return; the developer chose the OEM. The RFP (450 MW in 2025, 480 MW in 2026) is the door a developer can actually walk through.
4. **A framework supply agreement.** LG Energy Solution Vertech: $1.6 billion, eight projects, 1.5 GW / 6 GWh, two-year delivery, cells from Holland, Michigan. One supplier for a whole tranche, with price, schedule and domestic-content compliance locked. Which eight projects it covers is not stated, and the March order redacts the supplier — the public record and the deal do not line up.
5. **The failure.** Powin was named Trenton Channel's supplier in July 2024, missed deliveries, was terminated on May 19, 2025 and went bankrupt on June 9. DTE filed a replacement agreement with LGES in November, the commission approved it in April 2026 at more than $30 million less, and the plant is due at the end of 2026. Because the supply agreement is part of the approved project, a supplier's failure is a regulatory event — a new docket, a new price, a prudence question. Bankability to a regulated buyer is a regulator's word as much as a lender's.
6. **Domestic content.** LG's Michigan-made cells make the agreement "domestic-content-compliant" under the tax-credit bonus and keep it clear of the rules that fence out certain foreign-owned suppliers; DTE's release counts 1,800 LG jobs. The commission's approval story was written in those terms without the word FEOC appearing.

**Self-check:** name the buyer of the hardware in each of the four channels. (Self-build: the utility; build-transfer: the developer at bid stage; tolling: the developer; framework: the utility, across projects.)

## Module 5 — Credit, the plan and the gas plants

**The single idea:** the contracts' protections are the answer to "what if the campus never fills?", and the integrated resource plan is where the utility asks for the gas it says each further gigawatt needs.

1. **The protections.** Minimum billing demand (80 percent for 19–20 years; the AG asked 90); termination payment (up to ten years of minimum charges); collateral with downgrade triggers (redacted amounts — S&P's July 9, 2026 cut of Oracle to BBB- produced "additional collateral requirements" and more postings); the cost-tracking condition (DTE bears any unrecovered cost); an excess-margin flow-back in the 2026 rate case; curtailment priority (the data center is shed first). Concentration is the exposure — two customers are about 40 percent of retail sales at full ramp — and the contract design, not the customer, is what protects other ratepayers.
2. **The plan.** Michigan's IRP is a contested case with a 360-day clock, and its instrument is the **certificate of necessity**: a finding inside the plan that a specific plant is needed and its cost may be recovered up to a cap. DTE's 2026 plan (filed September 24, 2026) asks for two — a ~1,400 MW combined cycle in Livingston County ($3.9 billion, 2031) and a ~700 MW combined cycle in Monroe County ($2.5 billion, 2032), carbon-capture-ready — with 15 GW of renewables, 4.5 GW of storage including 1.7 GW long-duration, a 177 MW Fermi 2 uprate by 2036 and the exit from coal in 2032. Base case: the two signed contracts; high case: the full pipeline.
3. **The arithmetic.** 4.5 GW of storage against 2.1 GW of new gas, where Dominion's plan runs 2 GW of storage against 8.5 GW of gas. A utility whose first data centers brought their own batteries plans on storage for the next ones too. The turbine down payments and MISO queue positions were in hand by February 2026 — as at Duke, the equipment decision precedes the certificate, and the OEM is not named.
4. **The politics.** The Attorney General has appealed the Oracle approval and testified for 90 percent in the Google case; at least 18 local governments have adopted or proposed data-center moratoriums; an August 2026 poll found 62 percent of likely voters opposed; both gubernatorial nominees call for moratoriums; the county executive vetoed the Van Buren tax break the week the Google order was due. A court reversal would not unbuild the batteries but could reopen who pays for them.

**Self-check:** a customer is downgraded one notch above junk. Which contract protections change and which do not? (Collateral steps up on the downgrade trigger; the minimum bill and termination payment are unchanged by a rating move.)
