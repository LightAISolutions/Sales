# Duke Energy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach what a multi-jurisdiction regulated utility is and how it buys — one company answering to five state commissions, two RTOs and two non-RTO regions; how a data-center pipeline is reported honestly and why a conservative ladder looks small; what a gas-turbine order book is and why turbine slots became the scarcest input in the industry; how reserve margins are set where there is no capacity auction; why utility-owned batteries go to retired coal sites; how a $103 billion plan is financed (rate base, CWIP, tax-credit sales, hybrid equity, selling a fifth of a utility); what 80-year reactor licenses and uprates do to the rest of the mix; and what a commission's refusal of a data-center-justified peaker teaches — starting from high-school STEM. Companion: the in-app guide (Profiler → Duke Energy → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The Dominion plan taught the regulated purchase from the inside — rate base and the revenue requirement, the IRP → CPCN → RFP chain, the rate case and the docket, the large-load tariff and its minimum-demand arithmetic, PJM's capacity auction. The AEP plan taught the transmission-first version of the same machine across three RTOs. This plan assumes both and adds the layer Duke exposes: what changes when the same company runs that machine in six states under different rules, and what the equipment supply chain — turbines, batteries, reactors — looks like from the buyer's chair at that scale.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min — the five-commission map is the vocabulary everything else uses), Module 2 in one sitting (~30 min — read the ladder and the turbine book together), Module 3 in one sitting (~35 min — capacity without an auction, and storage at the coal site), Module 4 in one sitting (~35 min — the financing levers, with the arithmetic done by hand), Module 5 last (~30 min — the tariff spectrum and the CPCN denial), then the flashcard and self-test passes in the app.

## Module 1 — One company, five commissions

**The single idea:** a multi-state utility is not one regulated purchase but a set of parallel ones, each on its own clock, statute and market structure — and a seller who does not know which one is deciding is selling to the wrong room.

1. **The map.** Six electric utilities: Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress (North and South Carolina; to be combined on January 1, 2027), Duke Energy Florida, Duke Energy Indiana, Duke Energy Ohio and Duke Energy Kentucky. Five commissions — the NCUC, the PSCSC, the Florida PSC, the IURC and the PUCO — plus Kentucky's. Three market structures: the Carolinas and Florida are **vertically integrated and outside any RTO** (the utility is its own balancing authority and the state sets the reserve margin); Indiana is in **MISO**; Ohio and Kentucky are in **PJM**, and Duke Energy Ohio is a **wires-only** company that owns no generation since Ohio restructured.
2. **Why it matters.** The same question — should a data center pay 85 percent of contracted demand? — recovers a *plant* in the Carolinas and only *wires* in Ohio, where the generation is bought through auctions and PJM's capacity price passes through a rider. A certificate approved in South Carolina (Anderson County, March 2026) does not move North Carolina; a battery in Florida is bought under a ten-year site plan and a rate settlement, not under a resource plan. Each state has its own resource document (the Carolinas Resource Plan, Florida's Ten-Year Site Plan, Indiana's IRP), its own rate case cycle and its own large-load tariff docket.
3. **The seller's discipline.** For any Duke purchase, name the state, the document and the commission before asking about the equipment. The corpus's other utility dossiers (Dominion, Southern, AEP, Entergy, Oncor, Xcel) each map to one or two commissions; Duke maps to five, and its investor deck is a consolidation of five stories.

**Self-check:** a battery at a retired coal plant in North Carolina and a battery beside a solar farm in Florida — which document authorises each, and which body approves it? (Carolinas Resource Plan and a CPCN before the NCUC; Florida's Ten-Year Site Plan and the 2024 rate settlement before the Florida PSC.)

## Module 2 — The ladder and the turbine book

**The single idea:** a data-center pipeline is only comparable rung by rung, and a gas-turbine order book is a bet placed years before the certificate that would justify it.

1. **The rungs.** Inquiry or study stage (a study fee, nothing binding) → late-stage or advanced (a **letter agreement**: refundable capital advances, minimum bills, termination fees) → signed **electric service agreement** (term, minimum billing demand, credit support, termination damages) → under construction → energised. Duke publishes only the last three: ~7.8 GW signed, ~5.2 GW under construction, and a ~15.4 GW "high-confidence, late-stage" figure that *includes* the signed rung. Dominion's 53.8 GW counts study-stage requests; AEP's 69 GW counts executed agreements across eleven states. Compare signed with signed.
2. **From ESA to capital in two steps.** Signing moves the load forecast (2.7 GW of ESAs in one quarter moved the Carolinas plan to its high-load case); the forecast moves the plan only when a commission certifies the plant. The $5–10 billion of Indiana and Florida capital named in August 2026 is not in the $103B plan because nothing there is certified yet.
3. **What a turbine is.** A heavy-duty gas turbine compresses air, burns fuel and spins a generator. Simple cycle wastes the exhaust — cheap, fast, inefficient, a peaker. Combined cycle raises steam from the exhaust for a second turbine — about sixty percent efficient, a baseload machine. Duke builds both: CC where it needs energy around the clock (Person County, Cayuga, Anderson), CT where it needs the peak (Marshall, Buck, Smith).
4. **Why the slot is the scarce thing.** Three manufacturers build the large class; each machine takes years; the AI buildout queued utilities, developers and on-site generators at the same factories, and order books filled through 2030. So a utility reserves slots before it has certificates: Duke went 8 → 19 → 20 → 26 turbines between April 2025 and August 2026, and the first arrived at Person County a year before the plant is due. A denied certificate moves a slot to the next plant; it does not cancel the machine.
5. **Reading the two together.** Turbine count against certified megawatts tells you how much of a plan is still a bet. 26 turbines against 7,501 MW of plants, two of which had certificates only requested (and one since denied), means the six turbines added in August were for plants not yet filed.

**Self-check:** a deck reads "15.4 GW high-confidence, late-stage pipeline, inclusive of signed ESAs; 7.8 GW signed." How much late-stage load is unsigned? (About 7.6 GW.)

## Module 3 — Capacity without an auction, and storage at the coal site

**The single idea:** where there is no capacity market, the reserve margin is argued in front of commissioners with forecasts and accreditation assumptions — and the cheapest firm capacity a utility can add is a battery at a site that is already connected.

1. **Reserve margins by order.** In PJM, an auction three years ahead prices reliability and accredits each resource with an ELCC. In the Carolinas and Florida there is no auction: the commission sets the **planning reserve margin** in the resource-plan docket, the utility's model gives each resource a **capacity credit**, and the size of the gas and storage slice follows from the winter peak the margin is applied to (Duke's 2026 Carolinas plan raises it by more than 10 GW). The accreditation fight — how much a four-hour battery counts — is the same as PJM's, settled by a model instead of a market.
2. **When a plant is refused.** The margin does not fall by itself; the utility refiles, buys purchased power, or leans on demand response — South Carolina's PowerShare Storage pays customers per kilowatt to let the utility call their batteries at the peak (a $120/kW credit, $3.50/kW-month, 30–36 events a year).
3. **Why the coal site.** A retired coal plant has a transmission-voltage point of interconnection, generator step-up transformers, a switchyard, injection rights that surplus-interconnection rules let the replacement resource reuse, industrial zoning, water and roads — so a battery there skips years of queue and most network upgrades. It is also an "energy community" under the tax code, which added a 10 percent bonus to Allen 1's 40 percent investment tax credit. Allen (50 MW, then 167 MW), Riverbend (115 MW) and Mayo are all coal sites.
4. **Who picks the battery.** Three doors: self-build under the resource plan (the utility's engineering group and its EPC choose the BESS in a purchase order the docket never shows); a competitive solicitation (South Carolina's ~400 MW four-hour RFP with utility-ownership and service-agreement tracks; the North Carolina round paused by the commission in April 2026 and in court); and customer-sited or customer-funded batteries (PowerShare; the 2024 hyperscaler tariff agreements for long-duration storage, which have produced no named project). Duke has named no supplier for any of its ~4.5 GW by 2031.

**Self-check:** a 400 MW South Carolina battery RFP offers a utility-ownership track and an energy-storage-service-agreement track. Who chooses the OEM on each? (Utility-ownership: Duke's EPC and procurement group; service agreement: the bidding developer, at bid stage.)

## Module 4 — Funding a $103 billion plan

**The single idea:** a regulated plan is only as large as its balance sheet allows, and the five levers a utility pulls to keep building — CWIP, the credit metric, equity, tax-credit sales and selling part of a utility — tell you when the money actually moves.

1. **CWIP.** Normally a plant earns nothing until it is in service and the utility carries the financing cost. North Carolina's 2025 law (SB 266) lets baseload plants earn on construction work in progress; Indiana already does for Cayuga. That is why a plan whose plants arrive in 2028–2031 does not crush 2026 earnings.
2. **The credit metric.** Rating agencies judge a utility by funds from operations against debt; Duke targets 14.5 percent in 2026 and 15 percent long term. That caps borrowing and forces the third lever — about 35 percent of incremental capital as equity, $10 billion over 2027–2030: an at-the-market program that dribbles shares into the market, and equity units ($1.75 billion in August 2026) whose share purchase settles in August 2029, when the gas plants enter service. Do the reading: the settlement date approximates when the financed plant starts earning.
3. **Tax credits.** Nuclear plants earn a production credit per megawatt-hour, batteries an investment credit, solar a production credit. Under transferability a utility with more credits than tax sells them for cash — Duke agreed to sell up to $3.1 billion of 2025–2028 credits — and passes the value to customers (Florida returned a battery's $50 million ITC to avoid a 2027 rate step). A battery arrives with a discount attached; a gas plant does not. That is why storage is the easier certificate in a contested state.
4. **Selling a fifth of a utility.** Brookfield is buying 19.7 percent of Duke Energy Florida's parent for $6 billion in five tranches through June 2028 — $2.8 billion closed on March 3, 2026 — with two of eleven board seats, consent rights and a put; Duke keeps 80.3 percent and operates. Equity without issuing shares, at the operating-company level. The CFO's "Indiana genco structure under evaluation" is the same idea aimed at the next $5–10 billion. When upside capital and a minority-sale structure are announced together, they are one decision.
5. **The arithmetic, once.** A $1 billion plant financed 53 percent with equity at a 9.8 percent allowed return earns about $52 million a year for its owner; sold 19.7 percent to a partner, the owner keeps 80.3 percent of that and the partner's $6 billion funds the next plant. Compare the earnings-base line in the deck — $114 billion in 2025 to $180 billion in 2030 — with the 8.8 percent growth "net of the Florida minority": the sale shows up as the gap between 9.6 and 8.8 percent.

**Self-check:** why does a utility issue equity units that settle in 2029 rather than shares in 2026? (The plants the equity funds enter rate base around 2029; the hybrid delays dilution to when the assets earn.)

## Module 5 — The tariff spectrum and the load forecast on trial

**The single idea:** a large-load tariff is a negotiation whose opening bid the utility writes differently for each commission, and a certificate is where a load forecast stops being a slide and becomes evidence.

1. **Three drafts.** North Carolina (Duke's rate-case proposal): 50 MW threshold, 75 percent minimum bill, 10–15-year terms, termination charges; the Public Staff and advocates want 25 MW, 85 percent, 20 years and a separate class, and both settlements route the question into an expedited proceeding to finish before rates take effect on January 1, 2027. Florida (Docket 20260064-EI): 50 MW, 20-year term, 75–85 percent take-or-pay, a termination fee of three years of minimum bills plus the net book value of dedicated facilities, 100 percent of extension cost advanced as CIAC — and no rate schedule until the next rate case, which the Office of Public Counsel and a commissioner called "facially noncompliant" with the new statute. Ohio: a proposed Rate DC tariff on the AEP Ohio model (over 25 MW, 85 percent for 12 years). Dominion's GS-5 (14 years; 85/60 percent) is the benchmark advocates cite.
2. **Nuclear in the background.** Eleven units being renewed to 80 years (Oconee 2025, Robinson 2026, Brunswick's application by end-2026) keep 10,773 MW of baseload to the 2050s; about 300 MW of uprates by 2031 add output without a new license; an early site permit at Belews Creek (decision 2027) buys the option of a small modular reactor on the same 2036–2037 horizon as Dominion's. A fleet running at 96.9 percent capacity factor needs peakers and flexible storage beside it, not more baseload — which is why the near-term build is turbines and four-hour batteries.
3. **The denial.** On September 19, 2026 the NCUC refused a certificate for a 255 MW peaker at Smith, 3–1, because about 70 percent of the load growth behind it was data centers "never tested in contested proceedings", and required any reapplication to show compliance with the Ratepayer Protection Pledge Duke signed in July. The turbine slot is reserved; the plant is not. Every utility with a gas program justified on data centers now has a precedent for the question it will be asked.
4. **What a seller does with it.** Watch the resource-plan order (due December 31, 2026), not the plant docket — the plant follows. Read the pledge and the tariff proceeding as the conditions a reapplication must meet. And note the asymmetry: the coal-site batteries were approved without controversy, because a battery with a tax credit that replaces a retiring unit needs no data-center forecast to justify it.

**Self-check:** a commission denies a peaker and approves a battery in the same month. Name the two reasons the battery was easier. (It replaces a retiring unit at an existing interconnection and arrives with a tax credit passed to customers; it does not depend on a contested data-center load forecast.)
