# Enchanted Rock — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Enchanted Rock (private; owner-operator of modular gas microgrids sold as reliability-as-a-service, and of utility-partnered bridge plant) · **Written:** 2026-09-06 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no environmental-permitting or utility-regulation background.

**What this teaches.** How long a machine is allowed to run, and on whose balance sheet it sits. Duty as a legal classification rather than an engineering one; the emergency class and the trap inside it; potential-to-emit arithmetic and how plants are designed to fit beneath a threshold, including where that becomes illegitimate; how idle backup capital is made to earn through aggregation, and the three constraints that bound the model; the choice between a privately-owned bridge and a utility-owned one under an approved rate; and what a utility charges a site that makes its own power.

**Why this gap.** The repo had the physics of emissions nowhere and the procedure of permitting nowhere either. The procedural half is the more commercially consequential: two identical fleets can be permitted for 100 hours a year or for 8,760, the difference is a classification rather than a machine, and almost every "we can permit this quickly" claim in the sector turns on it. The tariff material closes a second gap — the corpus explained what a utility charges its ordinary customers but never what it charges a customer that generates for itself.

**Its place in the bridge-power set.** Fourth of six. Mainspring owns the chemistry and the geography of air quality; this guide owns the procedure, the hours and the ownership question, so the two divide permitting cleanly between them. VoltaGrid supplies the machine, ProEnergy the thermal efficiency, ON.energy the electrical interface, Prevalon the storage and the plant controller. The commission-approval machinery in Module 6 is treated at depth in the Key Capture Energy guide.

## Module 1 — The permit decides the hours
Why regulators treat backup and prime power as different things when the engines are identical: annual emissions are the rate multiplied by the hours, and hours are what a permit can control. The counter-intuitive consequence — the classification that made a fleet cheap to permit is exactly what stops it earning.

## Module 2 — Three duties, three regulatory worlds
Emergency, limited-use, prime and permanently islanded as permit classes rather than engineering categories. Each row's bargain between approval speed and operational freedom. Why a campus wants the first row's timeline and the third row's freedom and cannot have both — and why permitting strategy is settled before the machine is chosen.

## Module 3 — The hours that cost you the class
RICE NESHAP and the emergency allowance. Why the boundary is drawn around *purpose* rather than merely around hours, and what would follow if it were not. The single question to put to any proposal: *is this fleet permitted for what you are being told it will do?* Why gas displaced diesel once duty crossed from rarely to regularly.

## Module 4 — Designing a plant to fit beneath a line
Potential to emit as a ceiling calculated at maximum capacity for every hour, not as a forecast. Major against minor source and what each path costs in time. The three levers — emission rate, enforceable limits accepted as a synthetic minor, and plant size — and what each one costs. Where legitimate threshold engineering ends and paper fragmentation begins, and why the aggregation analysis exists to draw that line.

## Module 5 — Getting paid to be available
Backup generation as insurance bought with capital equipment. Aggregation as the mechanism that turns invisible individual sites into a dispatchable virtual power plant. The reliability-as-a-service model and its dual revenue. The three constraints: the fleet must be permitted for commercial operation; the customer's own reliability always outranks the market, so the revenue is interruptible rather than firm; and programme payments compress as more resources enter — the same saturation dynamic the merchant-storage guides describe for batteries.

## Module 6 — Two ways to own a bridge
Customer-side private ownership against utility ownership under a commission-approved rate. Speed and control against cheap capital, an existing operator and a second life for the asset. Why the second structure converts a merchant equipment sale into regulated infrastructure, and why the rate design — the beneficiary demonstrably paying the full cost so existing ratepayers are held harmless — is what makes it approvable.

## Module 7 — What the utility charges a self-generator
Demand charges and why one bad interval can set the month. Standby service rates as the price of readiness, and the largest surprise in most self-generation business cases. Interruptible service and flexible load interconnection. The strategic point: a campus with on-site generation is a better interconnection candidate than one without, so the plant is not only a bridge — it is why the connection came earlier and stays cheaper.

## Pacing
Seven modules, roughly 30 minutes each. Modules 1–3 are one continuous argument about duty and hours; take them together. Module 4 is the most technical and should be read after Module 4 of the Mainspring guide, which supplies the emissions arithmetic it depends on. Modules 5–7 are commercial and can be taken singly, though 6 and 7 pair well.

## Sources for the technology and industry content
Grounded in the company's dossier and the sources registered there, plus standard air-permitting and utility-tariff fundamentals. No new research was carried out for this guide. Regulatory allowances are described at the level of generality the concepts registry already uses; no specific threshold figures, programme rules or approved rates are asserted, since these vary by jurisdiction and change.
