# Energy Capital Partners — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Energy Capital Partners (ECP), the infrastructure platform of Bridgepoint Group plc, with Calpine/Constellation, Cornerstone/Talen, ProEnergy and the KKR–ECP partnership · **Written:** 2026-10-03 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1) · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no finance background.

**Purpose:** teach how a closed-end infrastructure fund turns power plants into cash, and why in 2026 the best buyer of a gas plant was a company powering data centres:
- the fund clock — why a ten-year fund must sell, and what DPI and carried interest are;
- enterprise value versus equity value, using one sale that was reported at three different prices;
- being paid in the buyer's shares — lock-ups, registration rights and the slow exit;
- FERC Section 203 — who may own a power plant, and how a 19% minority holder stalled a deal;
- why a gas plant beside a data-centre site became the asset of 2026;
- what an aeroderivative turbine is for, and what it costs in efficiency;
- what a fund-owned storage developer buys and when;
- reading a subsidiary through its listed parent's segment.

No company trivia: founding dates, partners' names and fund sizes stay in the dossier. The in-app guide (Profiler → Energy Capital Partners → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

## Module 1 — The fund clock

**The single idea:** a closed-end fund promises to return its investors' money after about ten years, so every asset it owns has a sale date, and the manager is paid most when it sells well.

1. **The commitment.** Investors (pensions, sovereign funds, insurers) commit capital to a fund; the manager draws it over about five years to buy assets. ECP's sixth fund closed in August 2026 at USD 8.1bn against a USD 5bn target.
2. **The fee and the carry.** The manager earns a management fee on committed capital, and once investors have their money back plus a hurdle, a share of profits called carried interest. Bridgepoint recognised carried interest from ECP V for the first time in the first half of 2026 — after the Cornerstone sale.
3. **The measure.** DPI, distributions to paid-in capital, counts only cash returned. A fund marked at three times its cost on paper has a DPI of zero until it sells. ECP's own explanation for raising a bigger fund: 'our DPI has played a very big role'.
4. **The clock in action.** ECP V bought Cornerstone's gas plants in August 2025 and sold them in June 2026 for 4.4 times its money. Calpine, held since 2018 by older funds and a continuation vehicle, went to Constellation in January 2026.
5. **For a seller.** A fund-owned plant spends to raise its value before a sale; after the sale the buyer inherits the equipment and the suppliers. Ask how many years remain on the owner's clock.

**Self-check:** a fund bought a plant fleet in August and sold it the following June. Why is a quick sale rational rather than reckless? *(The fund is judged on realised cash; if a strategic buyer pays a premium, selling early converts a paper mark into DPI and starts the carry.)*

## Module 2 — Enterprise value versus equity value

**The single idea:** the headline price of a company sale usually includes the debt the buyer takes on; the owners receive only the equity part.

1. **Definitions.** Enterprise value is equity value plus net debt — what the whole business is worth to someone who must also carry its borrowing. Equity value is what the shareholders are paid.
2. **One sale, three figures.** Constellation's purchase of Calpine was announced at about USD 26.6bn of enterprise value and about USD 16.4bn of equity (50 million shares plus about USD 4.5bn of cash), with about USD 12.7bn of net debt assumed. At closing a year later Bridgepoint stated USD 33bn of enterprise value — the same debt, a higher share price.
3. **Why headlines inflate.** Trade press reports enterprise value because it is the larger number; a seller counting its proceeds should use equity. The corpus has previously misread a transaction value as an equity price (the Blackstone–QTS case).
4. **Stock moves the price.** When the consideration is shares, the equity value changes every day until closing. Two-thirds of ECP's Calpine proceeds were Constellation stock.
5. **For a seller.** A plant's purchase price does not change what it spends on equipment; its new owner's debt load might.

**Self-check:** a sale is reported at USD 3.5bn; the filing shows USD 2.6bn of cash and USD 0.9bn of stock. Is the USD 3.5bn equity or enterprise value? *(Equity — it is the sum of what the owners received; enterprise value would add the target's net debt.)*

## Module 3 — Paid in the buyer's shares

**The single idea:** when a buyer pays in stock, the seller becomes a shareholder of the buyer and can only sell slowly, under a lock-up and a registration rights agreement.

1. **The lock-up.** Constellation paid two-thirds of the Calpine price in its own shares and locked half of them until 30 June 2026 and half until 30 June 2027. Talen locked the Cornerstone shares for 90 and 180 days.
2. **The registration rights agreement.** A contract obliging the buyer to register the seller's shares so they can be sold publicly in orderly blocks once the lock-up lifts.
3. **The first sale.** When the first Calpine tranche unlocked, the holders sold 11 million shares at USD 281 in one underwritten offering — ECP about 7.6 million — and Constellation bought back 2 million. ECP still held 4.0% of Constellation and 5.0% of Talen in October 2026.
4. **Why it matters.** The seller of a fleet remains a voice at the buyer for a year or more. ECP told Talen it was 'a significant shareholder' excited about Ohio's growth.
5. **For a seller.** The former owner's view of the fleet's spending does not vanish at closing; it moves to the shareholder register.

**Self-check:** why would a buyer want the seller locked up? *(To stop a large block of its own shares being dumped on the market immediately after the deal, which would depress the price it just paid in.)*

## Module 4 — Who may own a power plant: FERC Section 203

**The single idea:** every change of control over a US generator needs federal approval, and the regulator looks through to anyone holding 10% or more of the voting equity.

1. **The test.** FERC asks whether a transfer harms competition, rates or regulation. It treats a holder of 10% or more of voting equity as an affiliate whose control rights must be explained.
2. **The refusal.** In March 2024 FERC declined to approve Bridgepoint's purchase of ECP because the application did not explain how Blue Owl Capital — owner of 19.3% of ECP — was prevented from controlling it. The application was refiled with the missing information and the deal closed in August.
3. **The conditions.** For Calpine, FERC required the sale of about 3.5 GW of plants in the PJM market and capped Constellation's offers there. Opponents asked FERC to make ECP sell its Constellation shares; FERC did not.
4. **The pattern.** ECP's Lightstone purchase, Cornerstone's sale and the Calpine sale each ran the same gate, with months between signing and closing.
5. **For a seller.** A signed power deal is not a customer yet; and the conditions can move plants to a different owner.

**Self-check:** a fund buys a plant portfolio and tells FERC it will be the only holder of 10% or more of the voting equity. Why say so? *(Because any other 10%-plus holder would be an affiliate whose control FERC must assess; stating sole control simplifies the review.)*

## Module 5 — Why a gas plant beside a data centre became the asset of 2026

**The single idea:** the scarce input for new data centres is connected power, so an existing plant with a site next door is worth more than its electricity.

1. **The queue.** A new campus normally waits years for a grid connection and new generation. A campus on or beside a plant can take power behind the meter or through a dedicated line and skip much of the wait.
2. **ECP's version.** The USD 50bn KKR–ECP partnership pairs KKR's data-centre landlords with ECP's generation. Its first campus, CyrusOne's DFW10 in Bosque County, Texas, sits beside Calpine's Thad Hill plant under a 190 MW agreement, with 144 MW of initial IT capacity and operations expected in late 2026.
3. **Who paid for it.** Constellation bought Calpine and Talen bought Cornerstone at prices Bridgepoint describes as record multiples — the premium strategics now pay for generation that can serve load. ECP sold the plant owner while the campus was being built.
4. **The equipment.** Transformers, switchgear, turbines and ride-through storage at such a campus are specified by the plant owner and the developer, not by the fund that paired them.

**Self-check:** a fund sells the power plant beside a data-centre campus it helped finance. Who is the campus's power counterparty afterwards? *(The plant's new owner — the power agreement travels with the plant.)*

## Module 6 — The aeroderivative turbine

**The single idea:** an aeroderivative gas turbine is a jet engine adapted to spin a generator — fast to start, fast to deliver, less efficient than a heavy-frame machine — and in 2026 speed is what a data-centre developer pays for.

1. **Two families.** Heavy-frame turbines (hundreds of megawatts, built for continuous duty, delivery slots now years out) and aeroderivatives (tens of megawatts each, shipped in months, started in minutes). ProEnergy's PE6000 is a 50 MW aeroderivative it describes as interchangeable with GE's LM6000PC.
2. **The trade.** Simple-cycle aeroderivatives convert roughly 40% of the gas's energy into electricity; a combined-cycle plant exceeds 60%. The buyer accepts the fuel penalty for a unit it can have this year and move later.
3. **Bridge power.** A developer waiting for a grid connection installs turbines on site to run the campus until the utility arrives — Crusoe's 650 MW, thirteen-unit order from ProEnergy (April 2026) is the pattern. 'Bridge' means the owner expects to redeploy or sell the units.
4. **Who ECP is here.** ECP owns a majority of ProEnergy (bought 5 September 2024; Bloomberg's reported price about USD 275m for roughly 60%). ProEnergy builds and sells the units and runs its own peaker fleet; ECP's board seats decide whether to keep, list or sell the company — Bloomberg reported in September 2026 an IPO at USD 40–50bn or a sale was being weighed.
5. **The seller's angle.** The turbine order goes to ProEnergy; the switchgear, transformers and storage that let a turbine fleet serve a campus are specified by the campus developer with ProEnergy's engineers.

**Self-check:** why would a data-centre developer buy thirteen 50 MW turbines instead of one 650 MW plant? *(Delivery and flexibility — the small units ship in months, start in minutes and can be redeployed when grid power arrives.)*

## Module 7 — A fund-owned storage developer

**The single idea:** a developer owned by a fund buys batteries project by project, when each project's revenue contract and financing are in place — not on the fund's timetable.

1. **Convergent.** Convergent Energy + Power (New York and Toronto) is ECP's wholly owned storage and solar developer, with more than USD 1bn invested or committed. It develops, owns and operates behind-the-meter and front-of-meter storage for utilities and industrial customers.
2. **When it buys.** A storage project is ordered after a revenue contract (a capacity payment, a tolling agreement or a customer saving) and a lender's approval exist. The purchase follows the contract, so the order calendar is the project calendar.
3. **What the fund decides.** ECP sets Convergent's capital budget and approves large projects; it does not pick the battery supplier. Each project's lenders and independent engineer review the chemistry, warranty and integrator before money moves.
4. **Contracted versus merchant.** Atlantica (renewables, transmission and water on long contracts) and Grain LNG (the Isle of Grain terminal, 50/50 with Centrica, GBP 1.5bn enterprise value) earn under contracts of many years. Convergent's projects can carry merchant exposure after the first contract — storage revenue is less predictable than a regulated return.
5. **The fund's own clock still runs.** Convergent sits in a closed-end fund, so one day it will be sold whole or in parts; a supplier's relationship is with the company, which survives the sale.

**Self-check:** a fund 'commits USD 1bn' to a storage developer. Does the developer place USD 1bn of battery orders that year? *(No — it orders project by project as each contract and financing closes; the commitment is a ceiling, not an order book.)*

## Module 8 — Reading a subsidiary through its listed parent

**The single idea:** ECP publishes no accounts, but Bridgepoint Group plc does, and ECP is Bridgepoint's Infrastructure segment — so the parent's results are the only audited window on the manager.

1. **Where to look.** Bridgepoint's half-year and full-year results on the London Stock Exchange's RNS service carry an Infrastructure segment: assets under management, fee-paying AUM, management fees and a fund-performance table for ECP III to VI.
2. **The AUM line.** Infrastructure AUM was EUR 28.2bn at the end of 2024, EUR 36.0bn at the end of 2025 and EUR 27.7bn at 30 June 2026 — down because Calpine and Cornerstone were sold, not because the business shrank. AUM falls when a fund does its job.
3. **The fund table.** ECP III (2014 vintage, USD 5.1bn, DPI 1.9x); ECP IV (2018, USD 3.3bn plus USD 3.5bn of co-investment, DPI 1.0x); ECP V (2022, USD 4.4bn, marked above 3x in H1 2026 with its carry recognised for the first time); ECP VI closed at USD 8.1bn in August 2026 against a USD 5bn target.
4. **Currency traps.** Bridgepoint reports in euros and pounds, ECP raises in dollars and the SEC's Form ADV reports regulatory AUM in dollars on a different definition. A 'USD 36bn' that is really EUR 36.0bn is the kind of error that reads plausibly.
5. **What the parent cannot tell you.** Segment accounts show fees and marks, not which plants a fund will buy or sell next. For that, read the counterparties: Constellation's and Talen's filings described the Calpine and Cornerstone sales before Bridgepoint booked them.

**Self-check:** a manager's AUM falls by a quarter in six months. Name a reason that is good news for its investors. *(It sold large assets and returned the cash — Bridgepoint returned a record EUR 16.6bn in H1 2026, EUR 11bn of it from Calpine.)*

## Where it fails — the risks in one list

- Stock consideration (Constellation shares from Calpine) is worth what the market says on the day ECP can sell, not what the release said at signing.
- FERC Section 203 and CFIUS can delay or condition every plant sale; the 2024 Bridgepoint combination itself was refused once before approval.
- A ProEnergy IPO at a reported USD 40–50bn is a report; the valuation exists only when the shares price.
- Convergent's merchant storage revenue depends on market rules that regulators rewrite.
- ECP VI's size (USD 8.1bn) must be deployed in a market where strategics now outbid funds for generation.
