# Entergy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach how a multi-jurisdiction utility holding company buys for one campus at a time — what a holding company is to an investor and to five different regulators, how a formula rate plan resets rates yearly and what that does to earnings and to regulatory lag, what the MISO layer above all five operating companies decides and what it leaves to the states, the mechanism of building generation for one named customer under a service agreement that underwrites the plant, what a commission order that names the customer actually contains, the ratepayer-protection terms four states wrote in different words, and the storm-and-securitization background that sets how much political room any of it has — starting from high-school STEM and from the Dominion lesson plan, which this one does not repeat. Companion: the in-app guide (Profiler → Entergy → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**Prerequisite.** The Dominion Energy lesson plan (`study-prep/dominion-energy/`) teaches the revenue-requirement equation, the IRP → CPCN → RFP sequence, the rate case, the anatomy of a large-load tariff, the interconnection study and the RTO layer with PJM as the example. Do that plan first; the Southern Company plan (the elected commission, the no-RTO case) is a useful second. This plan assumes both and adds the holding-company structure, the formula rate plan, MISO South, and the one-customer certificate.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min — the five companies and their five regulators), Module 2 in one sitting (~30 min — the formula rate plan, with the arithmetic done by hand, and MISO South), Module 3 in one sitting (~45 min — the mechanism and the Louisiana order, read clause by clause), Module 4 in one sitting (~30 min — the three states' protection terms and the storm background), Module 5 last (~30 min — Entergy against the chain), then the flashcard and self-test passes in the app.

## Module 1 — One parent, five utilities, five regulators

**The single idea:** a holding company is one stock and five rate bases; the plant is certified and paid for in one state's docket, and the parent's earnings are the sum.

1. **The structure.** Entergy Corporation trades on the NYSE; the company that serves a customer, files a rate case and owns a plant is one of five regulated operating companies — Entergy Louisiana (1.1 million customers), Entergy Arkansas (738,000), Entergy Mississippi (459,000), Entergy Texas (538,000), Entergy New Orleans (209,000) — each with its own territory, rate base, allowed return and regulator, plus System Energy, which owns most of the Grand Gulf reactor under federal regulation. The revenue-requirement equation is solved five times on five calendars. A gas plant for a Louisiana campus is a Louisiana Public Service Commission decision paid for by Entergy Louisiana's customers; no parent-level proceeding can move it to another state's books.
2. **What is shared.** All five sit inside one grid operator — MISO's South region — so the wholesale market, the capacity auction, the interconnection queue and the regional transmission plan are common. A service company plans, operates and buys for the fleet, which is why one RFP can be issued for several operating companies and one turbine agreement can serve all of them. Read an announcement with the layers separated: the megawatts are the parent's story, the certificate is the state's.
3. **Who certifies what.** Louisiana: a five-member elected commission; certification under its general order on new generation (the Louisiana CPCN), where an order can name the customer; a formula rate plan. Arkansas: three appointed commissioners; the CECPN (certificate of environmental compatibility and public need); a formula rate plan under a 2015 statute. Mississippi: three elected commissioners; the CPCN, with a separate statute letting certified plant earn during construction; the state's oldest formula rate plan. Texas: five appointed commissioners; the certificate of convenience and necessity; contested base rate cases with generation and transmission riders between them — and Entergy Texas is the part of Texas outside ERCOT and outside retail choice. New Orleans: a seven-member elected city council regulating its own utility, with a formula plan carrying reliability penalties. Above all five: MISO (accredits, queues, plans, dispatches — never certifies), FERC (tariffs, the system agreement) and the NRC (five reactors).
4. **The row to memorize.** Louisiana: an elected commission, a formula rate plan and a certification order that can name a single customer. That combination is what Module 3's mechanism runs on.

**Self-check:** Entergy's earnings call raises guidance on a plant for a Mississippi campus. Name the body that decides whether that plant enters rate base, the instrument it uses, and the mechanism through which its cost reaches bills. (The Mississippi PSC; here a statutory pre-certification with prudence reviewed after construction; the formula rate plan's interim facilities rate adjustment, which pays a cash return on construction work in progress.)

## Module 2 — The formula rate plan and the MISO layer

**The single idea:** a formula rate plan replaces the contested rate case with an annual arithmetic reset, which makes earnings track rate base within a year — and MISO decides everything about a plant except whether it is built.

1. **The formula, worked once.** The Dominion chain set rates in a contested case every two years and let the earned return drift between cases — regulatory lag. A formula rate plan: each year the utility files its actual earned return on equity; the commission compares it with a band around the allowed return; if the company earned below the band, rates rise by formula, and if above, they fall. Suppose 9.5 percent allowed, a ±0.5-point band, and 8.6 percent earned on $10 billion of equity rate base: 0.4 points below the band's floor, $40 million of shortfall, recovered by the next reset, sometimes under a cap on the yearly change. Next year the new plant is in the filing and the reset runs again.
2. **What it does to the parent.** Three of five companies run on it, so most of the company's earnings track rate base with about a year's lag — which is why an investor can read the capital plan almost as an earnings forecast, and why a multi-billion-dollar plant certified for one customer shows up in guidance so soon after the order. Entergy's plan went from about $43.5 billion to $57 billion to $67 billion between February and June 2026; the 2030 EPS outlook followed.
3. **What it does to the commission.** A yearly look at earnings — more oversight in frequency, less in depth: the annual filing reviews the arithmetic, not the wisdom of the spend. The prudence review still exists. But the yearly reset makes a plant's cost visible on bills fast, and in an elected state that is political: a plant a customer's own contract pays for is one the formula can carry without moving anyone else's bill; a plant whose cost is spread is one the next election can reprice. Hence the company's shift from "the customer pays" to published bill reductions ($4.87 a month avoided in Mississippi in July 2026; about $7 billion over two decades claimed).
4. **MISO South.** Every Entergy company joined MISO in December 2013. Energy dispatched every five minutes; capacity cleared yearly in the Planning Resource Auction by season and zone (a one-year horizon, against PJM's three); resources accredited by ELCC-style methods that discount solar and storage against nameplate; interconnection through the RTO's queue, with an expedited path for resources tied to signed load; big lines planned in the MTEP with costs allocated across the footprint. MISO does not certify a plant. A combined-cycle unit for a Louisiana campus is a state decision; MISO studies its interconnection, accredits it and dispatches it.
5. **The Texas case.** Entergy Texas serves the state's southeast corner inside MISO, not ERCOT — outside retail choice, a vertically integrated utility certified by the Texas commission under a conventional certificate. Texas's 2025 large-load statute was written for ERCOT's queue; no source extends its fee-and-security mechanics to this territory, but the statute's reach is the state's. A campus on the Sabine River lives under Texas law, MISO's market and a regulated tariff at once.
6. **Two MISO features that matter more than the docket.** Accreditation: a battery or a solar farm counts toward the planning reserve margin at a fraction of nameplate, so a utility serving a flat, always-on load reaches for gas first. The seasonal auction: MISO South has cleared short in summer; a utility long on accredited capacity because it built plants for a customer can sell the surplus into the PRA — revenue that flows back through the fuel clause to everyone, and one of the arguments the utility makes when a commission asks who benefits from a one-customer plant.

**Self-check:** a battery developer tells you MISO approved Entergy's new plant. What did MISO actually do, and who approved the plant? (MISO studied the interconnection, accredited the capacity and will dispatch it; the state commission certified it.)

## Module 3 — Buying for one campus: the mechanism and the order that names the customer

**The single idea:** when one customer asks for a gigawatt in a territory whose whole peak is a few gigawatts, the utility does not fold the request into the forecast — it signs a service agreement, sizes plants to it, and asks the commission to certify those plants for that customer.

1. **The service agreement is the load-bearing document.** It fixes the megawatts and the years; sets a minimum monthly charge that bills whether or not the campus runs; requires collateral; and says what the customer owes on early exit. The plant is still rate base — it earns the allowed return and is recovered through the formula plan — but the revenue that recovers it comes, for the term, from the customer who caused it.
2. **The order reads differently.** It recites the customer's obligations as findings; it adds conditions the utility did not volunteer — reporting, cost reviews, renewables alongside the gas, who pays for transmission others will use; and it states the one thing the contract cannot settle: what happens when a fifteen- or twenty-year term ends before a forty-year plant does.
3. **Louisiana phase one, clause by clause (docket U-37425, approved August 20, 2025).** *Customer:* a Meta subsidiary, Richland Parish. *Plants:* three 1×1 combined-cycle units, about 2,260 MW — two at a new Franklin Farms site beside the campus for late 2028, one at the Waterford site for 2029; about $2.4 billion for the first two. *Wires:* about a hundred miles of new 500 kV, eight 230 kV lines, two utility-owned and six customer-owned substations; the customer funds the interconnection in full. *Term:* fifteen years with up to three five-year extensions and a one-year non-renewal notice, per the intervenors' reading of the agreement. *Minimum bill:* monthly charges designed so the customer's payments recover the annual revenue requirement of the new infrastructure during the term — a revenue-requirement match, not a percentage of demand. *Security:* termination payments; a parent guaranty from Meta through the original term. *Customer adds:* at least 1,500 MW of solar and storage through expedited certification, a bill-assistance contribution, a stated $650 million of customer savings over fifteen years, about a tenth off storm charges. *Vote:* four to one on a settlement placed on the agenda ahead of the hearing officer's deadline; the dissenter said he could not verify the numbers. *What the record could not show:* the contracted megawatts and the minimum-bill percentage (redacted); the utility acknowledged it ran no formal competitive solicitation for the turbines.
4. **Phase two (docket U-37882, filed March 2026, decision targeted December 2026).** Seven 754 MW units, 5,278 MW, about $12.9 billion — four beside the campus, three near an existing plant in Pointe Coupee Parish — for 2030–2031; three batteries; a $1.4 billion 500 kV line funded by customer contributions; a twenty-year term with automatic five-year renewals and three-year notice; minimum charges sized to the full incremental cost with true-ups; early termination at all unrecovered cost or net book value; a parent-company guaranty plus credit-default insurance; more than $1 billion toward embedded cost of service and about $700 million toward storm and resilience costs. Fast-tracked four to one in April 2026 so the commission can vote without a hearing officer's recommendation. In August 2026 the commission voted three to one not to compel the customer to disclose jobs and load data.
5. **What changed after the vote.** The customer moved the campus into an 80/20 financing joint venture registered the day of the phase-one vote; intervenors asked whether the parent guaranty still binds an entity that is no longer the customer's parent. The commission declined to investigate. The collateral finding in a one-customer order depends on who owns the customer.
6. **The turbine slot.** A plant that must run when a 2028 campus opens cannot wait for a case that ends in late 2026. The company's answer, in its own filings: an exclusivity agreement with one manufacturer for power islands and turbines in the largest frame class — one slot per quarter, a minimum order raised from fifteen to twenty-one to twenty-seven sets as the filings multiplied, ten fulfilled by mid-2026, "10 CCCTs secured for growth". The equipment decision precedes the certificate by years and is made at the parent level against campuses that have not yet signed. A turbine competitor at a certification hearing is two years late; a balance-of-plant, transformer or switchgear seller at the EPC selection is on time; a battery seller answering the operating company's storage RFP is on the one lane the exclusivity does not touch.

**Self-check:** read a one-customer certificate for three numbers. (The contract term; the plant's life; the share of shared transmission the customer funds. The gap between the first two is the exposure; the third is how much of the system the deal admits it uses.)

## Module 4 — Ratepayer protection in four dialects, and the storm background

**The single idea:** the same mechanism was certified quickly in Louisiana under a waiver, found needed but not yet prudent in Arkansas, pre-certified in Mississippi with the test moved to the end, and handled conventionally in Texas — and every one of those dockets happens in a territory hurricanes cross.

1. **Louisiana.** The "Lightning Amendment" (December 2025, four to one): where a signed electric service agreement with a new load has an in-service date within five years, the commission's solicitation requirement is waived — a fifteen-year minimum contract, the customer covering at least half of new infrastructure cost, an eight-month target. UCS's arithmetic: where a thirty-year plant life exceeds a fifteen-year contract, ratepayers could fund up to 75 percent of the capital. The company's answer is the twenty-year phase-two term and its true-ups.
2. **Arkansas.** The certificate kept, with a clock: Act 373 of 2025 puts 180 days on public-interest and prudence findings and creates a rider recovering financing cost during construction — CWIP recovery under another name — worth $5.77 a month on a residential bill from June 2026 for Ironwood, Jefferson and Cypress. The Google campus: a special-rate contract approved December 2025 (with the requested ratemaking treatment of Google's upfront payments denied), a 600 MW solar-plus-350 MW storage resource approved in early 2026, a 754 MW gas unit on which the commission found need but not cost prudence in January 2026. A newspaper's records request put the contract's numbers in public — $526 million of payments plus $190 million for transmission, a 480 MW minimum against 600 MW, a termination fee near $2 billion — and the commission's staff testified the customer is paying well below the resource's full cost. Hearing November 2026.
3. **Mississippi.** A 2024 statute pre-certifies facilities serving data-processing customers: no contested certificate before construction, prudence reviewed after completion, a cash return on construction work in progress through the formula plan's interim adjustments. Three 754 MW plants for the Amazon Web Services campuses — Delta Blues (May 2028), Vicksburg (August 2028), Traceview (2029) — at about $1.2 billion each; about $3.9 billion pre-certified in all. An independent report puts residential payments at $38 million through March 2026 and $10.60 a month; the company says customers are not subsidizing data centers and points to a $4.87 monthly increase avoided in July 2026. Both are readings of the same mechanism.
4. **Texas, the control case.** Orange County (1,215 MW, in service Q3 2026), Legend (754 MW, Port Arthur) and Lone Star (453 MW) for 2028, the 145-mile SETEX 500 kV line for 2029 — conventional certificates, an appointed commission that imposed a $2.4 billion hard cap and said it had been "backed into a corner" by the absence of a solicitation, and a build-to-suit lease that sold Legend to investors mid-construction. No named data-center customer.
5. **Who pays when the customer pays — the two readings.** *The customer pays, so no one else does:* the load is real by construction; a contract-paid plant is the cleanest growth a formula plan can carry; early-year surplus sells into the PRA and flows back through the fuel clause. *Everyone pays something, so the terms must say what:* a fifteen-year contract against a forty-year plant; transmission that serves the region once built; fuel and reserve-margin effects shared regardless. The commission's conditions are where the two meet, and the order is read again at every formula reset.
6. **The storm background.** A major hurricane can do billions of damage to one operating company's wires in a week. The Gulf's instrument is securitization: a legislature authorizes a dedicated bill charge, bonds are sold against it at near-sovereign rates, the utility recovers at once and customers pay over years at a low rate. Francine (2024): about $180 million, settled at $15 million a year. Winter Storm Fern (January 2026): about $450 million; Mississippi passed a securitization statute in April 2026 with a filing planned for the third quarter. Two consequences: the same non-bypassable-charge machinery is what a commission reaches for to carry a data-center plant's cost in a way it can explain; and the storm charges already on the bill are the affordability baseline every new plant is judged against — which is why the company now says the customer will pay about a tenth of Entergy Louisiana's storm and resilience charges.

**Self-check:** three states, one grammar. State in one sentence each what Louisiana, Arkansas and Mississippi changed about the certificate. (Louisiana waived the solicitation where a service agreement exists; Arkansas kept the certificate and put a clock and a construction-cost rider on it; Mississippi removed the pre-construction certificate and moved the prudence test to the end.)

## Module 5 — Entergy against the chain: the programs, the megawatts, who buys, who competes

**The single idea:** the Dominion chain with five commissions instead of one, and two rungs reordered — the customer contract comes before the plan, and the turbine commitment comes before both.

1. **The chain, rung by rung.** *The plan:* a system-wide resource plan built by the service company; the parent's capital plan and its "planning for 24 GW" investor-day resource plan — too early for a purchase, the place to learn which state's need is real. *The customer contract:* the ESA — not a seller's document, but the date it is signed is when the plant's equipment is committed. *The certificate:* Louisiana certification, the Arkansas CECPN, Mississippi pre-certification, the Texas certificate — balance-of-plant and EPC selection run alongside it. *The solicitation:* operating-company RFPs through one portal — all-source, combined-cycle, renewables-and-storage, stand-alone BESS — the battery lane, the one channel outside the turbine exclusivity. *The rate order:* three formula plans, one rate case with riders, the council's plan; riders that recover construction cost during the build. *Interconnection:* MISO's queue with its expedited process for signed load; the utility's own 500 kV program. *The regional layer:* MISO South — the PRA, accreditation, the MTEP.
2. **Megawatts on the clock.** Owned and leased capability 24,621 MW; decade-average summer peak 22,168 MW; approved or in-progress gas units 8,146 MW across ten units for 2026–2029 (1,215 in Texas for 2026; 754 + 754 + 754 + 453 + 446 + 1,508 for 2028; 754 + 754 + 754 for 2029); phase-one Louisiana 2,262 MW; phase-two Louisiana 5,278 MW pending; potential data-center additions the company calls 7–12 GW beyond the base case; about 7 GW of renewables and storage nameplate set aside, most still to be identified. The plants filed for one customer's second phase exceed a quarter of the whole system's decade-average peak.
3. **The storage lane.** Cypress Solar in Arkansas (600 MW solar, 350 MW / 1,400 MWh storage, about $1.6 billion, 2028 — developer Orion Renewables, EPC Wanzek, per trade press; battery supplier unnamed); a 170 MW Penton battery in Mississippi (2028); three Louisiana batteries filed with phase two; a 2025 Louisiana BESS RFP whose selections were delayed as of August 2026; Mississippi and Arkansas renewables-and-storage RFPs. No battery supplier or integrator is named on any Entergy-owned project in any source found. In megawatts the listed storage is about a gigawatt against roughly eight of approved gas.
4. **Who buys, who competes.** For turbines: one J-class OEM (Mitsubishi Power at Orange County and Delta Blues by name; the exclusivity vendor by inference) — a market closed for the decade. For balance of plant and wires: the EPCs (Sargent & Lundy and TIC at Orange County; unnamed elsewhere) and the transmission organization. For storage: the developer bidding the RFP or the utility's own project team. For the customers: Meta, Google, Amazon Web Services and Hut 8 by name; Dominion's published GS-5 class and Entergy's sealed single-customer ESAs are the two ends of the regulated-contracting spectrum a hyperscaler compares.
5. **Where it fails, in one line each.** The date moves — MISO's agreement, the 500 kV line's certificate and the turbine slot are three clocks. The term ends before the plant does. The second phase is filed before the first is built, and the exclusivity minimum rises to match. Five regulators, five readings. The formula makes the cost visible within a year — one election cycle. The storm arrives. Sold to the wrong desk — the turbines were committed at the parent years before any certificate.

**Self-check:** a transformer supplier wants into the Louisiana phase-two plants. Which meeting, and when? (The EPC selection for the named plants, running alongside the certification docket in the second half of 2026 — not the October hearing, and not the parent's turbine agreement, which is closed.)

## Whole-plan self-test

1. What is a holding company to an investor and to a regulator? (One stock; five separately regulated rate bases.)
2. Write the formula rate plan's reset in one sentence. (Earned return compared with a band around the allowed return; rates move by formula to bring it back, without a contested case.)
3. What does MISO decide about a one-customer plant, and what does it not? (Interconnection, accreditation, dispatch, regional lines; never the certificate.)
4. Why is Entergy Texas not in ERCOT, and what follows? (It serves the southeast corner inside MISO South, outside retail choice — a vertically integrated utility under a conventional Texas certificate.)
5. Name the four terms of a service agreement that let a utility carry a one-customer plant to its commission. (Megawatts and term; minimum monthly bill; collateral; early-termination obligations.)
6. What did the phase-two Louisiana ESA change from phase one? (Twenty-year term against fifteen; three-year notice against one; minimum charges sized to full incremental cost with true-ups; a parent-company guaranty plus credit-default insurance.)
7. What does Louisiana's Lightning Amendment waive, and on what condition? (The solicitation requirement, where a signed ESA has an in-service date within five years.)
8. How did Arkansas and Mississippi each change the certificate? (Arkansas: a 180-day clock and a construction-financing rider; Mississippi: pre-certification with prudence reviewed after construction and a cash return on CWIP.)
9. Why does the turbine exclusivity agreement matter more to a seller than any certificate? (The turbine is committed at the parent years before the state decides who pays; the open lanes are balance of plant, storage and wires.)
10. What is securitization, and how does it shape a data-center docket? (Bonds backed by a dedicated bill charge that recover an approved cost at once; the storm charges already on the bill are the affordability baseline a new plant is judged against.)
