# Excelsior Energy Capital — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach **the fund layer above the storage and solar platforms this corpus already covers** — what a closed-end renewable infrastructure fund is and why power plants need one, how to read a private fund from its public filings (Form D, Form ADV, boilerplate, counterparty records), the three moments at which a fund can touch a project (operating, construction start, development), what a battery supply reservation is and why a buyer signs one for more gigawatt-hours than it has projects, how the domestic-content bonus turns a procurement decision into a tax position, how an operating portfolio is priced when it is sold (EV/EBITDA, consideration versus enterprise value, the harvest period), and how a hyperscaler buys a solar farm's output without ever contracting with its owner (the green tariff). Excelsior Energy Capital is the worked example because it is small enough to read whole — two funds, USD 1.5 billion of regulatory AUM, 25 people — and because its record is unusually legible: SEC filings, a wholly-owned platform (Lydian Energy), two exits with prices, and 9.7 GWh of batteries reserved years ahead of the projects that will use them.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **Aypa** plan teaches what a buyer pays for a contracted platform, the financing stack and tax-credit monetisation; the **GridStor** plan teaches the sponsor-created specialist and buying late-stage projects; the **Spearmint** plan teaches the merchant developer; **Dominion** teaches the regulated side of the green tariff. This plan climbs one level to the manager who raises the money and repeats none of those. The **Fluence** and **LG Energy Solution** dossiers carry the seller's side of the two supply agreements taught here.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** A closed-end fund is a pool of money with a calendar, and everything the manager does — buy, build, sell — happens on that calendar. A reservation agreement is an option on supply, not a delivery schedule, and its value is a policy bonus captured before the projects exist. And when a renewable owner says it "supplies Meta" or "supplies Google", the name on the contract is almost always a utility.

**One boundary, held throughout.** Business Wire, Excelsior's release channel, could not be read from this environment; every Excelsior release was read through a syndication mirror (Yahoo Finance, AOL, Utah Business, Oil and Gas Press, Pulse 2.0) or the firm's own news page, and is cited as such. Fund returns, fees and the Form ADV Part 2 brochure were not available; where the plan gives an illustrative figure (the domestic-content arithmetic) it says so.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min). Module 2 with the Form ADV PDF open (~35 min). Module 3 (~25 min). Module 4 with the two supply releases side by side (~35 min). Module 5 (~25 min). Then flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — What a closed-end fund is, and why power plants need one

**The single idea:** a closed-end fund is a fixed pool of commitments with a ten-to-twelve-year life; investors cannot withdraw, which is exactly what lets the manager own assets that cannot be sold in a week.

**The calendar.** Fundraising window (a year or two) → investment period (four to five years, during which committed money is called and deployed) → harvest (assets sold, capital returned) → wind-up. Excelsior's Fund I: first sale 8 September 2017 (Form D), final close USD 504 million January 2021, "fully deployed across 16 investments totaling 1.95 GW", first exit December 2023, second exit (830 MW to Enel, about USD 1 billion) February 2026. Fund II: first sale 19 December 2022, first close USD 502 million March 2023, final close "just over $1 billion" against USD 750 million on 8 April 2025, "Over 50% deployed across 15 investments" at close.

**Who the investors are.** Limited partners: pension plans, insurers, fund-of-funds, asset managers, endowments, family offices, financial institutions, from the US, Japan, Europe, Australia and the Middle East; the Development Bank of Japan as Fund II's anchor investor (and Fund I's seed partner). The Form ADV's "47% non-US clients" is the anchor's footprint.

**How the manager is paid.** Management fee (1–2% a year, runs the firm) and carried interest (a share of realised profit above a hurdle, rewards the sale). Excelsior publishes neither; the Form ADV shows 5% related-person ownership of the funds and a USD 10 million minimum commitment — founders co-invest, investors are institutions.

**Why this structure fits power plants.** Contracted, long, illiquid cash flows want patient capital whose liabilities are equally long; the fund rents the manager's judgment for a decade and returns the money on a date.

**Self-check:** why does the manager sell an asset with fifteen years of PPA left? *(Because the fund must return capital within its life, a strategic buyer with cheaper capital pays more for the same cash flows, and carried interest is earned on realised gains.)*

## Module 2 — Reading a private fund from its public filings

**The single idea:** a private manager publishes no accounts, but three public records exist and each answers a different question.

**Form D.** Filed by the fund vehicle within 15 days of first sale, amended as more is sold: total offering, amount sold to date, number of investors, first-sale date, placement agents, named executive officers. Fund I's D/A (29 January 2021): USD 503,787,879 sold to 27 investors; agents Probitas, Trinity, Consilium, MMX Global. Fund II's last amendments (2024) show about USD 680 million across the main LP and two feeder funds — a floor filed a year before the USD 1 billion close. Lesson: a Form D is authoritative *at its date* and is not re-filed at final close.

**Form ADV Part 1.** Filed by the adviser annually by 30 March: regulatory AUM (USD 1,505,418,687 across two pooled vehicles), employees (25), share of non-US clients (47%), the fund vehicles with their 805- identifiers, minimum commitment, related-person ownership. Lesson: "regulatory AUM" is an SEC-defined gross figure and can differ from a firm's "equity capital" — at Excelsior they happen to agree.

**Boilerplate.** "over $1 billion of equity capital" (mid-2024) → "over $1.45 billion in assets under management" (October 2024) → "over $1.5 billion of equity capital" (December 2024 on). Lesson: the boilerplate switches definitions; quote the filing.

**Counterparty records.** Enel's about USD 1 billion / USD 1.3 billion EV for 830 MW (Excelsior issued no release); KeyBanc's record of the BlackRock sale; Deutsche Bank's "US$3bn of equity investments across 2.3 GW". Lesson: the firm's largest transactions may be documented only by the other side.

**Grid and regulator records.** The ERCOT queue record for Headcamp showed July 2026 against a Q4 2025 release target. Lesson: the grid operator's record is the schedule check.

**Exercise:** open the Form ADV PDF and find the three numbers above; then find the sentence in the Enel coverage that names the selling entity ("Excelsior Renewable Energy Intermediate Fund I LP") and match it to the EDGAR company list.

## Module 3 — Own, operate or develop: three moments a fund can touch a project

**The single idea:** the earlier a fund enters, the more of the development margin it keeps and the more risk it carries.

**Operating.** Buy a running plant with a contract: Prairie Breeze wind from Invenergy (109 MW, AA-rated municipal offtakers), El Centro, Syncarpha, and the 830 MW later sold to Enel. Lowest risk, lowest return.

**Construction start.** Partner structures in which a developer originates and Excelsior "acquires projects upon construction start" (Unico 2020, Nokomis 2019–2020, Regis/REX 2022), and Faraday Solar, bought from Parasol and Clenera and financed by Excelsior at USD 1.3 billion (USD 400 million PTC tax equity, USD 460 million senior loan, USD 300 million tax-exempt loan, USD 250 million ancillary). Construction risk, no development risk.

**Development — through a platform.** Lydian Energy, co-founded 2023, wholly owned by Fund II per trade press, with its own CEO and pipeline: Texas batteries (550 MW / 1.1 GWh, USD 233 million, July 2025), the USD 689 million February 2026 tranche, and Hanwha's late-stage Atlas North (1,000+ MW solar, 450 MW / 1,800 MWh BESS, April 2026). The fund never develops; its platform does — and even then late-stage. No source shows origination by either.

**Why a platform.** It captures the margin between permitted and operating, keeps a team between deals, and gives the fund's reserved equipment (Module 4) somewhere to go. The cost is schedule risk and a pipeline number that moves (1.75 → 4.7 → 4.4 → 5.1 GW).

**The category question.** Excelsior is an investor that owns a developer. The `developer` label rests on Lydian.

## Module 4 — Reserving batteries before the projects exist

**The single idea:** a supply framework is an option on manufacturing volume, signed to capture a policy bonus and de-risk supply, and it is only as real as its draw-down.

**The two agreements.** Fluence, 30 July 2024: 2.2 GWh of Gridstack Pro, cells "primarily manufactured in Tennessee" (AESC, 305 Ah LFP per Fluence's site), modules made in Utah, deliveries "beginning in 2025", "five to 10 project sites" in ERCOT and CAISO. LG Energy Solution Vertech, 19 December 2024: 7.5 GWh of "fully integrated" LFP long-cell systems with AEROS software, "First delivery slated for April 2026", "All projects will meet domestic content requirements". Neither names a project. Heliene, 10 April 2024: 2 GW of mostly US-made modules.

**Why more GWh than projects.** Supply certainty (Frantz: the Fluence deal "reduces battery supply risks for our projects"); the domestic-content bonus — 10 extra ITC points for projects whose manufactured components meet the US-made threshold; and insulation from tariffs and FEOC rules on Chinese cells.

**The arithmetic (illustrative).** A 200 MW / 400 MWh battery at USD 600/kWh installed ≈ USD 240 million; base ITC 30% ≈ USD 72 million; the 10-point adder ≈ USD 24 million. Across several GWh the adder exceeds a US supplier's price premium. (The dossier carries no Excelsior battery prices; the point is the order of magnitude.)

**The trap.** Two years on, no source names a project that received Gridstack Pro; Excelsior appears in no Fluence SEC filing or earnings call; Lydian's first three batteries use CATL cells, its CEO said the frameworks "do not have an impact on Lydian's three projects", and Energy-Storage.news calls LG a "secondary supplier". When you read a GWh supply headline, ask which project drew on it and when.

**Exercise:** put the Fluence and LG releases side by side and list what each promises (volume, product, timing, domestic content, value, named project). Then find the sentence in the Lydian USD 233 million coverage that names the battery supplier — and notice the release itself does not.

## Module 5 — Selling the portfolio, and the hyperscaler behind the utility

**The single idea (sale):** an operating renewable portfolio is priced on its contracted cash earnings, and the fund's calendar decides when it is sold.

**The Enel sale.** 830 MW of operating wind and solar; about USD 1 billion consideration (EUR 850 million); about USD 1.3 billion enterprise value; about 2.1 TWh a year; about USD 145 million EBITDA — roughly 8x EV/EBITDA (Enerdatics). The USD 300 million between consideration and EV is project debt the buyer assumes; the fund's equity receives the consideration. Seller: a Fund I vehicle — this is the harvest period. Conditions: antitrust, FERC, foreign-direct-investment review; closing expected Q3 2026. Excelsior issued no release.

**Why now.** The fund's life; a strategic buyer's lower cost of capital (Enel pays from operating cash); carried interest on realised gains. The BlackRock sale (38 distributed projects, 89 MWdc, December 2023) was the test at small scale.

**The single idea (tariff):** in a regulated territory a data centre cannot buy from the solar farm across the street; the utility signs the PPA and passes the project through to the customer on a green tariff.

**Faraday Solar.** 685.3 MWdc / 525 MWac, COD 30 September 2025, "PacifiCorp's 20-year power purchase agreement via Rocky Mountain Power's Schedule 34 green energy tariff", Meta "purchasing energy and environmental attributes". The lenders underwrote PacifiCorp's credit; Meta is a tariff customer. Skyhawk (TVA Green Invest, Google) is the same structure. For the owner: investment-grade credit, but no repeatable relationship with the hyperscaler.

**Where it fails** (recap for the app's callout): the reservation that never draws down; schedule slip on a merchant asset; the fund clock against the market; boilerplate quoted as data; the hyperscaler that is not your customer; a pipeline number read as a commitment.

**Self-check:** a release says a fund "supplies Meta" from a Utah solar farm. Whose name is on the PPA, and what document would show it? *(PacifiCorp's; the Schedule 34 tariff filing and the project's PPA — the owner's release names the tariff and the buyer, not a direct contract.)*
