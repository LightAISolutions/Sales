# Exelon — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach the wires-only utility — why a company that connects 11 GW of prospective data-center load owns no power plants and cannot buy the batteries its load requires; what a transmission security agreement is, why it is a federal contract rather than a state tariff, and what a $1 letter of credit tested; how to read a pipeline ladder whose top number went down by design; what a capacity auction clearing at its cap three years running means for a customer's bill and a utility's politics; and who does buy the storage in a restructured state — starting from high-school STEM. ## Module 1 — The wires-only utility

**The single idea:** in a restructured state the utility owns transmission and distribution, customers buy energy from suppliers or a state procurement, and the utility earns a return only on wires.

1. **Restructuring.** In the late 1990s Illinois, Pennsylvania, Maryland, New Jersey, Delaware and DC separated generation from delivery. Exelon completed its own separation in February 2022 when it spun off its power plants as Constellation; the 10-K now says 'The Registrants do not own generation'. Its six utilities — ComEd, PECO, BGE, Pepco, Delmarva Power, Atlantic City Electric — serve about 10.7 million customers across 25,550 square miles in PJM.
2. **What the bill contains.** A delivery charge set by the state commission (ComEd's multi-year plan; Maryland's multi-year plans; Pennsylvania rate cases) and a supply charge set by the market and the state's procurement — in Illinois by the Illinois Power Agency. Exelon says 75 percent of recent bill increases were supply; it does not earn on that half.
3. **Where growth goes.** A wires-only utility grows by building wires: the 2026–2029 plan is $41.7 billion, raised in 2026 by adding $1.5 billion of transmission while cutting $350 million of distribution and deferring $1.1 billion at PECO and BGE; transmission rate base grows about 16 percent a year against 7.9 percent overall, and the company cites $12–17 billion of further transmission it has not yet put in the plan. ComEd's transmission earns an 11.50 percent allowed return under FERC's formula — higher than any state distribution return in the group.
4. **What it cannot do.** It cannot build a plant or a battery into rate base without a statute — which is why every Exelon 'generation' story in this dossier is a legislative campaign (Module 4).

**Self-check:** a battery supplier wants to sell to Exelon. Why is there no purchase order today? (The utility owns no generation or storage; Illinois buys its batteries through a state agency, Maryland through solicitations, and the one utility-owned battery — ACE's 500 MW Pittsgrove — is a petition pending a February 2027 decision.)

## Module 2 — The transmission security agreement

**The single idea:** a TSA is a federal contract in which a data center commits revenue and collateral for the transmission built to serve it — and because it is federal, its disputes go to FERC and federal court, not the state commission.

1. **Two layers.** At the Illinois Commerce Commission, ComEd's Rider DE and Large Demand Project Applicant rules (order of March 19, 2026) set the entry fee for any request of 50 MW or more: a $1 million deposit for 50–200 MW plus $500,000 per additional 100 MW above 200 MW, a letter of credit when deposits exceed $2 million, two deposits (study; long-lead materials), refundable net of ComEd's costs if the project dies. The commission called it 'an important first step' and opened an eight-month investigation into a separate tariff. At FERC, the TSA carries the commercial terms: 'defined ramp, customer facility readiness obligation, credit obligations, committed revenue contributions, shortfall payments if usage misses commitments, and published termination-fee schedule', with letters of credit covering ten years of transmission revenue.
2. **Why federal.** Transmission service is FERC-jurisdictional; a bilateral transmission contract is filed at FERC and accepted under the Mobile-Sierra presumption — the negotiated rate stands unless it seriously harms the public interest. The Illinois attorney general protested that 'the safeguards in the TSA do not prevent existing customers from paying the costs'; FERC accepted eight agreements between February and March 2026 anyway, with one commissioner warning bilateral deals 'may not be sufficient'.
3. **The $1 letter of credit.** ComEd cancelled the 1.8 GW PowerHouse Hillwood Joliet TSA on July 24, 2026 over the timing and form of the initial security; the developer had posted a $1 letter of credit and called the cancellation the use of 'monopoly power as a bludgeon'. On September 22 FERC rejected the cancellation but declined to decide whether $1 satisfied 'ambiguous contract terms involving credit support' — that question is now in the Northern District of Illinois. One commissioner: 'an embarrassing legal fiction'. Seven weeks later ComEd signed two more TSAs: six active, more than $1 billion of collateral.
4. **Compare the instruments.** Dominion's tariff sets a minimum demand charge; DTE's special contract funds utility batteries; WEC's tariff sells a subscription to named plants; NV Energy's LLESA charges for generation up front. Exelon's TSA secures the wires only — it can, because the utility builds nothing else — and it is the one the other states are copying (New Jersey's A796 lets its regulator use TSAs).

**Self-check:** why does a dispute over a ComEd data-center contract end up in federal court rather than at the ICC? (The contract is for transmission service, filed and accepted at FERC; the ICC's rules govern deposits and procedure, not the ramp, revenue and termination terms.)

## Module 3 — Reading the ladder

**The single idea:** the top number of a pipeline is worth nothing until you know which rung it is on — and Exelon's went from 18 GW to 11 GW because it moved projects down, not because demand fell.

1. **The rungs.** 'Under study' is everything that has applied (25 GW at Q2 2026). 'High probability' was projects in official engineering phases with deposits (7 GW). 'Under signed TSA' is projects that have posted collateral and accepted a ramp and shortfall terms (4 GW, about $1 billion). The refined queue is the sum: 36 GW, down from 43. The headline '11 GW' is the top two rungs; about 9 GW is ComEd, about 2 GW the Mid-Atlantic.
2. **The history.** 16 GW high-probability at year-end 2024; 18 GW 'advanced' at Q3 2025 with 6 GW 'close to signing TSAs'; 18 GW 'about 45 percent secured with TSAs' at Q4 2025; 18 GW unchanged at Q1 2026; 11 GW at Q2 2026 — 'we have now weeded out speculative projects'. Timing from 2025 still stands: about 10 percent online by 2028, a third by 2030, three-quarters by 2034, with a typical interconnection of about 36 months.
3. **The open arithmetic.** 45 percent of 18 GW is about 8 GW under TSA at Q4 2025; the Q2 2026 figure is 4 GW — while collateral stayed about $1 billion. The deck that would reconcile them was unreadable; the PowerHouse cancellation (1.8 GW) is the only named removal. A careful reader records the gap and asks on the next call.
4. **What it means for a seller.** The 4 GW is the most bankable data-center load in the corpus; the 25 GW is the least. The developers holding the TSAs — Amazon at PECO; PowerHouse, Tract, Equinix, QTS, Aligned, Karis, Monarch Rock Air at ComEd — are the buyers of the equipment; the utility is the gate.

**Self-check:** Exelon's 'high probability' fell 40 percent in a quarter. Did demand fall? (No — the vetting process reclassified projects that had not posted collateral; the queue itself fell from 43 to 36 GW, and TSA-backed load is stated at 4 GW.)

## Module 4 — The auction at its cap

**The single idea:** a capacity market is supposed to pay generators to exist; when it clears at a price cap three years running and still comes up 6.8 GW short, the utility that does not own generation argues to own some.

1. **The Base Residual Auction.** PJM buys capacity about three years ahead; every load-serving utility pays the clearing price. The 2028/29 auction (July 14, 2026) cleared every zone at the $325/MW-day cap, procured 6,831 MW less than the reliability requirement — a requirement up 1,374 MW 'largely due to additional Large Loads' — and attracted 525 MW of new generation and uprates. The collar (cap and floor) was extended by FERC in 2026 with Exelon's support.
2. **What it does to bills.** Exelon's simulation without the cap: about $555/MW-day RTO-wide and $777 in ComEd's zone. Atlantic City Electric residential bills would rise $14.70–23.64 a month if the cap lifts after the December auction; Mid-Atlantic supply costs could rise 'up to 80 percent over five years'. None of that revenue is Exelon's.
3. **The campaign.** Butler: 'Even at the highest allowed price, the market is not attracting the level of new supply the system needs'; 'utility-owned generation needs to be part of that mechanism as a cost-effective complement to market-based solutions'. Pepco Holdings' chief: 'If the merchant, independent guys won't build, we will.' The evidence base is a Charles River Associates report estimating $9.6–20 billion of savings for PJM customers in 2028/29 from utility-generated power. The vehicles are Maryland's HB 1561 ('utility-owned backstop, particularly storage and renewable resources'), a Pennsylvania bill the CEO calls 'a long shot', and PJM's reliability backstop procurement, which consumer advocates asked FERC to reject.
4. **The other side.** Exelon petitioned FERC in 2024 against Constellation's nuclear co-location deals and, with AEP, protested the Talen–Amazon Susquehanna agreement — the wires-only utility arguing that generators should not serve large loads behind the meter either. Generators call co-location the fastest path to supply; the utility calls it a cost shift.

**Self-check:** why does a company that earns nothing on supply campaign so hard about the capacity price? (Because its customers pay it, its regulators and legislators answer for it, and the only route to earning on generation again is a law written in response to it.)

## Module 5 — Who buys the batteries; five rulebooks

**The single idea:** in Exelon's states the storage is bought by a state agency, a solicitation or a developer — and the utility's one ask to own a battery is the test case.

1. **Illinois.** The 2026 Clean and Reliable Grid Affordability Act sets 3,000 MW of storage by 2030, procured by the Illinois Power Agency through Indexed Storage Credits — a payment that rises when market revenue falls, so the developer's total is stable. The first procurement (August 2026) awarded 600 MW of a 1,038 MW target, 80 MW of it in ComEd's zone. ComEd's role is a virtual power plant rider over about 1.8 GW of connected distributed resources; it owns nothing.
2. **Maryland.** The Next Generation Energy Act's 150 MW distribution-storage program gives BGE 29 MW (3 MW utility-owned) and Pepco Holdings 14.5 MW of third-party batteries; BGE and Pepco bid into a transmission-connected solicitation; an expedited-permitting round for dispatchable generation opens October 1, 2026 to third-party developers. The 2026 Utility RELIEF Act lowered the large-load threshold to 25 MW, banned forecast test years and ended a 0.5 percent transmission return adder.
3. **New Jersey.** Atlantic City Electric's July 2026 petition: a 500 MW / four-hour battery on ACE land at Pittsgrove, ACE-owned in rate base, developed, built and operated with Invenergy, about $1 billion, in service late 2030, tax credits and PJM revenues covering about 70 percent, a 9.6 percent return plus incentives, decision about February 2027. If approved, Invenergy — not the utility — selects the battery, as at We Energies' build-transfer projects.
4. **Pennsylvania, Delaware, DC.** Pennsylvania's regulator issued a voluntary model large-load tariff (over 50 MW; 'but for' cost allocation; deposits, minimum terms, exit provisions, self-build) that PECO has not yet filed under, after PECO withdrew a $510 million rate request the governor called 'pure greed' and lost its CEO. Delaware paused interconnections of 25 MW or more until Delmarva's large-load tariff exists. DC's court reversed Pepco's multiyear plan. The Mid-Atlantic carries about 2 GW of the 11 GW book; the rulebooks explain why.

**Self-check:** name the buyer of storage in each Exelon state today. (Illinois — the IPA via Indexed Storage Credits; Maryland — the state's solicitations with a 3 MW BGE exception; New Jersey — ACE, if the BPU approves Pittsgrove; Pennsylvania, Delaware, DC — no utility purchase on the record.)
