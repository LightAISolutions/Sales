# Florida Power & Light — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach how the corpus's largest utility-owned battery program and its first fully specified large-load tariff were built in one rate settlement — what it means for a utility to own its batteries in repeated blocks and buy them through a parent's supply chain; why a large-load tariff's term is set to a battery's life and how an incremental generation charge, an exit fee and credit-scaled security work; how a 500 kV zone with a class cap becomes a siting tool; what a settlement with a solar-and-battery adjustment and a rate stabilization mechanism buys for four years; how a state statute was written from a tariff; and why a $67 billion merger at the parent changes nothing at the subsidiary — starting from high-school STEM. No company trivia: executives, share counts and vote tallies stay in the dossier. Generated 2026-09-29 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1). Companion: the in-app guide (Profiler → Florida Power & Light → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The Duke plan taught the self-build storage buyer; the Pinnacle West plan taught the toll buyer; the Salt River Project plan taught public power; the TVA plan taught the federal case. This plan closes the ring: FPL is the self-builder at the largest scale, inside an investor-owned utility whose commission approved the storage, the tariff and the return in a single settlement, and whose parent is the merchant developer the corpus already covers as NextEra Energy Resources. ## Module 1 — The utility that owns its batteries

**The single idea:** FPL puts every battery in rate base, builds them in 74.5 MW blocks at its own solar sites, and buys the equipment through NextEra's enterprise supply chain — so the purchase order is inside the group and the supplier is never named.

1. **The fleet.** 991 MW at year-end 2025: the 409 MW / 900 MWh Manatee Energy Storage Center (2021, Quanta's Irby Construction as EPC), two 30 MW solar-charged plants and seven 74.5 MW batteries in northwest Florida energized in late 2025. The 2026 Ten-Year Site Plan adds 1,419.5 MW in 2026, 819.5 MW in 2027, 596 MW a year in 2028–30, 1,192 MW in 2031 and 7,454 MW over ten years — 8,445 MW by 2035, the largest utility-owned program in the corpus.
2. **The block.** FPL builds solar in 74.5 MW centers — 120 of them, about 8,826 MW, by August 2026 — and its batteries in 74.5 MW blocks co-located at the same farms (Big Brook, Cedar Trail, Sea Grape, Owen Branch). A "battery project" at FPL is a repeated design, not a bespoke 400 MW plant with its own EPC.
3. **Who buys.** Procurement runs through NextEra's engineering, construction and supply-chain organization, the same desk that buys for NextEra Energy Resources' merchant fleet. No OEM has been named for any FPL battery since Manatee, and the Ten-Year Site Plan gives nameplate MW only — no MWh, no duration.
4. **The hedge.** The same January 2026 order that set rates approved a $78 million Long-Duration Battery Storage Pilot (10 MW / 100 MWh, "beyond lithium-ion"), which the Commission said lets FPL "diversify its supply chain." A non-lithium vendor's door at FPL is that pilot.

**Self-check:** Duke names GE Vernova as its turbine supplier and its battery suppliers appear in rate filings; FPL's do not. Where would a seller find FPL's next battery decision? (Inside NextEra's enterprise supply chain — the same organization NEER's merchant procurement runs through — not in an FPSC docket.)

## Module 2 — A tariff whose term is the battery's life

**The single idea:** LLCS-1 and LLCS-2 make a large load pay for the generation built to serve it, for twenty years, with an exit fee and security sized to that charge.

1. **Eligibility.** Both schedules (effective January 1, 2026): a projected new or incremental load of 50 MW or more at a single location, a projected load factor of at least 85 percent, transmission-voltage service with dedicated metering, no aggregation, no resale. The threshold was raised from 25 MW because FPL's studies showed most large-load customers exceed 100 MW.
2. **The two schedules.** LLCS-1 exists only in three zones near FPL's 500 kV substations — Sunbreak (St. Lucie), Tesoro (Martin), Sugar (Palm Beach) — with a 3 GW class cap: $14.61 per kW of base demand plus an $11.67 per kW Incremental Generation Charge, sized on the 1 GW FPL expects to serve under it by end-2029 (filed at $28.07, cut to $12.18 on that basis and to $11.67 at the settlement's return). LLCS-2 covers the rest of the territory at $4.08 per kW plus a charge computed from the customer's share of the generation and interconnection revenue requirement — cost of service, not a fixed number.
3. **The term and the minimum.** Twenty years from the in-service date, "based on useful life of batteries," then evergreen with two years' notice. After the ramp the customer pays demand charges on at least 70 percent of contract demand (cut from 90 percent) and the Incremental Generation Charge on all of it.
4. **Exit and security.** Leaving early costs the net present value of the remaining Incremental Generation Charges — even after a force-majeure exit over a year. Within five days of signing the customer posts five years of that charge if rated BBB or better and ten years if not, by cash, letter of credit, surety bond or parent guaranty; a contribution in aid of construction is due within 45 days; FPL keeps sole discretion over what generation it builds.

**Self-check:** a customer rated BB signs LLCS-1 for 300 MW and leaves in year twelve. What does it owe and what has FPL already been holding? (The NPV of eight years of Incremental Generation Charges, drawn first from ten years of the charge posted as security.)

## Module 3 — The settlement that bought four years

**The single idea:** a rate settlement fixed FPL's return, funded the storage schedule and created the large-load class in one order, and its mechanisms carry the plan to 2029 without another case.

1. **The case and the settlement.** Filed February 28, 2025 ($1.545 billion for 2026, $927 million for 2027, an 11.9 percent return); settled August 20, 2025 with thirteen signatories — industrials, retailers, the data-center developers' association, Walmart, EV charging companies, the federal agencies, a clean-energy group — against five non-signatories led by the Office of Public Counsel; approved unanimously November 20, 2025; Final Order PSC-2026-0022-S-EI January 22, 2026: $945 million from 2026, $705 million from 2027 (FPL's own August release said $766 million — the order controls), a 10.95 percent return in a 9.95–11.95 band on 59.6 percent equity, through at least December 2029.
2. **SoBRA.** The Solar and Battery Base Rate Adjustment lets FPL add solar in 2027–29 and batteries in 2028–29 to rates on a showing of need, without a new case — the storage program's later tranches are funded by a filing.
3. **The RSM.** The Rate Stabilization Mechanism lets FPL amortize up to about $1.5 billion of reserves to keep its earned return inside the band — the regulatory-lag problem APS is trying to solve with a formula rate, solved here by a reserve. Storm costs recover from 60 days after a petition, capped at $5 per 1,000 kWh in the first year; the 2024 hurricanes' $1.2 billion was collected in 2025 and $80 million refunded in September 2026.
4. **The appeal.** The non-signatories' reconsideration was denied in April 2026; the Florida Supreme Court consolidated their appeals in June. Rates, SoBRA and the LLCS schedules stand while it runs; the 2028–29 battery tranches depend on a mechanism the appellants oppose.

**Self-check:** FPL wants 596 MW of batteries in rates in 2029. What must it do, and what could stop it? (File a SoBRA showing of economic or reliability need; a Supreme Court ruling against the settlement's mechanisms.)

## Module 4 — The statute and the zone

**The single idea:** Florida wrote FPL's tariff into law, and the tariff's cheaper schedule tells a data center where to build.

1. **Chapter 2026-65.** Senate Bill 484 (signed May 2026, effective July 1) creates s. 366.043: a large-load customer is 50 MW or more of anticipated monthly peak at a 15-minute interval at a single location, colocation tenants included; tariffs must recover full cost of service with no nonpayment risk on other customers; the Commission may approve CIAC, incremental generation charges, financial guarantees, minimum load factors, take-or-pay, minimum terms and early-termination fees; a utility may not knowingly serve a load controlled by a foreign country of concern; every public utility files a compliant tariff by October 1, 2026. Instrument by instrument, that is LLCS.
2. **The zone.** Extra-high-voltage substations are where a gigawatt can be delivered without a new line, and FPL's 500 kV backbone runs through St. Lucie, Martin and Palm Beach. Pricing those zones at the fixed $11.67 charge and capping them at 3 GW steers the first campuses to the corridor FPL can serve fastest — "as soon as 2028" — and keeps the rest of the state on a charge that recovers whatever must be built.
3. **The funnel.** Roughly 21 GW of interest; 12 GW in advanced discussions; an expectation raised from 6 to 8 GW by 2032; a Ten-Year Site Plan assumption of 3 GW by 2035; a 1 GW-by-2029 sizing basis for the charge; and, as of September 29, 2026, no signed customer announced. The developers' association told the Commission its members hold confidential agreements with FPL already and that LLCS charges are 65 percent above the ordinary large-load tariff.
4. **What the statute did to the market.** The largest proposed campus in an LLCS-1 zone withdrew its application in February 2026 as the bill advanced; the state paired the statute with land-use and water provisions. A seller should read the tariff and the statute as one document.

**Self-check:** a 300 MW campus at 90 percent load factor sites in Jacksonville. Which schedule, and why does its charge differ from Fort Pierce's? (LLCS-2, at a cost-of-service Incremental Generation Charge, because it is outside the three 500 kV zones that get the fixed $11.67.)

## Module 5 — The merger, the map and where it fails

**The single idea:** the NextEra–Dominion merger reorganizes the parent and leaves FPL's ownership, management and procurement untouched; FPL's plan fails on a missing customer, a missing supplier, an appeal and a credit test.

1. **The merger.** Signed May 15, 2026: 0.8138 NextEra shares plus a pro-rata share of $360 million in cash per Dominion share; about $66.8 billion by third-party accounts; both shareholder votes passed September 3, 2026; the Virginia commission's hearing is November 17, 2026; closing is expected in the second half of 2027. FPL stays wholly owned with the same president; Dominion becomes a sister subsidiary. The "130 GW of large-load opportunities" is the combined pipeline at the interest rung — 48.5 GW Dominion, 21 GW FPL, more than 60 GW NEER.
2. **Who buys what.** Batteries and solar: NextEra's enterprise supply chain, OEM undisclosed. Gas: FPL buys Vandolah (672 MW, June 2027) and plans 3,276 MW of new peakers in 2032–35 with no combined cycle and no named OEM. Nuclear: Turkey Point relicensed to 2052–53, St. Lucie pending, SMR early-site-permit work in 2026–27. Large loads: the customer funds studies, CIAC and security, and FPL picks the generation.
3. **The sister.** NextEra Energy Resources' merchant fleet, 110 GW storage pipeline and 30-hub data-center program are a separate book; a trade-press claim that Manatee is NEER-owned is contradicted by FPL's own filings. The corpus's NEER dossier is intelligence about the buyer's supply chain, not the account.
4. **The failure modes.** No signed LLCS customer nine months in, and the one large campus in a zone withdrew. No supplier named for the largest owned battery program in the corpus. One order under Supreme Court review carries the return, the mechanisms and the tariff. Solar added after 2027 keeps its federal credit only if construction began before July 5, 2026, and the plan does not say which centers pass — FPL's May 2026 bonds carry a "Tax Credit Event" redemption right for a reason.

**Self-check:** the merger closes in late 2027. What changes about who buys FPL's 2028 batteries? (Nothing — FPL remains a wholly owned subsidiary, its president stays, and NextEra's supply chain already buys for it.)
