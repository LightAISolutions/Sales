# Fluidstack — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach **the tenant-of-record structure** — how a company with about USD 2.6 billion of equity comes to sign roughly USD 27 billion of base-term rent across five US campuses, and why every party in that chain accepts it. The plan works through the six seats between the roof and the model (a former bitcoin miner as landlord, the landlord's bondholders, a bankruptcy-remote tenant subsidiary, the intermediary's parent, a guarantor and the frontier lab that actually uses the racks), the recognition agreement by which Google may "assume the Fluidstack Lease as the tenant", the backstop and the penny warrants that pay for it, the lockbox, the capped completion guarantee, the 180-day termination right, and the three megawatt figures — critical IT, gross, utility — that must never be added together. Fluidstack is the worked example because its landlords are public companies whose 8-Ks, 10-Qs and 10-Ks print the mechanism clause by clause (TeraWulf's Lake Mariner, Cipher's Barber Lake, Hut 8's River Bend, the Abernathy joint venture it now controls and Meridian Arc's New Lebanon), and because July 2026 — when the end user signed 401 MW at Hawesville directly — shows what the position is worth when the customer no longer needs it.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **TeraWulf guide** teaches the landlord's side of this same chain — brownfield power, gross against critical IT megawatts, how a thin-credit tenant signs a USD 6.7 billion lease, and reading a landlord's P&L. The **CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe and IREN guides** teach the neocloud as an operator: GPU-backed debt, take-or-pay, goodput, contracted against connected power, the service ladder, energy-first siting and the miner-to-AI pivot. The **OpenAI and xAI guides** teach the lab as a compute buyer and the own-or-rent decision. This plan sits in the seat none of them occupies — the intermediary tenant that owns neither the building nor, until July 2026, any campus, and whose product is a signature that a lender will accept — and it repeats none of their material: no GPU-cloud economics, no goodput, no siting, no landlord accounting beyond what the tenant's lease forces onto the landlord's page.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** The tenant is a shell by design, and the shell exists so that the guarantor's credit, not the tenant's, is what the landlord's lender prices. A backstop that starts at commencement leaves construction uninsured, so the chain's weak point is the building, not the tenant. And a position built on speed and borrowed credit is replaceable by the customer the day that customer can borrow its own.

**One boundary, held throughout.** Fluidstack is private and publishes no revenue, headcount or backlog; its funding is disclosed by its investors and its landlords, not by the company — USD 830 million of Series A in January 2026 at a USD 7.5 billion valuation is announced, the USD 1.5 billion round at USD 18 billion reported in September 2026 is not. Google has published nothing naming Fluidstack; every term of the backstop comes from the counterparties' filings and from law-firm client alerts (Quinn Emanuel) that read them. The plan works from those filings, the leases' summarised terms, the company's own site and press releases, and says so where the record stops — the exact warrant strike economics, the rent per critical MW, the cap on Google's aggregate exposure beyond the "more than USD 6 billion" the landlords disclose in pieces.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~35 min). Module 2 with TeraWulf's 10-Q lease note open (~45 min) — it is the one that pays. Module 3 (~30 min). Module 4 (~30 min). Module 5 (~25 min). Then flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — The chain: six seats between the roof and the model

**The single idea:** every seat holds a different risk, and the lease is written so that no seat holds the one it cannot price.

**The seats.** A landlord that already owns energised acreage — TeraWulf, Cipher, Hut 8, Meridian Arc — because a converted bitcoin site has the substation, the interconnection and the permit, which is the part that takes years. The landlord's lenders, who fund the shell and the fit-out from bond proceeds and price the lease, not the landlord. A tenant subsidiary — one per campus, bankruptcy-remote, with no assets but the lease and the sublease — whose only job is to be the counterparty. Fluidstack's parent, which arranges the chain, delivers the racks and the network inside the shell in months, and sells "gigawatts of compute in 6 months". A guarantor, Google, whose recognition agreement makes the tenant's rent bankable. And the end user, Anthropic, which subleases the capacity, pays the rent that flows up the chain, and whose credit was — until mid-2026 — not something a bond investor could underwrite alone.

**Why a shell.** If the operating company signed the lease, every campus would stand behind every other campus's default and the parent's own creditors. A single-purpose tenant isolates each lease; the lender then asks who stands behind that tenant, and the answer is the recognition agreement, not the parent's balance sheet.

**Self-check:** a lender is shown the tenant's audited accounts and finds a lease, a sublease and no cash. Is that a red flag? *(No — it is the design. The question is what stands behind the tenant, and that is the next module.)*

## Module 2 — How a backstop works: the recognition agreement, step by step

**The single idea:** the guarantor does not lend money; it promises to become the tenant.

**The mechanism.** The recognition agreement sits between landlord and guarantor. If the tenant subsidiary defaults, the landlord notifies the guarantor; the guarantor "may assume the Fluidstack Lease as the tenant" or otherwise fund the rent, up to a stated cap per campus. TeraWulf's 10-K calls that support "a key factor in enabling efficient financing"; Cipher carries the guarantee as a USD 544.5 million asset on its own books, which is the clearest statement of what it is worth to a landlord. The backstop "becomes effective as of the commencement date" — the day the halls are delivered — not the day the lease is signed.

**What it costs.** Penny warrants in two landlords: Google receives shares in TeraWulf and Cipher for a nominal strike, so the guarantor's consideration is equity in the landlord, with a top-up clause at Cipher if the shares fall. A lockbox account through which rent passes, so the guarantor's exposure is measured against cash it can see. And a completion guarantee of at most USD 100 million from Fluidstack's side for construction, which is the number to hold against Module 4.

**What the lender sees.** The landlord's bonds are secured on the campus and priced against a lease whose rent is guaranteed, from commencement, by an investment-grade name. Neither the tenant's nor the end user's credit is in the coupon.

**Self-check:** the landlord defaults on its own bonds while the tenant is paying rent. Does the recognition agreement fire? *(No — it protects the landlord against the tenant, not the lender against the landlord. The lender's protection is its mortgage on the campus and the rent that keeps flowing to a new owner.)*

## Module 3 — Reading the lease: three megawatts, two rent structures, one escalator

**The single idea:** the rent line hides three numbers that must never be added, and a rent structure that decides who pays for the power.

**Three megawatts.** Critical IT load is what the racks may draw — the figure the lease prices (378 MW at Lake Mariner, 168 at Abernathy, 207 at Barber Lake, 245 at River Bend, 430 at New Lebanon; about 1.4 GW contracted). Gross capacity adds cooling, losses and house load and is what the halls are built for. Utility capacity is what the interconnection agreement allows at the fence and is what the landlord actually owns. A landlord announcing "1 GW" and a tenant signing "378 MW" may be describing the same building.

**Modified gross against triple net.** Under a modified gross lease the rent includes some operating costs and the landlord eats variance in the rest; under triple net every tax, insurance and operating cost passes through to the tenant. In this chain the power bill is the largest of them and its treatment — pass-through at cost, or bundled at a fixed rate — decides whether a hot summer in Texas is the landlord's problem or the model's. Read the pass-through clause before the rent figure.

**The escalator and the term.** Base terms of ten years with a fixed annual rent escalator and tenant extension options; about USD 27 billion of base-term rent across the five leases. Multiply annual rent by term and add the escalator before comparing two campuses — a lower first-year rent with a higher escalator can be the dearer lease.

**Self-check:** two campuses are quoted at the same rent per critical MW, one modified gross and one triple net. Which is cheaper for the tenant? *(Unknown until the pass-throughs are priced — the modified gross rent already contains costs the triple-net tenant will pay on top.)*

## Module 4 — Construction-period risk: who eats a delay

**The single idea:** the guarantor's clock starts at commencement; the lender's clock started at the bond closing.

**The gap.** Between bond closing and commencement the landlord's bondholders have financed steel, transformers and switchgear against a lease whose guarantee has not yet switched on. Fluidstack's completion guarantee is capped at USD 100 million; the landlord's own equity and the bond covenants cover the rest. Quinn Emanuel's reading is the reference: a delay beyond 180 days lets the tenant terminate "and Google's backstop obligation would never trigger" — the one clause that can make the whole chain evaporate without anyone defaulting.

**The schedule has moved.** CB-5 at Lake Mariner from "2H 2026" to "early 2027"; Abernathy from "2H 2026" to "Q4 2026"; Barber Lake reported on schedule. Every slip is a month in which the bondholder is unsecured by the guarantor and the end user is short of capacity it planned revenue on. Transformers, switchgear and chillers — not chips — are the choke point on these builds.

**Three seats, three losses.** The landlord loses rent and covenant headroom; the tenant loses nothing but the sublease margin and, past 180 days, gains an exit; the end user loses the capacity. The guarantor loses only the option to have been paid.

**Self-check:** a campus commences 150 days late. Who has been carrying the risk for those 150 days? *(The landlord's bondholders, against a USD 100 million cap and the landlord's equity — the guarantor's obligation has not begun.)*

## Module 5 — Where the intermediary stands, and how it is bought

**The single idea:** the product is credit substitution plus speed, and both are replaceable.

**What is being sold.** To the landlord: a tenant with a guarantor, which converts a miner's stranded power into a bond-financeable lease in months. To the end user: capacity years sooner than a hyperscaler's queue, without signing a lease in its own name. To the guarantor: a distribution channel — the same chain that leases the halls delivers the chips, and Google's warrants, backstops and reported equity talks with the tenant make it a channel as much as a credit.

**How the chain is bought.** The end user picks the intermediary for delivery date and for the guarantor it brings; the landlord signs for the guarantee; the lender lends on it. Nobody in the chain is bought on price per megawatt alone, and every clause above is a negotiation between counterparties that never meet: the end user does not sign the recognition agreement, the guarantor does not sign the sublease.

**Routing around it.** In July 2026 Anthropic signed 401 MW at Hawesville with TeraWulf directly, "supported by an investment-grade credit", with no Fluidstack entity in the lease, and has since contracted with Nscale, Lambda, Riot and Hut 8 in other structures. The intermediary's answer is to become an owner — the Abernathy purchase for about USD 530 million, Okmulgee, Sullivan County and Wythe County in its own name — which is a different business with a different balance-sheet question, and the one to watch.

**Self-check:** the end user's own paper becomes investment grade. What happens to the intermediary's margin on the next campus? *(It shrinks to the value of speed alone — the credit half of the product is now something the customer can supply itself.)*
