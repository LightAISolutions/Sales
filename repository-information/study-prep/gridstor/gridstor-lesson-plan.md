# GridStor — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach **the storage specialist a bank built** — how an asset manager's infrastructure fund deploys limited partners' money into standalone batteries by creating its own developer; why a fund-owned specialist buys late-stage projects rather than originating; how a battery bought at notice-to-proceed is contracted before it is built (resource adequacy, tolling, a revenue swap); what a sponsor's name lets a young company borrow that a founder's cannot; and where the model fails.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **Aypa plan** is the contracted platform priced by a buyer; the **Spearmint plan** the merchant developer financing a battery with no PPA; the **Invenergy plan** the diversified developer and its institutional owners; the **Available Power plan** the seller of the late-stage projects GridStor buys. The **Classroom** owns the hour-by-hour revenue of a battery. None of that is repeated.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** A fund is a clock with money in it — it must invest, show returns and exit, and every GridStor choice follows from that clock. Buying late converts sponsor money into operating batteries in two years, at the price of paying away the originator's margin. And a sponsor's name is collateral: a repeat lender, a corporate letter-of-credit facility and early credit transfers arrived years before the company had a track record.

**One boundary, held throughout.** The premise this session was given — a CAISO/ERCOT merchant specialist sponsored by GSAM's Infrastructure business — was wrong on both counts and imprecise on the third. Every project is contracted (SCE resource adequacy, APS and Fortune 500 tolls, an Axpo swap), the markets are California, Texas, Arizona, Oklahoma and Colorado, and the sponsor is 'a fund managed by Goldman Sachs Asset Management' that trade press names as Horizon Energy Storage. No fund commitment, ownership split, EPC, or supplier beyond Tesla at Goleta is public.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 with the Latitude Media origin article open (~30 min). Module 2 with the five acquisition releases open (~35 min). Module 3 with the Axpo and White Tank releases open (~30 min). Module 4 with the NORD/LB releases open (~30 min). Module 5 (~20 min). Then flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — How an asset manager's fund owns a developer

**The single idea:** the owners are the fund's limited partners; the decision-maker is the fund's committee; the company is a vehicle the fund created. Goldman bought Goleta, hired Chris Taylor, and built the team around the project. The board has two GSAM investors and a retired Goldman partner.

**What a fund wants:** contracted, financed, operating assets it can hold and sell — not options. Hence 'focused on contracted revenues'.

**Self-check:** the media kit says GSAM 'has raised over $1 billion' for storage. Is that GridStor's capital? *(No — it is the strategy's fundraising; no commitment to this company is published, and the CEO says only 'a fraction' is deployed.)*

## Module 2 — Buying late

**The single idea:** five purchases in eighteen months, each from an originator who stopped at or near ready-to-build — Balanced Rock Power twice, Black Mountain, Capacity Power Group, Strata (with its APS toll attached), Accelergen. The buyer's job is contract, EPC, supply, finance, build; the team was hired for exactly those steps.

**The pipeline number.** 'Over 5 GW' became 'over 3 GW' in two months. A buyer's pipeline is a shopping list.

**Self-check:** Hidden Lakes was bought as 'up to 450 MW / 900 MWh' and built at 220 MW / 440 MWh. What does that tell a seller? *(That the headline size at acquisition is an option, and the buyer re-scopes to what it can contract and finance.)*

## Module 3 — Contracting before building

**The single idea:** three structures, three allocations of market risk. A resource-adequacy contract pays for availability and leaves the owner dispatching (Goleta, 17 years with SCE). A toll hands dispatch to the counterparty for a fixed fee (White Tank, 20 years with APS; Gunnar with a Fortune 500 company). A revenue swap exchanges merchant revenue for fixed payments on a share (Hidden Lakes, 100 MW with Axpo) and leaves the rest merchant.

**Self-check:** which of the three would a lender prefer, and why is the swap the newest? *(The toll — the counterparty's credit replaces the market; the swap exists because ERCOT has no capacity payment and the owner wanted the battery financeable after it was already merchant.)*

## Module 4 — What a sponsor can finance that a founder cannot

**The single idea:** the sponsor's name is collateral. NORD/LB in four of five disclosed transactions; a USD 50 million development letter-of-credit facility; credit transfers to J.P. Morgan (one of the first for a battery, at 'ninety something cents') and Foss & Company (USD 86 million); tax-equity bridge and construction loans from lenders doing their first storage deals with the company. None of it is guaranteed by the sponsor; all of it was priced on the sponsor's ownership.

**Self-check:** why is one repeat lender both the strength and the risk? *(Each deal is priced on the last, so it is cheap and fast; a change in that bank's view takes the pipeline's financing with it.)*

## Module 5 — Where it fails

**The single idea:** the clock and the contract disagree. A ten-to-twelve-year fund owns twenty-year tolls, so the assets will be sold before the contracts end at a price the sponsor does not control. Add: an undisclosed commitment, a single lender, a pipeline that shrinks, procurement paused pending Treasury's FEOC guidance, a Los Angeles portfolio that produced one named project, and a first Texas build that was halved and late.

**Self-check:** a supplier calls GridStor in 2026. What does the CTO's February statement mean for the call? *(That longer-dated purchases are on hold until the FEOC rule is settled; the counterpart exists — CTO, SVP EPC, Director of Procurement — but the order does not yet.)*
