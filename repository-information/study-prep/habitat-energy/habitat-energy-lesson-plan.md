# Habitat Energy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Habitat Energy (private, Quinbrook-owned; third-party battery and renewables optimiser in Great Britain, ERCOT and Australia's NEM; under a reported sale process) · **Written:** 2026-09-12 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no power-markets background.

**What this teaches.** What a grid battery is actually paid for and why the money keeps moving between the parts of the revenue stack; why the timing decision is a forecasting problem with a memory (state of charge) and a cost (degradation); how three market designs — Great Britain, Texas and Australia — reach the same trading desk through three different registrations; the four contract shapes between an owner and an optimiser and who carries the price risk under each; how to read an optimiser's scorecard; what a market redesign (ERCOT's RTC+B) does to every optimiser's software; and the industry map of utility desks, vendor bidders, pure-plays, owner-traders and tollers.

**Why this gap.** The corpus's software-and-optimization guides teach the site controller and asset-performance software (FlexGen, Stem), the vendor's bidder sold with its hardware (Fluence, Tesla) and the owner who trades for itself (Hunt Energy Network). None teaches the pure-play third-party optimiser — the company that owns nothing, holds the registrations and sells the instruction on revenue share — which is the seat the segment's landscape was held for. Habitat is the only such company with an independent ranking, so it is the right vehicle.

**Its place in the segment set.** Between the Hunt Energy Network guide (the owner who trades) and the Stem guide (the software the owner runs). It owns the route-to-market layer: registrations, forecasting, the desk, and the floors and swaps an optimiser adds when tolling threatens its seat. The EMS, the cells and the inverters are held for their own guides; Gridmatic's guide covers the fund-backed variant of the same seat.

## Module 1 — What a battery is paid for
Energy arbitrage against the duck curve; ancillary services and why every fast service saturates; capacity and balancing payments; the revenue stack as a moving target.

## Module 2 — The forecast problem
Why a wrong timing bet costs twice; state of charge as the constraint that links intervals; degradation cost as the price of a cycle; why a probabilistic forecast beats a point forecast; the company's own (untested) accuracy claims as an example of how such claims are phrased.

## Module 3 — Three markets, one desk
Great Britain: the Virtual Lead Party, the Balancing Mechanism, Dynamic Containment and the 2023 saturation. ERCOT: the qualified scheduling entity, day-ahead and real-time energy, reserves, and five-minute settlement. The NEM: five-minute energy and eight FCAS markets, Causer Pays. What all three have in common.

## Module 4 — Route to market
Why an owner rents registrations and a desk; the roughly two-gigawatt in-house threshold; the registration as the moat; the alternatives — utility desks, vendor bidders, licences, owner-traders — and the pure-play pitch.

## Module 5 — How the optimiser is paid
Revenue share, floor agreement, tolling agreement and software licence compared on who carries the price risk and who needs capital; why the tolling wave turns a fee business into one with a book, collateral and a bank charge.

## Module 6 — Reading the scorecard
TBx capture rate, percentage of perfect, megawatts optimised, awards and audited fees: what each proves and what it hides; why one month on a small fleet is mostly noise, including when it flatters the company.

## Module 7 — When the rules change
ERCOT's real-time co-optimisation as the worked case: what changed, why every bidder was rebuilt, and why a desk with people adapts faster than a licence run alone.

## Module 8 — The industry map
Who buys (owners below the threshold, retailers holding tolls); who competes from four directions; why a sale of the leading independent is a segment event rather than a company event.

## Pacing
Eight modules of roughly 30 minutes. Modules 1–2 are the physics and economics core and should be read together. Module 3's table is the one to keep open in any conversation about a specific market. Modules 5–6 are the commercial core and pair with the Gridmatic guide's offtake menu. Modules 7–8 read in one sitting.
