# Key Capture Energy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Key Capture Energy (SK-owned storage developer; NYISO first mover with an ERCOT and MISO fleet) · **Written:** 2026-09-06 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no regulatory background.

**What this teaches.** How storage is bought where the buyer is a regulated body rather than a market. The resource-plan-to-approval sequence and the prudence-review discipline that shapes every preference inside it; the five structures a public buyer can use; why location inside a constrained system can matter more than size; what state programmes actually do to project economics; and the particular cost — and durable prize — of being the first battery a market has ever processed.

**Why this gap.** The merchant picture describes one part of the United States and most of it badly. Most of the country's storage is bought by a utility, a public power authority or a state programme, through a documented process that must survive a hearing. That process shapes the product, and nothing in the corpus explained it.

**Its place in the buyer set.** Fifth of five, and the counterweight to the others. Apex Clean Energy covers the project lifecycle, Arevon the contract, Eolian the revenue stack, Jupiter Power the dispatch decision, and this one the regulated purchase. Chemistry stays with REPT and Gotion.

## Module 1 — The regulated purchase
Resource plan, solicitation, contract, approval. Why the need is established and litigated in public before anything is bought, and why scoring weighs deliverability, interconnection status, credit and safety record alongside price. Commission approval as the step with no merchant equivalent. Public power's parallel path under its own boards and procurement law.

## Module 2 — Prudence review
The single discipline that explains most regulated behaviour: a decision can be examined years later against what was known at the time, with cost recovery at stake. Why that systematically favours proven equipment, durable suppliers, risk transferred to the developer, and a paper trail — and why a bid that cannot document compliance loses regardless of price.

## Module 3 — Five structures
Utility-owned and rate-based; long-term power purchase or tolling; build-own-operate-transfer; energy storage service agreement; state-programme support. Who owns, who operates, what the buyer pays, where the risk lands. Why the choice is regulatory politics as much as economics: rate-basing grows the utility's earning asset base and is contested for exactly that reason.

## Module 4 — Zones and locational value
Systems that are one pool against systems that are a set of pockets joined by limited transmission. New York as the clearest example, and the installed-capacity market's locational requirements: a megawatt inside a constraint is not substitutable for one outside it. New England's version of the same problem plus winter fuel-security constraints. Why brownfield industrial land near a constrained urban substation is the classic solution — and carries the strictest review.

## Module 5 — Non-wires alternatives
Storage sited to relieve a local constraint so a line or substation can be deferred: the utility buys a service instead of steel, and the battery is paid for a transmission function. A small category that produces some of the most durable contracts in the market, because the need is specific, local and physical.

## Module 6 — What a state incentive actually does
Three shapes and their different consequences. A capital grant flatters the stack but leaves revenue uncertain, so it helps only projects that were nearly financeable. An index-style credit pays the difference to a bid strike price and claws back the excess — a contract for difference in substance, which removes merchant risk and therefore finances far more cheaply, and which makes a bid an implicit forecast of the bidder's own market revenue. A procurement mandate converts a target into ordinary utility contracts. Plus the conditions state support attaches: content preferences, labour standards, community terms, reporting.

## Module 7 — Being first
What a market lacks before its first storage project: a participation model for a resource that both consumes and produces, metering and settlement treatment, an accreditation method, interconnection study assumptions, a commissioning template. What the first mover pays in schedule, and what it keeps afterwards — procedural knowledge, relationships with the people who write the rules, a scored jurisdictional track record, and positions taken before the market was crowded. Why that advantage is jurisdiction-specific and must be re-earned in each new market.

## Pacing
Seven modules, roughly 35 minutes each. Modules 1–2 belong together and are the conceptual core. Module 4 pairs well with Eolian's five-market module, which approaches locational value from the merchant side. Module 7 is the most transferable: the same argument applies to any regulated market a company enters for the first time.
