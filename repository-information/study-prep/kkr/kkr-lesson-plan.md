# KKR — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** KKR & Co. Inc. (NYSE: KKR), with Global Atlantic and Helix Digital Infrastructure · **Written:** 2026-09-26 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1) · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no finance background.

**Purpose:** teach how a capital firm built around power becomes, or fails to become, a buyer of power equipment:
- how an insurer's balance sheet funds infrastructure, and why that capital is patient;
- the difference between core infrastructure and building new, and why an open-ended vehicle exists;
- what 50:50 co-ownership of a data-centre landlord means for decisions and for exits;
- why an investor takes 19.9% of a transmission company it will never run;
- how a data-centre vehicle can be designed to buy power from one of its own investors;
- buying a data-centre platform abroad with a strategic partner — and the DC-power testbed it inherited;
- what an independent power producer buys, and the two battery orders at KKR platforms in 2026;
- how an exit to a strategic buyer recycles capital.

No company trivia: founding dates, executives and fund sizes stay in the dossier. The in-app guide (Profiler → KKR → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **BlackRock** plan, written the same day, teaches index ownership, consortium control and utility take-privates from the other co-owner of CyrusOne. The **Blackstone** plan teaches the manager's own profit and loss; the **Vistra** and **AEP** dossiers carry the two utility-side counterparties here.

**Suggested pacing (before 7 October 2026):** Module 1 (~15 min), Module 2 (~10 min), Module 3 (~10 min), Module 4 (~15 min), Module 5 (~15 min — the most unusual structure), Module 6 (~15 min — the one with a DC-power programme), Module 7 (~15 min), Module 8 (~10 min), then the flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — An asset manager that owns an insurer

**The single idea:** an insurer collects money now and pays it back over decades, so it needs investments that pay steadily over decades — which is what infrastructure does.

1. **What Global Atlantic sells.** Retirement and life insurance, chiefly **annuities**: a saver pays a sum now and the insurer guarantees payments or a return later. Global Atlantic held USD 220bn of assets at 30 June 2026 and has been wholly owned by KKR since January 2024.
2. **How it earns.** It invests the premiums in its **general account** and keeps the spread between what the investments yield and what it promised savers — USD 1.1bn of insurance operating earnings in FY2025.
3. **Why it matches infrastructure.** A promise due in twenty years is best backed by an asset that pays for twenty years — a transmission line, a contracted solar farm, a leased data centre. Global Atlantic's accounts bought 50% stakes in TotalEnergies' operating solar and wind portfolios and help fund KKR's partnership with Energy Capital Partners.
4. **The criticism.** Trade press argues that insurer-backed managers are more leveraged than they look, and a June 2026 account said KKR's shares had lost more than a third of their value over the year amid private-credit worries. The model works only while the investments pay as expected.
5. **What it means for a seller.** Insurance money buys finished, income-producing assets. It rarely buys equipment directly — it finances the owners who do.

**Self-check:** why would an annuity writer prefer a stabilised data centre on a 15-year lease to a new battery project in a wholesale market? *(The lease pays a predictable amount for years, matching the insurer's fixed promises; a merchant battery's income varies with prices.)*

## Module 2 — Core infrastructure and the open-ended vehicle

**The single idea:** 'core' means buying finished assets for steady income at lower returns, and an open-ended fund can hold them indefinitely instead of selling after ten years.

1. **Two kinds of fund.** A closed-end fund raises money once, invests it over about five years and returns it by about year ten. KKR's Global Infrastructure Investors V closed at USD 19.2bn in August 2026 on this model.
2. **The open-ended alternative.** An **open-ended fund** keeps accepting money and has no end date. KKR's Diversified Core Infrastructure Fund, with USD 14.9bn of commitments, holds long-lived assets such as its AEP transmission stake.
3. **Core versus value-add.** **Core infrastructure** is operating and contracted or regulated. Value-add and development build or expand. Returns rise with risk: core funds accept less in exchange for predictability.
4. **Why a new vehicle for AI.** KKR's CFO explained in July 2026 that its infrastructure funds 'carry diversification guidelines that limit how much we could dedicate to a single theme'. A diversified fund cannot put too much into data centres, so KKR created Helix Digital Infrastructure as a separate, perpetual vehicle for that one theme.

**Self-check:** a closed-end fund bought a data-centre platform in its fourth year. Roughly when will it want to sell? *(Within about six years, before the fund's ten-year life ends — unless it moves the asset into a continuation or open-ended vehicle.)*

## Module 3 — Fifty-fifty: co-owning a landlord

**The single idea:** two sponsors with equal votes must agree on every major decision, which protects each from the other but makes the platform slower to change course and harder to sell.

1. **CyrusOne's owners.** KKR and GIP (now part of BlackRock) took CyrusOne private in March 2022 for about USD 15bn including debt. The split is widely reported as 50:50; no first-party document read for the dossier states it. The European Commission cleared the deal as **joint control**.
2. **How co-control works.** A shareholders' agreement lists reserved matters — budgets, large contracts, new debt, a sale — that need both owners' consent. Neither can impose a strategy alone.
3. **What it looks like in public.** Both sponsors' partners were quoted together when CyrusOne named a new chief executive in July 2026, and both appear in its financing documents.
4. **How it ends.** Co-owners must agree on an exit. Reuters reported in August 2026 that the owners are preparing an IPO as early as 2027 — a route that lets both sell gradually without one buying out the other.
5. **What it means for a seller.** A large equipment frame at CyrusOne may need sign-off from two investment committees, not one.

**Self-check:** what does 'joint control' let each co-owner do that a 25% minority holder cannot? *(Veto the reserved matters — major budgets, contracts, debt and a sale.)*

## Module 4 — 19.9% of a transmission company

**The single idea:** a utility can sell a minority stake in its transmission subsidiary to raise equity while keeping full control of the wires, and an investor accepts that because the regulated return is predictable.

1. **The deal.** KKR and PSP Investments, 50/50 partners, paid USD 2.82bn for 19.9% of AEP's Ohio and Indiana Michigan **transmission companies**, completed on 5 June 2025. AEP 'will continue to operate and maintain these transmission facilities'.
2. **How a transmission company earns.** It owns high-voltage lines and substations and recovers their cost plus an allowed return through a federal formula rate. More investment means a bigger rate base and more earnings.
3. **Why AEP sold.** AEP needed equity to fund a large capital plan without issuing as many new shares; the sale was meant to offset about USD 5.35bn of equity need through 2029.
4. **Why a minority stake.** The buyer gets steady returns without operating risk; the utility keeps operation and planning, and at 19.9% the investors stay a clear minority. The deal still needed FERC and CFIUS approval.
5. **What it means for a seller.** The transmission company's purchases — transformers, breakers, lines — are still AEP's decisions. KKR's money pays for them without deciding them.

**Self-check:** KKR owns 19.9% of a transmission company that is buying 50 large transformers. Who picks the supplier? *(AEP, which operates the company; the minority investor funds but does not decide.)*

## Module 5 — A data-centre vehicle that buys power from its investor

**The single idea:** Helix Digital Infrastructure is built so that a power company invests in it and then sells it power, and so that investors get the first look at supplying its projects.

1. **What Helix is.** Launched by KKR on 11 June 2026 with more than USD 10bn of long-duration commitments to build hyperscale data centres together with 'baseload and flexible power generation; transmission and distribution infrastructure; fiber'. NVIDIA, the Kuwait Investment Authority and Vistra are among the founding investors.
2. **The power-for-capital loop.** Vistra committed up to USD 1.0bn: USD 500m up front and USD 500m more once Helix meets 'commercial power supply milestones for existing generation assets', or at Vistra's election. Vistra is Helix's 'preferred power partner', so part of Vistra's investment is tied to Helix buying Vistra's electricity.
3. **First-look rights.** KKR's release says founding investors 'may have certain rights, such as priority or first look rights, to provide goods or services to Helix investments'. A first-look right means the investor sees an opportunity to supply before anyone else and can match or take it.
4. **Why power sits inside the vehicle.** KKR's own research argues that 'the ultimate limiting factor for building data centers isn't capital; it's power'. A vehicle that owns generation and grid assets next to its data centres controls the scarce input.
5. **What is still unknown.** No project, site, ownership split or supplier had been disclosed by 26 September 2026.

**Self-check:** you sell equipment to a Helix project. Which two advantages might an investor-supplier have over you? *(A first look at the opportunity, and an interest in the project's success as an owner.)*

## Module 6 — Buying a data-centre platform abroad, and a DC-power testbed

**The single idea:** KKR bought control of STTGDC with a local strategic partner, and the platform came with a live high-voltage DC testbed — the nearest thing to a solid-state-transformer buyer in KKR's portfolio.

1. **The deal.** KKR and Singtel agreed in February 2026 to buy the roughly 82% of STT GDC they did not own from Temasek's STT Communications for S$6.6bn in cash, at about S$13.8bn of enterprise value, completing on 2 September 2026. KKR holds 75% and Singtel 25%.
2. **Enterprise value versus price.** **Enterprise value** is the whole company including debt; the S$6.6bn buys the equity not already owned. Half of it is **deferred consideration**, payable on the first anniversary or on an earlier change of control.
3. **Why a strategic partner.** Singtel is a local telecoms group with its own data centres; it keeps a stake and equity-accounts it. A foreign investor buying critical digital infrastructure in Singapore gains from a domestic partner.
4. **The HVDC testbed.** In January 2026 STT GDC launched what it calls 'Southeast Asia's first live HVDC-powered AI infrastructure testbed', built with LITEON and supported by the SST start-up Amperesand, validating at least 325 kW, and said it 'plans to deploy this technology in future data centres in Singapore'. Distributing power as DC at high voltage removes conversion stages between the grid and the chips; a **solid-state transformer** does the medium-voltage-to-DC step electronically.
5. **What it means for a seller.** STTGDC is the one KKR platform with a DC-power programme on the record, and its pilot already includes an SST vendor. The door is STTGDC's engineering team; the question is whether the testbed becomes a purchase.

**Self-check:** why does removing AC-DC conversion stages matter at 780 MW of operating capacity? *(Each stage loses a few per cent of power as heat; across hundreds of megawatts that is megawatts of load and cooling saved.)*

## Module 7 — What an independent power producer buys

**The single idea:** an independent power producer builds and owns generation and storage, and signs the equipment contracts itself — which is why KKR's pending purchase of one matters more to a seller than its minority stakes.

1. **The pending deal.** EDF agreed on 30 June 2026 to sell EDF power solutions' US and Canadian business to KKR for about USD 4.2bn, plus up to USD 0.39bn. It reports 5.6 GW of net renewable capacity. FERC must approve under **Section 203**: the application (Docket EC26-151) was filed on 12 August with comments due 13 October 2026.
2. **What comes with it.** EDF's unit signed a framework in May 2026 with Ford Energy for up to 20 GWh of DC-block battery systems from 2028. A framework sets prices and terms for future orders without obliging each order.
3. **An IPP KKR already owns.** ContourGlobal, taken private by KKR funds in December 2022, signed on 10 August 2026 for 3 GWh of CATL battery systems for the UK, Greece and Chile: 526 containers of 5.64 MWh, each able to discharge for four hours.
4. **A developer KKR controls.** Avantus, majority-owned by KKR funds since 2024, bought a 200 MW / 800 MWh Fluence system with turnkey construction for its Rexford 2 project in California (July 2026).
5. **Container arithmetic.** 526 × 5.64 MWh ≈ 2,967 MWh ≈ 3 GWh. At four hours, 3 GWh delivers about 740 MW of power. Energy (MWh) and power (MW) are different specifications; a buyer states both.

**Self-check:** a 200 MW battery holds 800 MWh. How many hours can it discharge at full power? *(Four: 800 MWh ÷ 200 MW.)*

## Module 8 — Exits and recycling capital

**The single idea:** a fund makes its return when it sells, and a strategic buyer — a company in the same business — can pay more than a financial buyer because it gains synergies.

1. **CoolIT.** KKR's Global Impact Fund II bought CoolIT, a liquid-cooling maker, in 2023 and sold it to Ecolab for about USD 4.75bn in cash, closing on 2 July 2026 — about 15 times its cost per KKR. Ecolab, a water and hygiene company, was the strategic buyer.
2. **USI.** KKR and other shareholders agreed on 31 August 2026 to sell the insurance broker USI to Aon for USD 17.0bn; closing is expected in Q4 2026. KKR expects about USD 3.3bn of after-tax proceeds.
3. **Where the money goes.** Proceeds return to the fund's investors and to KKR's balance sheet, and feed the next commitments — Helix and the infrastructure funds among them.
4. **Signed is not closed.** USI, EDF power solutions and the Sempra Infrastructure Partners stake were all signed but not closed on 26 September 2026. A dossier records each as announced until closing is confirmed.

**Self-check:** why might a cooling-equipment maker be worth more to Ecolab than to another fund? *(Ecolab can sell cooling alongside its water and chemicals business and cut shared costs — synergies a financial buyer lacks.)*

## Where it fails — the risks in one list

- Helix's first-look rights and preferred-power arrangement could close doors to outside suppliers on its projects.
- Co-control at CyrusOne means two committees must agree; an IPO could change the owners again.
- Pending deals (EDF power solutions, Sempra Infrastructure Partners, USI) can be conditioned or delayed by regulators.
- Private-credit and insurance-leverage worries can slow fundraising and, with it, new platforms.
