# Lightsource bp — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Lightsource bp (a solar and storage developer operating across several continents under different support regimes) · **Written:** 2026-09-07 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, plus the first buyer-side course in this corpus; no international energy-policy or project-finance background.

**What this teaches.** What is set by the country rather than by the technology. Six ways a state can pay for a megawatt-hour — two-way contract for difference, fixed feed-in tariff, feed-in premium, green certificate under a quota, corporate PPA, and merchant — read as a matrix of what each fixes, what it leaves floating and what behaviour that combination actually produces; then the financing consequence read backwards, which is that policy design rather than engineering sets a large part of what a country's electricity costs. How a national auction differs from a regulated utility's solicitation: price rather than proposal, prequalification paid for before the result is known, bid security that converts a win into an obligation, indexation frequently worth more than the headline price, and the winner's curse as a property of the format rather than a mistake by a bidder. The currency problem, which is asymmetric in time because equipment is bought once in a hard currency and revenue arrives for twenty years in a local one, and which cannot be hedged at the tenor that matters. What a network rulebook can decide: connection allocation regimes, whether curtailment is compensated, per-connection loss factors that move revenue administratively, grid-code duties including system-strength remediation, and whether foreign test evidence is recognised at all. Land tenure and consent regimes, with the finding that consent risk is about variance rather than difficulty. What a developer can carry across a border and what it has to rebuild. And what a multi-country portfolio is genuinely diversified against — effective against politics, weak against finance and supply chains, useless against a design fault.

**Why this gap.** The first buyer-side course compares five US wholesale markets, and that comparison is real but bounded: it varies the revenue mechanism inside one legal system, one currency and one set of institutions. The corpus contains no guide in which the law, the money, the permitting authority, the land tenure and the compliance regime all change at once — and it holds no other international project developer, so there is no company through which the question has ever been asked.

**Its place in the portfolio-owner set.** Third of four. It takes the reference design from the Plus Power guide and asks what happens to it at a border, and it takes the supply-chain and capital arguments from the NextEra Energy Resources guide and identifies which of them are global shocks rather than diversifiable ones. It hands the technology-risk thread on to the Terra-Gen guide, which closes the course.

## Module 1 — Six ways a state pays
The instrument matrix: what each fixes, what it leaves floating, what it therefore rewards. Why a fixed tariff produces deployment and no system responsiveness, why a premium preserves the signal, and why a scheme with two floating prices is the hardest of all to finance. The reverse reading: a country lowers its energy cost by changing its instrument, without anyone building a better plant.

## Module 2 — The auction as an institution
What is removed when there is no utility in the middle and no prudence review, and what replaces it. Prequalification as risk moved earlier and spend committed before the outcome is known. Bid security and delivery penalties as what suppresses speculative bidding and makes auction results a firmer indicator than any pipeline figure. Indexation as the term that decides real value, and the standard error of comparing awarded prices across countries without it. The winner's curse, why it is structural, and why undersubscription is the opposite failure of the same design problem.

## Module 3 — A project's money is not one money
Three currencies in one project, and the asymmetry in time that makes the exposure hard: equipment bought once, revenue arriving for two decades. Why liquid forward cover does not reach the tenor that matters, so the tail is priced rather than hedged. The three ways it is priced — required return, hard-currency indexation, or local-currency borrowing at a higher nominal rate — and why the third is the honest one and the least used. Indexation as a negotiation about who is the cheaper bearer of risk. The feedback into equipment choice, which looks irrational until the currency column is on the page.

## Module 4 — What a network can decide
Connection allocation: first-come, first-ready, auctioned capacity, or operator plan — and whether an early position is therefore a tradeable asset or a place in a line. Curtailment compensation across its full range, and why uncompensated curtailment at a constrained node appears in no price forecast. Per-connection loss factors, recalculated administratively, as revenue risk with no physical cause. Grid-code duties and system-strength remediation charged to the connecting party. And why a certified product is not a permitted one where foreign test reports are not recognised.

## Module 5 — Land and consent
Tenure forms and what each supports, including a lease shorter than the plant's life as a residual-value risk negotiated from a weak position. Who decides, and whether a statutory deadline exists. The appeal right as the variable that dominates the schedule and cannot be quantified. Seasonal ecological survey windows as a floor money cannot lift. Mandated against negotiated benefit-sharing, and the counter-intuitive result that the mandated version is easier to live with. The rule that generalises: variance, not difficulty.

## Module 6 — The same project in five regimes
Elapsed time from site control to first export by regulatory archetype. The central distinction: a congested network is a long but datable wait, which can be financed and traded; an appealable consent is an unbounded wait, which cannot be sold to a lender at any length. The last archetype as the international form of the first-project problem the regulated-procurement guide describes.

## Module 7 — What crosses a border
The portable half — design and engineering standards, financial structuring, supplier relationships and buying power, and the process itself — against the non-portable half: consent and network knowledge, type approval and compliance evidence, land relationships, a local delivery record, and the legal and language layer. The asymmetry that decides everything: portable capability is capital-like and reusable, non-portable capability is time-like and earned by doing projects. Why market entry is usually an acquisition, and why the price looks high against the balance sheet.

## Module 8 — What diversification is actually against
The genuine and unusually cheap benefit: uncorrelated political and policy risk. The three common shocks that undo more of it than expected — a global supply chain, a global cost of capital, and a common design. The counter-intuitive one worth dwelling on: a portfolio of long fixed-price contracts is more exposed to interest rates than a merchant one, because a fixed strike price is a bond. The three-part summary a reader should insist on whenever a portfolio is described as diversified.

## Pacing
Eight modules, roughly 15–20 minutes each. Modules 1 and 2 are the policy core and should be read together. Module 3 is the one most often skipped by technical readers and is the one that most often decides whether a project was a good idea. Module 8 is the closing argument and should be read after the Plus Power guide's replicated-defect module, which it depends on.
