# Mainspring Energy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Mainspring Energy (private; developer and manufacturer of linear generators for on-site power) · **Written:** 2026-09-06 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM chemistry, no combustion or air-quality background.

**What this teaches.** What comes out of a stack, and why that decides where a plant may be built. Why burning anything in air produces nitrogen oxides and why the reaction is exponential in temperature; the three mainstream ways to cool a flame and the fourth way, which is to remove it; what after-treatment actually demands in return; why three vendors' "clean" claims are usually incomparable; what changes when a project sits in a county whose air already fails a federal standard; efficiency across the load range; what multi-fuel operation really requires; and the difference in kind between burning fuel and converting it electrochemically.

**Why this gap.** Air permitting is the binding constraint on most on-site generation projects and the corpus treated it as paperwork. The chemistry underneath it is teachable at high-school level and explains almost every design decision in the sector — lean burn, premixing, water injection, catalysts, and the existence of flameless architectures. Without it, a reader cannot tell a genuine emissions advantage from a favourably-quoted number.

**Its place in the bridge-power set.** Third of six, and the course's chemistry module. It owns emissions formation, after-treatment, measurement units and the geography of air quality. The *procedural* side of permitting — duty classes, run-hour ceilings, potential-to-emit arithmetic and thresholds — is deliberately held back for the Enchanted Rock guide, so the two are complementary rather than overlapping: this guide is what comes out of the stack and where you may put it; that one is how many hours you may run it and who owns it.

## Module 1 — Why a hot flame is a dirty flame
Air as four-fifths nitrogen and the strength of the nitrogen–nitrogen bond. The threshold above which it starts to break, and the exponential temperature dependence that follows. Why peak temperature matters more than average temperature, so combustion engineering for emissions is the pursuit of uniformity rather than merely of coolness.

## Module 2 — Cooling the flame, or removing it
Lean burn, dry low-NOx premixing and water injection as three attacks on the same exponential, each with its own cost — lean flames sit close to blow-out and flashback, water is a consumable. Then the logical end point: compress a very lean charge until it reacts everywhere at once, with no flame front and no hot streak. Why that requires an unconventional architecture, and what a free-piston design buys and costs.

## Module 3 — What after-treatment actually does
Selective catalytic reduction and its four demands: a narrow temperature window, stored reagent with its own permits, an ageing catalyst, and dosing tuned between under-treatment and ammonia slip. Oxidation catalysts and particulate control. The systemic value of needing none of it — no reagent logistics, no slip limit, no catalyst replacement, one less subsystem to own.

## Module 4 — Three clean machines, three incomparable numbers
Concentration, rate and annual mass as answers to different questions. Why ppm can be diluted and why oxygen correction exists. Why pounds per megawatt-hour is the honest comparator between technologies, because it rewards efficiency. Why tons per year is what the permit is written against. The disciplined move: convert to lb/MWh for the technology comparison, then to tons per year at the intended hours for the permitting question.

## Module 5 — Where the air is already failing
Criteria pollutants and regional designations. BACT with cost weighed, against the lowest achievable emission rate where it is not. Offsets as the harder requirement — a market in the same airshed that can simply be empty, turning a financial constraint into an absolute one. Why an ultra-low-emission machine's advantage is *geographic* and must be underwritten county by county rather than asserted nationally.

## Module 6 — Efficiency across the load range
Why machines are compared at full load and then run at partial load for most of their lives. The two distinct mechanisms that produce a flat efficiency curve — fleet staging, which works down to the output of one unit, and genuine thermodynamic flatness, which holds lower. Why the area under the curve, not its peak, determines the fuel bill for a load that varies as an AI campus's does.

## Module 7 — What multi-fuel really demands
Wobbe index, methane number, dilution, flame speed and fuel-bound nitrogen as the properties that actually vary. Why blending twenty percent hydrogen is a tuning exercise and burning it neat is a redesign. The ammonia trap: fuel-bound nitrogen opens a NOx route that cooling the flame does not close, so a machine clean on natural gas is not automatically clean on ammonia. The four questions to put to any fuel-flexibility claim.

## Module 8 — Burning fuel, or converting it
Thermal conversion against electrochemical conversion as a difference in kind. The Carnot limit and why raising firing temperature is the whole history of turbine development. What a fuel cell escapes and what it pays: slow load-following, slow starts, a stack that is a consumable, and no inertia. Why that makes it a base-load layer of a hybrid plant rather than a complete answer for a swinging campus.

## Pacing
Eight modules, roughly 30 minutes each. Modules 1–2 are one continuous argument and must be taken together — everything else in the guide depends on the exponential in Module 1. Module 4 is the most practically useful in the whole six-guide course and should be re-read; it is the module that lets a reader audit a vendor claim. Modules 5–8 are independent.
