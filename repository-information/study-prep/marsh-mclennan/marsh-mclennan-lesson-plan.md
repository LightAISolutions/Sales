# Marsh McLennan — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach **how a risk that nobody will hold whole gets covered anyway** — what a broker actually does between a client's exposure and an underwriter's signature, how a tower of layers is assembled and where it silently fails, why the price of every part of it is set by a cycle no participant controls, and what a broker builds when the market will not offer enough capacity at any price. Marsh McLennan is the worked example because it is the largest broker in the world and because, inside eighteen months, it built three separate structures for a risk class that did not previously have a market: data-centre construction, the excess liability tower above it, and an operational property exchange targeting ten billion dollars on a single placement.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **kWh Analytics plan** taught the underwriter's seat — delegated authority, the anatomy of a property policy, hail, the revenue put, parametric against indemnity. That guide's own dossier draws the boundary this plan begins at: kWh "is not a broker (the seat Marsh and Aon hold)". The **DNV** and **Sargent & Lundy** plans taught the engineer's seat, and the **CSA Group** plan taught the certifier's. This is the fourth chair at financial close and it assumes all three. The **Aon plan**, written in the same session, takes the capital side of the same problem — read this one first.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** A broker's product is a *submission*: the case it builds for why this risk is better than its class. A broker's revenue is a percentage of a price it does not set, so separating cycle from performance is the whole analytical problem. And when the traditional market runs out, the broker's response is to manufacture capacity — which is what a facility is, and why five of them appear in this dossier.

**One boundary, held throughout.** Marsh McLennan's battery-storage credentials — a USD 200m Asia facility, 3.8 GW of UK grid-scale storage placed, 650 GW of renewables supported, a 60-strong London team — appear only on its own website, and no independent source names it in a battery placement. Its *data-centre* record, by contrast, is documented in detail by four trade publications. Where this plan uses a company figure it says whose figure it is.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min), with a real insurance certificate or schedule in front of you if you can get one. Module 2 (~35 min). Module 3 (~40 min) — the arithmetic module; work the commission and rate-on-line examples on paper. Module 4 (~30 min). Module 5 (~30 min). Module 6 (~25 min). Then the flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — Whose agent is this, and whose capital is at risk

**The single idea:** every party in an insurance transaction can be located by two questions, and almost every confusion in the industry comes from failing to ask them.

**The two questions.** *Whose agent is this party?* and *whose capital is at risk?* A retail broker is the agent of the buyer and has no capital at risk. A managing general agent is the agent of the carrier and has no capital at risk. Those two look similar from outside — both are intermediaries paid by commission who never pay a claim — and they are opposites.

**The chain, downwards and upwards.** Read downwards it is a distribution channel: sponsor, retail broker, wholesale broker, MGA, carrier — each step adding a commission and reaching a market the last one could not. Read upwards it is a capital stack: carrier, reinsurer, retrocessionaire, capital markets — each step existing because the one below could not hold the whole risk. A hardening market is the top of the stack withdrawing and the price of every step below it rising.

**Where one group occupies four seats.** Marsh McLennan is a retail broker (Marsh Risk), a reinsurance broker (Marsh Re), a middle-market retail agency, and — through Victor — a managing general agent. The last combination is the structurally awkward one, and the company does not address it in published materials.

**Self-check:** a client asks its broker to recommend a carrier, and the broker receives a contingent commission from one of the candidates. Which of the two questions has been violated? *(The first. The broker is the buyer's agent, and a contingent commission pays it on the seller's behalf. This is why the 2004-05 investigations happened, why contingents were eliminated in 2005, and why their return — disclosed in the FY2025 10-K as "primarily driven by MMA and parts of Marsh Risk's international operations" — is worth noticing.)*

## Module 2 — The placement cycle, and the three ways a broker gets sued

**The single idea:** what a commission actually buys is sixteen weeks of work, most of it before anyone has agreed to anything.

**The sequence.** Exposure data and total insured value; the submission; the market strategy; a lead underwriter who sets terms and price; a following market subscribing percentage lines down a slip until each layer is full; the tower structured against price; the lender's insurance adviser reviewing the wording; a binder at inception; the policy issued and checked; and — if things go wrong — claims advocacy, which is unpriced at placement and the part clients remember.

**Why the lead matters more than the price.** A layer without a lead does not get placed at any price. The lead sets terms and wording; followers price off it. Persuading a credible lead to take a novel risk is the single hardest thing in broking, and it is what Marsh's award-winning USD 2bn advanced-reactor placement actually consisted of — two years spent educating more than eighty insurers, ending 128 percent oversubscribed with Munich Re leading.

**The three failure modes, two of which are clerical.** The wording did not match what was agreed. The notice was late — Marsh USA is defending a claim that it notified an insurer roughly 57 hours after a policy expired. And the advice was wrong, which is much larger: the USD 425m Greensill provision is a broker errors-and-omissions exposure against claims reported at both about USD 5bn plus interest and about A$7bn, with no source reconciling the two.

**The letter that moves everything.** A broker-of-record letter transfers a client and its commissions instantly. Most competition between brokers is a fight over these letters, which is why the sector spent 2025 and 2026 in a lateral-hire litigation wave — and why, in one case, twenty employees resigned within a 38-minute window and joined a competitor seven days later.

**Self-check:** why is claims advocacy unpriced at placement, and what does that imply about broker selection? *(Because commission is earned on the premium at binding, before any loss. The implication is that the service a client values most is the one the pricing model does not capture — which is why large accounts move to fees.)*

## Module 3 — Reading a broker's results: cycle against performance

**The single idea:** a broker's revenue is a percentage of a price set by a cycle it does not control, so any growth number has to be decomposed before it means anything.

**The revenue lines and what each is really a function of.** Commission — a function of the rate environment. Fee — a function of work. Contingent commission — a function of what the broker steered. Fiduciary investment income — a function of interest rates, not of the broker at all. Consulting fees — a function of demand for advice.

**The cycle in one paragraph.** Losses erode capital; capacity withdraws; prices rise; high prices attract capital; capacity returns; prices fall. Eight consecutive quarters of decline through Q2 2026, global property down 12 percent, property-catastrophe reinsurance down 16 percent at the midyear renewal, dedicated reinsurance capital up 9 percent. Casualty is the exception, up 2 percent globally and 7 percent in the US.

**The decomposition, worked.** Marsh McLennan's underlying growth halved from 7 percent in FY2024 to 4 percent in FY2025. Aon's was 6 percent in the same environment. Both faced the same rates, so roughly the same cycle drag applies to both — and the two-point gap is the part attributable to the businesses. Marsh Re's revenue actually **fell 2 percent** in Q2 2026 despite double-digit new business and high-90s retention, which is the purest illustration available: excellent performance, negative revenue, because commission is a percentage of a shrinking premium.

**The soft-market effect that matters for storage.** In a soft market you can decline to build a resilient asset and still get insured cheaply. The 2026 Broker Council readout records exactly this — rates "reducing" and "the urgency to build more weather-resilient assets has somewhat diminished". Marsh's own representative gave the counter-argument: "Resilience isn't really about cost savings. It's about continued insurability."

**Self-check:** a broker reports flat revenue in a year when its index shows rates down 10 percent. Good year or bad? *(Good on the face of it — flat revenue against a 10-point price headwind implies real volume or share gains. The only way to be sure is to check whether acquisitions closed in the period, which is what organic growth strips out.)*

## Module 4 — Building a tower, and the three words that break one

**The single idea:** large risks are not insured, they are assembled — and the assembly has three standard failure points that a headline limit conceals.

**The structure.** A primary layer, then excess layers each attaching where the last stops, each subscribed by several carriers taking percentage lines. Munich Re's net capacity for one data-centre construction project is about USD 250m; a USD 10bn tower therefore means forty-plus carriers.

**The three words.** *Attachment point* — where a layer starts paying. *Sublimit* — a cap inside a policy lower than its headline limit, applying to one peril, and the reason a policy that looks adequate is not. *Aggregate limit* — the most paid across all losses in a period; once exhausted the cover is gone however many months remain, which is why reinstatement terms decide whether a second event in one season is covered.

**Rate on line, and how to spot a disguised increase.** Premium divided by limit. A renewal at "the same premium" for a lower limit is a rate rise; rate on line is how you see it.

**Where the numbers actually are, in ascending order:** the Renewables Facility's USD 25m of liability for sub-50 MW solar and battery projects; Nimbus Casualty's USD 75m excess of USD 25m; the hydrogen facility's USD 300m per risk; Nimbus at USD 2.7bn; Willis at USD 3bn; Aon's programme at USD 5bn; Stratus targeting USD 10bn. Note that the only Marsh structure naming battery storage in its own scope is capped at 50 MW per project — an order of magnitude below grid-scale.

**Self-check:** a policy has a USD 500m limit, a USD 25m hail sublimit and a USD 5m deductible. Hail causes USD 80m of damage. What is recoverable, and what would you have negotiated differently? *(USD 20m — the sublimit governs, less the deductible. What you would have negotiated is the sublimit, and you would have had to know at placement which peril actually drives losses at that site.)*

## Module 5 — What a facility is, and why brokers are building them now

**The single idea:** ordinary broking earns a percentage of whatever the market charges; a facility earns from the design. In a market where prices fall 12 percent a year, that is the whole strategy.

**The mechanics.** The broker negotiates terms once with a panel of carriers for a defined class, writes the wording, and then places qualifying accounts into it. The client gets speed and certainty — quotes in three to five business days on the Renewables Facility. The carriers get flow they did not have to underwrite one at a time. The broker gets a product only it can sell.

**What a facility is really for.** Every one of Marsh's five is a class the open market underwrites badly for the same reason: no loss history. Mid-scale solar and storage; green and blue hydrogen; data-centre construction; the excess casualty tower above it; operational data-centre property. This is the same condition that lets a specialist MGA exist at all.

**The newest variant, and read it precisely.** In a conventional facility the carriers commit shares in advance. On Stratus, per Marsh's own product page, "underwriters are able to evaluate each risk individually and decide whether and how to support a placement". The broker built the venue and the rules; capital shows up per risk. That is why USD 10bn is a *target* and why "more than USD 4bn in verbal commitments" is the number that matters.

**The competitive read.** Nimbus stood still at USD 2.7bn from January 2026 while Aon's programme went to USD 3.5bn in April and USD 5bn in July. Marsh's answer was not to match it but to open the operational front instead. Whether that was strategy or necessity, no source says — the sequence is what is on the record.

**Self-check:** why would a carrier join a facility rather than underwrite the same accounts individually? *(Because it acquires a pre-screened flow of business in a class it wants at an acquisition cost far below individual underwriting — an expense-ratio argument. What it gives up is the right to decline individual risks, which is exactly what a prior-submit exchange gives back.)*

## Module 6 — The named-standard gap, and how to read an absence

**The single idea:** the most useful finding in this dossier is a thing that is not there, and knowing how to establish an absence responsibly is a transferable skill.

**The finding.** Across seven Marsh battery-storage documents spanning 2021 to 2026 and four company web properties, no certification body or standard is named — not UL, UL 9540A, NFPA 855, DNV, CSA, TÜV, IEC, IEEE or FM Global. The closest the battery page comes is "varying installation, operation, and engineering standards around the world": invoking standards as a problem while naming none. It then states insurer requirements in engineering terms — separation between enclosures, routing power to the grid independently of the battery. NFPA 855 is the code that specifies separation.

**It is not one firm.** Aon's guidance does the same thing, asking for "three metres or more of spacing" and citing only SCOR's 2022 handbook. Two incumbents, independently, across documents spanning years. That makes it a property of broker-authored risk guidance rather than an oversight — and it locates precisely the gap a certifier or a data-priced MGA occupies.

**How to establish an absence responsibly, in four steps.** Enumerate the corpus you searched and say how large it was. Search normalised text, not raw text — this session's research nearly produced a false "renewables is never mentioned" finding because a PDF extractor had introduced letter-spacing artefacts ("r ene wables"). State the search terms. And distinguish "not found in the extractable text of these documents" from "does not exist", because the two are different claims and only one of them is defensible.

**Self-check:** you find that a company's website never mentions a competitor. What can and cannot be concluded? *(That it does not name competitors in published marketing — which is itself a finding, and true of Marsh across roughly fifty pages reviewed. What cannot be concluded is anything about how it discusses competitors internally or in a pitch. Aon, by contrast, names four competitors in its own 10-K, which shows the difference is a choice rather than a convention.)*
