# MasTec — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** MasTec (listed; a diversified infrastructure contractor spanning renewables, power delivery, communications and data-centre work) · **Written:** 2026-09-06 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no construction-labour, industrial-engineering or occupational-safety background.

**What this teaches.** Why craft labour is categorically different from every other input to a construction project, and why a shortage of it is a schedule risk rather than a cost line. The trades that actually appear on a power or data-centre site and which of them becomes the binding constraint, with the structural observation that the scarcest skills are needed latest. How the labour market really works: locality, travelling crews, hiring through agreements or direct employment, wage floors and apprentice-hour requirements as compliance inputs, the multi-year training pipeline, why firms buy companies instead of hiring people, and supervision as the least-discussed scarcity. Productivity as a measurable quantity — the unit rate a bid is built on, the factor tracked weekly, the repetition effect, and the five specific mechanisms that destroy it. Then safety, taught as a production system: the arithmetic of a recordable rate and why it is statistically unstable at small scale, the insurance-priced modifier that prices the past, leading against lagging indicators, why the events that dominate the injury rate are not the events that kill, and prequalification as a genuine barrier to entry.

**Why this gap.** The corpus treated construction labour, where it appeared at all, as a cost line that rises when demand rises. That model is wrong in the way that matters: in a tight market the hours are not available at the higher price either, so scarcity propagates into the schedule, which is the only place on a project with damages attached. Nothing in the corpus explained the recordable-rate formula, its behaviour at small scale, or why a prequalification threshold built on it excludes capable firms structurally — all of which are load-bearing for reading this market.

**Its place in the construction set.** Second of four. It supplies the mechanism behind the Blattner guide's acceleration cost curve, explaining why hours have declining returns and workers are not available on demand. It hands the quality consequence of crew density and fatigue to the SOLV guide, where it becomes a commissioning problem. And its prequalification argument is the direct sibling of the Samsung C&T guide's surety line: two independent gates that both bear hardest on the firms that would benefit most from passing them.

## Module 1 — The input you cannot order
Why land, equipment, money and permits are procurable and craft hours are not. The regional market, the fixed training pipeline, and the fact that the people are already employed. The claim that follows and the analytical error it corrects: a shortage does not raise the estimate by a wage delta, it removes hours from the plan.

## Module 2 — The trades and the constraint
Operators and earthwork, pile crews, ironworkers and mechanical, electricians, linemen, controls technicians and commissioning testers — what each does and why each becomes limiting. The two structural observations: the scarcest skills are needed latest, so shortage and schedule compression arrive together by construction; and the two scarcest trades are the ones data-centre and grid programmes bid for, which turns a regional market into a national one.

## Module 3 — How the labour market works
Locality as the default and travelling crews as rented capacity. Hiring through agreements and halls against direct employment and internal training, and what each model actually differs in. Wage floors and documented apprentice hours where public money or tax benefits are involved, and why that makes workforce development a compliance question. The apprenticeship as a multi-year pipeline whose response time exceeds the length of most booms. Why acquisitions buy workforces, supervision, licences and standing that recruitment cannot assemble. Supervision ratio as the real cap on how fast a project can grow, and the ramp rather than the total as the plausibility test for any staffing plan.

## Module 4 — Productivity
The unit rate as the quantity every fixed-price bid rests on. The productivity factor as the earliest honest signal a project gives, because it measures work in place rather than time elapsed. The repetition effect, and why mobilisation and small scopes are so expensive. The five destroyers: sustained overtime, trade stacking, out-of-sequence work, waiting, and rework. The asymmetry that makes disruption the hardest claim in construction, and contemporaneous records as a commercial asset built during the work.

## Module 5 — Why a shortage is a schedule risk
The propagation chain: fewer hours, longer duration, moved date, damages running, acceleration bought, productivity destroyed. Two structural consequences — the contractor's rational response to unstaffable work is to decline rather than to price it high, so capacity clears as a queue; and the binding constraint on how fast a sector can build is the one that responds over the length of an apprenticeship rather than over a price cycle.

## Module 6 — The arithmetic of a safety record
What counts as a recordable and why a regulatory definition is what makes comparison possible. The rate formula written out, with the constant explained as one hundred workers for a year. The worked contrast between a two-million-hour firm and a two-hundred-thousand-hour project, and the instability that follows. The severity subset. The insurance modifier and its multi-year lag. Leading against lagging indicators, and the trap that a rising near-miss count is a good sign that looks like a bad one.

## Module 7 — Safety as a production system
The compliance reading against the production reading, and why the mechanisms overlap almost completely — planning the task, isolating energy, controlling access and sequence, housekeeping. The reverse direction, where every productivity destroyer is also an exposure, so acceleration is a safety decision. Arc-flash energy as the hazard specific to this industry, and why a study must be reissued whenever the electrical system changes — which on a live project is continuously. The shape of severe outcomes, and why a low injury rate is compatible with a lethal exposure profile.

## Module 8 — The prequalification gate
A hard numerical threshold against a review of the safety programme, with the honest case for each. Why the threshold is defensible, fast, transmissible down the chain and legible to insurers — and why it rejects on variance rather than on practice, rewards classification management, cannot admit a new entrant, and freezes the bidder list in the markets where competition is scarcest. Prequalification as a market-structure decision rather than an administrative one.

## Pacing
Eight modules, roughly 20 minutes each. Modules 1 and 5 are the argument and can be read as a pair. Module 4 is the practical heart for anyone reading a contractor's performance. Modules 6 and 7 belong together and should not be split: the arithmetic without the production reading invites exactly the number-management the module warns against. Module 8 is short and is best read immediately before the Samsung C&T guide's section on surety capacity.
