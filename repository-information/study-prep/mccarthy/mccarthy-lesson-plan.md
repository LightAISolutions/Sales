**How this plan relates to what you already have.** This app carries **nine** guides written from a contractor's chair and this plan repeats none of what they teach. **HITT** covers the anatomy of a data centre, the commissioning ladder, speed economics and the geography of Data Center Alley. **Holder** covers how hyperscalers buy construction, containment, joint ventures and reading a private contractor's numbers. **Turner** covers the delivery chain, procurement as the product, six-nines reliability and offsite construction. **DPR** covers employee ownership, design for manufacture, VDC, the inside of an AI data hall and schedule science. **Mortenson** covers EPC against GC, substations and voltage, MW against MWh and entitlement. **Rosendin** covers the electrician's trade, the power-chain walk, electrical prefabrication and the apprenticeship pipeline. **Kiewit** covers combined-cycle gas, HVDC, advanced reactors and progressive design-build. **Primoris** covers how a solar farm gets built and percentage-of-completion accounting. **Quanta** covers craft labour as the product and energised-line work.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** The site is **discovered, not designed** — the ground is the one input nobody chose. Concrete is a **chemical reaction on a clock**, not an installation. And a building is **proven, not finished** — by a chain of independent parties, any one of whom can stop it.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min). Module 2 is the one that pays — read it with a structural drawing if you can get one (~40 min). Module 3 (~30 min). Module 4 (~30 min). Module 5 (~25 min). Then flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — The site is discovered, not designed

**The single idea:** the first thing a builder buys on a greenfield campus is information about dirt, and almost every expensive surprise is a fact about the ground nobody paid to learn early enough.

**A geotechnical investigation is a sampling programme, not a survey.** A rig drills borings on a grid — perhaps one every hundred to two hundred feet, deeper under the heavy things — and at intervals down each hole drives a standard sampler with a standard hammer and counts the blows. That **standard penetration test** number is the crudest possible index of how dense or stiff the soil is, and also the most widely understood number in the industry. Samples go to a laboratory for grain size, moisture, plasticity and strength. The report says, in effect: here is what you may stand on, here is what you must remove, and here is where the water is.

**Its real output is a bearing capacity** — the pressure the ground will carry without failing or settling more than the structure tolerates. Good ground carries several thousand pounds per square foot and takes ordinary footings. Poor ground carries a few hundred and forces a different foundation, which is a different price and a different schedule.

**Three findings change a project rather than inform it.** **Expansive soil** — clay that swells wet and shrinks dry — moves whatever sits on it through the seasons, and is dealt with by removing it, stabilising it, or spanning over it, never by hoping. **Shallow groundwater** turns an excavation into a dewatering operation with a discharge permit attached. **Undocumented fill** — a previous owner's buried demolition debris, an old pond, a filled ravine — is worthless as support and nobody knows how much of it there is until it is being dug.

**Then the sitework, and its unifying property is irreversibility.** Clearing and stripping; **mass grading** to the design elevations with a **cut and fill** balance designed so as little soil as possible is hauled; engineered fill placed in thin lifts and **compacted** to a percentage of its laboratory maximum (the **Proctor** density), tested lift by lift by a third party; **stormwater detention** basins that hold rain and release it slowly, which is a permit condition occupying land the site plan had allocated to a building; and underground utilities, which must be in and inspected before the slab covers them.

Concrete can be demolished, a wall moved, equipment swapped. Ground compacted badly under a standing building cannot be fixed without taking the building off. That is why earthwork is inspected continuously, in real time, and why the reports are kept for the life of the asset.

**Self-check:** fill was placed in thick lifts to save time and passed a visual walk-through. When does this surface, and what is the repair? *(Two to five years later, as differential settlement or a cracked slab. The only repair is to take the load off the ground first.)*

## Module 2 — The foundation, the concrete, and the floor

**The single idea:** a foundation is a division problem — load divided by area must stay under the bearing capacity — and the interesting part is what happens when the answer comes out wrong.

**Spread footings** are the default: an isolated reinforced pad under each column. Cheap, fast, independent — and each settles on its own, so differential settlement between two of them is what cracks the structure above. A **mat foundation** replaces all of them with one thick slab under the whole structure, used where soil is weak, loads heavy and closely spaced, or differential settlement must be eliminated rather than tolerated. A **deep foundation** — piles or drilled shafts — gives up on the near-surface soil entirely and carries load down to competent material; it is the answer where the top of the site is fill, soft clay or liquefiable sand, and it is the most expensive of the three by a wide margin.

**The data-centre twist is that the heaviest things are not the building.** A large transformer, a standby generator, a chiller and its tower, and a battery room are point concentrations of mass arriving on their own **equipment pads**, each engineered for static weight, for the dynamic loads a rotating machine imposes, and for crane and maintenance access. Those pads are usually on the critical path: equipment cannot be set until its pad has cured, and equipment that cannot be set cannot be terminated, tested or commissioned.

**And the quiet trap is the embed and the sleeve.** Anything that will bolt to concrete — anchor bolts, rails, brackets — and anything that will pass through it — conduit, chilled-water pipe, busway — must be placed while the concrete is wet, from drawings coordinated between four trades who have never met. After the pour, a missing embed becomes a drilled-and-epoxied repair that must be designed, tested and argued about, and a missing sleeve becomes a hole cut through structure that an engineer has to approve. **A five-minute coordination failure becomes a three-week schedule item.**

**Concrete is a chemical reaction on a clock.** The **water-cement ratio** is fixed at the batch plant and is the strongest single determinant of finished strength — which is why adding water at the chute to make placement easier, the most common quality failure in the industry, trades permanent strength for temporary convenience. **Curing** means keeping it moist and in temperature range while hydration proceeds; it is not drying, and concrete left to dry in the sun is permanently weaker. Test **cylinders** are crushed at seven days as an early warning and at twenty-eight days as the contractual test — and a failed twenty-eight-day break triggers core sampling, a structural review and, at worst, removal of concrete other trades have already built on. Below about 5 °C hydration nearly stops and the pour needs heated enclosures; above about 32 °C it sets too fast and needs ice, chilled water and night pours. **The same slab costs different money in January and July.**

**The floor is a specification, not a surface.** A data hall's slab-on-grade is a structure, a working surface, a vapour barrier and a load path, and each role is a separately specified number: a **uniform load** rating in pounds per square foot; a **point load** rating for a rack castor or a jack, governed by punching shear at the contact patch rather than by the uniform number; **floor flatness** F-numbers measured within days of the pour and effectively permanent afterwards; thickness and reinforcement; joint layout, because an unjointed slab cracks randomly instead; a vapour retarder; a hardened, sealed, non-dusting finish; and every embed and sleeve.

Vertiv's guide names floor loading as a line on a UPS datasheet. This is the other side of that line: **the number on the datasheet is a request, and the slab is where it is granted or refused.** The awkward case is always the retrofit — a hall poured for air-cooled racks asked to accept liquid-cooled ones two or three times heavier, in the same footprint.

**Self-check:** a hall's uniform load rating comfortably exceeds the new racks' weight per square foot, and the floor still cracks at move-in. Why? *(Point load. Liquid-cooled cabinets concentrate their extra mass on the same small castor contact patches; punching shear there is governed by different arithmetic from the distributed rating.)*

## Module 3 — The shell, the envelope, and the live building

**The single idea:** three genuinely different industrial processes enclose a building, and the choice is a schedule instrument.

**Tilt-up** casts wall panels flat on the building's own slab and cranes them upright — fast, cheap, weather-tolerant, and it occupies the floor while panels cure, with panel size capped by the crane. **Precast** casts them in a factory and trucks them in — off the critical path and out of the weather, with tighter tolerances and factory-controlled embeds, at the cost of transport limits and a booked plant slot. **Steel with a light skin** gives the longest spans and the most flexible interior, easy to penetrate later for the pipe and busway liquid cooling keeps adding — at the cost of mill lead times, added fire protection and more trades to reach weather-tight. On a campus of identical buildings the answer tends to be whichever method makes building six faster than building one — the same learning-curve argument the modular-reactor plan makes about factories, applied to a wall.

**The envelope of a building that is mostly heat source inverts the usual rules.** The **air barrier** still matters, for moisture and dust rather than for heating energy: a hall at controlled humidity beside outdoor air at a different dew point will move vapour through any gap, and unfiltered air brings particulate into equipment intakes. **Fire compartmentation** is the internal half, and the unglamorous decisive discipline is firestopping: every conduit, pipe, tray and duct through a rated wall sealed back to that wall's rating with a tested, listed assembly, documented per penetration — tens of thousands of them, installed by every trade, inspected one at a time. And **the roof became a floor**: chillers, dry coolers, generator exhaust and whole mechanical yards sit on it, so it is designed for equipment weight, maintenance access, wind uplift and vibration, with a membrane people walk on weekly.

**Working in a live building is the discipline healthcare invented.** Building inside an operating hospital produced a codified procedure — an **ICRA** — that classifies work by dust generation and space by vulnerability, then prescribes the containment. It is a permit that hangs on the barrier, signed, with the class written on it. **The mechanism is pressure, not sheeting**: the area is sealed and held at slightly *lower* pressure than its surroundings with filtered exhaust, so every leak flows inward, with air locks, tacky mats, HEPA exhaust and a manometer showing the differential is actually there. **Interim life-safety measures** are the second half: construction routinely disables sprinkler branches, bags smoke detectors against dust, turns exit corridors into work zones and opens rated walls — each a temporary condition needing a fire watch, temporary alarms, a signed alternative route and a daily log.

A hospital and a data hall are the same kind of building for a builder: continuously occupied by something that cannot be turned off, dense with mechanical and electrical systems, where a change to one system can take down the whole. And the schedule consequence is the part people miss — live work is slower per unit *and* the units are smaller, because tie-in windows are handed out in hours rather than weeks. **Pricing it at open-site productivity is one of the most reliable ways to lose money in construction.**

**Self-check:** how do you know a containment is working? *(A manometer showing the pressure differential. The sheeting alone proves nothing.)*

## Module 4 — Water as a building, and who carries the risk

**The single idea:** a campus that promises to reuse its water has committed to constructing and operating a treatment plant.

The train is the same unit operations a municipal works uses, at campus scale: **screening and grit removal** (protecting every pump downstream); **clarification** (settling out the bulk of the suspended load, because filtering what could have been settled is expensive); **filtration** (protecting heat-exchanger approach, and therefore plant efficiency); **softening or reverse osmosis** (deciding how many cycles of concentration a tower can run before it scales); **disinfection** (a mandated Legionella regime, not an option); and **residuals handling** — the concentrated reject, sludge and blowdown every stage above produces, which is where a reuse promise most often quietly fails.

Two honest points. Reuse does not eliminate water consumption; it moves it and adds an energy cost, because pushing water through a membrane at pressure is work — a site that reuses aggressively will find its WUE improves and its PUE does not. And this is a **plant**, with operators, chemistry, permits and failure modes: committing to it is committing to run a small utility.

**Then the contract, which decides who carries which risk.** **Design-bid-build** buys the lowest price for a fully defined scope, from a builder with no reason to have improved a design it never saw. **CM at risk** buys constructability advice and buyable early packages, capped by a guaranteed maximum price, at a negotiated rather than bid price. **Design-build** buys a single point of responsibility and an early date, with the owner relying on the contract to protect quality the design has not yet specified. **Progressive design-build** selects on qualifications and agrees price later against an open book, with an exit ramp — which is why it has taken over complex power and process work. **IPD** puts everyone in one contract with a shared risk-and-reward pool, and requires an owner willing to participate.

The mechanics matter more than the label. A **guaranteed maximum price** is only as good as its scope definition and allowances. **Contingency** — money held for work certain to be needed and not yet identified, and the question of whose it is and who keeps what is unspent — is the single most revealing term in a construction contract. **Early work packages**, issuing foundations while the building above is still being designed, are how any of these beat a linear schedule, trading rework risk for months. And the **change-order** log is the honest measure of how complete a design was when construction started, which is why experienced owners read it before the schedule.

**Self-check:** an owner asks for "a GMP so the price is fixed". What have they actually bought? *(A cap on a defined scope. Everything outside that scope, and everything the allowances underestimated, is still theirs — which is why the argument is always about what was inside it.)*

## Module 5 — Proven, not finished

**The single idea:** a building is complete when a series of independent parties have each certified a piece of it, in sequence, and the last has issued a document.

**Special inspection** is the structural layer: code-required independent inspection of soils and compaction, concrete placement and testing, structural welds and bolted connections, sprayed fireproofing and anchors — engaged by the owner, scheduled in advance, performed while the work is exposed, and reported to the building department. **Work covered up before its inspection gets uncovered**, which is the most avoidable and most repeated schedule loss on a large project.

**The AHJ is the layer nobody designs around.** Codes are national; interpretations are local. Two identical buildings in two counties can require different fire pump arrangements, exiting and generator enclosures. This is why a national builder's local knowledge is a real asset — it is a map of who interprets what, and how long each of them takes.

**Then the fire and life-safety chain** — sprinkler hydrostatic tests, fire pump acceptance at rated flow, alarm device-by-device verification, smoke control, and the interlocks between them. These are cross-system tests that cannot happen until electrical, mechanical and fire systems are all substantially complete, which puts them at the end of a schedule with the least float.

**And finally the certificate of occupancy**, which converts a finished building into a usable one — and can be held by a landscaping condition, a traffic-signal contribution, a stormwater as-built or a health-department sign-off on a break-room sink. Owners routinely pursue a temporary or partial certificate; that is negotiated, not granted.

HITT's guide covers the commissioning ladder itself. This is the *other* set of gates running alongside it, held by people who do not work for the owner and cannot be scheduled by the project. **A building can be fully commissioned and still not legally occupiable**, and that gap is where a power-on date most often slips at the very end.

**Self-check:** every system is commissioned and the tenant's equipment is on the dock, and the building cannot be occupied. What are you looking for? *(An outstanding certificate-of-occupancy condition, most likely unrelated to the hall — and a temporary or partial certificate you should have started pursuing months ago.)*
