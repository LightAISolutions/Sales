I.1The one-page cheat sheet

The technology, in five sentences

A solid-state transformer is a converter, not a transformer: it rectifies medium-voltage AC through a stack of series-connected silicon-carbide H-bridge cells, chops each cell’s DC into a 5–50 kHz square wave, isolates it through a suitcase-sized medium-frequency transformer, and parallels every cell’s output into one regulated 800 V DC bus. [PRIMER ch.3] Because the transformer core shrinks with frequency — raise f from 60 Hz to 10 kHz and core area falls by roughly 170× — a 4 MW unit weighs about a fortieth of its 60 Hz equivalent, which Infineon puts at 20 tonnes down to about 500 kg. [PRIMER ch.3.1, ch.5.5] The price of that is that it has the overload manners of a computer rather than of a block of iron: an oil transformer carries 200% load for 30 minutes on thermal mass alone, and an SST’s continuous rating sits near 1.0–1.2× nameplate with no vendor publishing a curve. [PRIMER ch.5.1] 800 V DC and the SST are two separate decisions — a customer can build an 800 V DC hall in 2027 with a transformer-rectifier unit and never buy an SST. [PRIMER ch.2.2] NVIDIA’s own execution paper does exactly that: it builds the first power block from a TRU and reserves the MV-direct SST for the generation “toward 2029”. [GUIDANCE nvidia-800vdc p21–23]

The market, in five sentences

Only one covered vendor’s filing describes an MV-to-800 V DC solid-state transformer as launched and supplying the market — Sungrow’s EnerNeo, specified 10 kV to 13.8 kV, 3 MW standard and 1.5–4.5 MW configurable at 98.5%. [DOSSIER sungrow v9] The most completely specified product in the public record is Heron Link at 34.5 kV and 4.2 MW with over 98.5% conversion efficiency, and it has shipped nothing, with mass production dated late 2027. [DOSSIER heron-power v1] The only orderable solid-state medium-voltage product from an incumbent is ABB’s HiPerGuard, and it is a static MV UPS whose output is medium-voltage AC — not an MV-to-800 V DC converter. [DOSSIER abb v7] The single strongest field evidence anywhere is a 1 MW research unit built at NC State and validated with the New York Power Authority and EPRI on a real distribution feeder, announced in August 2026 — a research unit, not a product, against vendor product claims of 3–7.5 MVA. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] And no vendor had completed UL certification for a data-centre SST as of the most recent analyst survey, which is a claim by a house that sells the newsletter it appears in. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Megmeet, in five sentences

Megmeet is the only mainland-China power vendor NVIDIA named for GB200 NVL72 power and appears on both of the rosters NVIDIA published before it stopped naming vendors — the October 2025 ecosystem post and the August 2026 component tier, and its own H1 2026 interim describes it as “one of NVIDIA’s designated recommended data-centre power suppliers” — membership of a set, not a rank. [DOSSIER megmeet v7] Its shipping chain begins at three-phase 380–480 V AC: an 800 V HVDC sidecar delivering over 1 MW per rack, an 800 V / 570 kW side rack integrating battery-backup and power-conversion functions, 33 kW and 110 kW shelves, BBU and supercapacitor shelves, CRPS and M-CRPS units to 5.5 kW, and a point-of-load Power Brick. [DOSSIER megmeet v7] The solid-state transformer sits at the top of that published chain and is the one item not built — described only as grid high-voltage input to 800 V DC at an expected efficiency above 98.5%, with no service-voltage class disclosed anywhere. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The power-products group grew 60.92% to RMB 1.841 billion in the first half of 2026, its gross margin expanding to 25.06% — the only one of the three largest segments to expand, against smart living and new-energy transport, which both contracted. [DOSSIER megmeet v7] Against that, headline attributable profit rose 45.21% while ex-non-recurring profit fell 16.13% and operating cash flow fell 84.62% — quote both numbers or neither. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Analysis — the one thing to hold in your head

Everything below the converter in Megmeet’s chain is a socket: a standardised form factor that gets re-competed every architecture generation, where the company is measurably competitive and growing. The converter is a block: something a customer specifies once for a campus and then buys for years. Megmeet is winning at the socket and is not yet in the contest for the block, because a block is specified by its input class and Megmeet has not published one. That is not a capability judgement — it is a disclosure fact, and a specification can be published in a quarter. The risk is only that the block consolidates around two or three certified converters before it is.

I.3Five things that moved since 12 September

The primer closed with a dated watch-list on 12 September 2026 — its §8.2, What to watch, and when. [PRIMER ch.8.2] Three company dossiers landed on 19 September and one on 21 September, and three of the five rest on web findings that postdate the primer. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Analysis: these five are what a reader of the primer does not yet know.

1 · DG Matrix is not shipping the product the primer implies

The primer places DG Matrix at “pilots” in the hall-edge layer and describes “a 12–34.5 kV AC MV skid to 400–1,500 V DC … with paid pilots since Q1 2025”. [PRIMER ch.7.3] The dossier that landed on 19 September establishes that the product on the market is the Interport Flex Series: a six-port low-voltage skid whose own specification sheet rates the AC port at 400–480 V three-phase, the DC port at 300–850 V, and peak efficiency at 96–97%. [DOSSIER dg-matrix v1] The medium-voltage Interport Cell Series that would compete with Heron and Amperesand at the hall edge is dated “2028+” by the company’s own CFO deck. [DOSSIER dg-matrix v1] This is the sharpest single correction in this document: anyone placing DG Matrix among the shipping medium-voltage vendors is placing the wrong product.

2 · A sixth SST name exists and the primer does not carry it

Novos Power is a San Diego company registered in California on 7 November 2025, with two to ten employees, no disclosed funding round, no SEC Form D, no patent or paper visible in any public database and no named customer. [DOSSIER novos-power v1] Its only named product is the Variable Airgap Solid State Transformer, and “variable airgap” is undefined on the record — no company document, patent, application, paper or talk abstract explains what is varied or why. [DOSSIER novos-power v1] Its input range is stated three ways across its own channels, 13–48 kV on the site and 35 kV in a July 2026 post, and 48 kV lies above the 38 kV ceiling of UL 2877, the standard that would govern a medium-voltage power supply. [DOSSIER novos-power v1] It is not a competitor; the reason to know the name is that a buyer may raise it.

3 · NVIDIA opened a qualification programme and SSTs are not in it

NVIDIA DSX Ready launched on 21 September 2026 as a qualification programme for partner products meeting the DSX AI-factory reference-design requirements, and at launch it covers exactly two categories: battery energy storage — Hitachi Energy, LG Energy Solution and Tesla — and coolant distribution units — LG Electronics, LiquidStack and Vertiv. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The two mechanics differ: BESS partners run the required qualification tests and submit supporting data for NVIDIA review and approval, while CDU partners use a self-qualification suite. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] NVIDIA says further categories will follow but publishes no timeline and names no transformer, switchgear, rectifier or MV-conversion category. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Analysis

There is no qualification path for a solid-state transformer, and which of the two mechanics a future MV category adopts is the largest open commercial variable in this layer. An NVIDIA-adjudicated test-data gate is a long, expensive, defensible moat; a self-qualification suite is a fast, cheap, crowded one. The useful posture in a first meeting is not to ask when the category opens but to offer to help write it — the category being unwritten is the opportunity, not the obstacle.

4 · The ride-through case got a bigger event and a harder number

The primer’s anchor is the Virginia event of 10 July 2024: a lightning arrester failure on a 230 kV line produced six faults in 82 seconds and about 1,500 MW of data-centre load left the grid on customer-side protection, roughly 1,260 MW of it at the third voltage depression and none of it disconnected by utility equipment. [PRIMER ch.6.3, fig.12] A second and larger event is reported on 22 July 2026 in Dominion’s territory, in which 3.8 GW of data-centre load dropped on a normally cleared fault. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Meanwhile ERCOT’s NOGRR 282 has become a numeric acceptance test rather than a principle: ride through two seconds at 0.85 per unit and 150 milliseconds below 0.35 per unit, draw no more than 150% of normal current, recover 90% of normal consumption within two seconds, and withstand six sags in 90 seconds. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Do not conflate these two events

Analysis: they are two years apart, with different magnitudes. The 2024 event is the one NERC’s incident review documents and the primer reconstructs; the 2026 event is the one the current standards work is being written against. A briefing that merges them into “the Virginia event” will be corrected by anyone who works on large-load interconnection.

5 · The policy fence moved twice, and the two regimes do not line up

The primer records the FCC’s 28 July 2026 action adding foreign-produced connected power inverters to the Covered List. [PRIMER ch.7.4] Executive Order 14421, “Declaring a National Emergency to Secure the United States Bulk-Power System”, followed on 26 August 2026, covering transformers, inverters, circuit breakers, generation turbines, reactors and battery storage plus associated software, firmware and remote-access capability linked to a covered foreign entity — but applying to transmission facilities rated 69 kV and above, with local distribution explicitly excluded. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] An August 2026 FCC update then removed pure AC-to-DC rectifiers and off-grid inverters from the Covered List and added a 45X safe harbour. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Analysis — the asymmetry is the finding

A 34.5 kV behind-the-meter hall-edge SST is probably outside Executive Order 14421, because it sits on local distribution below 69 kV, and inside the FCC Covered List, which has no voltage threshold at all and captures any bidirectional AC↔DC device with remote communication. A unidirectional rectifier-only architecture may sit outside both. The two regimes were written for different problems and they do not compose; the binding one for a hall-edge converter is the FCC list.

Analysis — the three words that do the most work

Class. Not “voltage” — class. A buyer’s engineer does not ask what voltage your converter takes, they ask what class, because the ANSI C84.1 class sets the insulation, the BIL, the cell count and therefore the product. Someone who says “we can do medium voltage” has told them nothing; someone who says “35 kV class, 150 kV BIL” has told them everything.

Boundary. Every efficiency figure in this market is meaningless without it. When a vendor says 98.5%, the right response is “across which terminals, at what load point, including auxiliaries and cooling or not?” Nobody publishes the answer, which means the first vendor who does gains an advantage that costs them nothing.

Undisclosed. Used precisely, it is a credibility asset rather than a weakness. It says: I know exactly what my company has and has not published, and I will not guess in front of you. Used loosely — as a way of avoiding a question — it is the opposite.

5Limitations → mitigation → who → status

Extends the primer’s chapter 5 (overload, faults, insulation, reliability, cost and standards) and chapter 6 (grid, supply chain, ride-through, codes, sites, economics). The primer describes the limitations. What it does not do is name, for each one, who is actually working on it and how far they have got — and the answer for a whole family of them is “nobody”. That is the finding of this chapter and of chapter 7, which takes the same table and reads it as an adoption question rather than an engineering one.

The status word is from the fixed set and is assigned from evidence, not from vendor language: unsolved means no named owner or no published method; lab means an identified research programme; pilot means a named party is doing it at scale but not shipping; product means it is purchasable, even if only as a vendor claim; standardised means a standards body or regulator owns it.

The full twenty-five-row table is chapter 7. This chapter takes the four that are intrinsic to the converter — the ones a customer’s engineer raises in the first meeting, in the order they raise them.

5.1 Overload — the thermal reservoir an SST does not have

A liquid-filled 60 Hz transformer tolerates 200% load for 30 minutes because tonnes of oil and iron take time to heat, and a data hall exploits that margin every time a row steps from idle to full load or a fault draws current until a breaker clears. [PRIMER ch.5.1] An SST’s silicon has microseconds of thermal margin, so surge tolerance collapses to transient events and continuous rating sits near 1.0–1.2× nameplate. [PRIMER ch.5.1] No first-party SST vendor publishes an overload curve, and no named body owns the gap as a published rating. [PRIMER ch.5.1] Status: unsolved as a rating, product as a design property vendors assert. The practical consequence is that an SST is sized for peak rather than average, and DC-bus storage — which NVIDIA already requires for GPU swings — becomes part of the overload strategy rather than an option. [PRIMER ch.5.1]

5.2 Fault handling — the deepest structural difference

ORNL’s 2026 review is blunt: MV-SSTs “exhibit limited fault withstand capability … unlike conventional transformers, which benefit from substantial thermal and magnetic fault tolerance, MV-SSTs must actively detect, limit, and interrupt fault currents within timescales compatible with semiconductor safe-operating limits”. [PRIMER ch.5.2] On the DC side the problem is capacitor energy: Siemens measured that on a common 800 V bus a fault in one load drew discharge from all three healthy loads’ capacitors, fused clearing took about 20 ms, and interruption at a 200 A/µs rise rate “was only possible with solid-state circuit breakers; other devices were not able to react fast enough”. [PRIMER ch.5.2] Named owners exist here: ABB claims the first fully IEC-certified solid-state circuit breaker in production, and Eaton has published a 1,500 V / 1,250 A hybrid SSCB. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Status: product at the device, pilot for a complete 800 V DC hall balance of system.

5.3 Insulation and partial discharge — the hardest hardware problem

The medium-frequency transformer must hold medium-voltage insulation in a small volume while being driven by PWM square waves with very fast edges; TDK’s assessment is that it is “the biggest challenge”, needing “three times the insulation” — a 35 kV interface requiring roughly 100 kV. [PRIMER ch.5.3] ORNL identifies two distinct stresses: insulation coordination under IEEE C62.82.1, a framework written for iron, where “localized insulation overstress may occur even when terminal-level clamping appears adequate”; and converter-induced stress where fast dv/dt makes voltage sharing “dominated by parasitic capacitances” and drives partial discharge that shortens insulation life. [PRIMER ch.5.3] There is a named academic owner: a Georgia Tech group identifies the mismatch between metal-oxide-varistor protective levels and SST insulation strength, and “the incompatibility of standard impulse test on SST protective structures” — in plain terms, the standard BIL test does not apply cleanly to an SST. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Status: lab.

5.4 Reliability — thousands of devices and one unsolved failure mode

A 4 MW SST contains on the order of a hundred or more SiC modules plus their capacitors, drivers and sensors, against a requirement of 20-year life at 99.9–99.999% availability. [PRIMER ch.5.4] The modular design gives inherent redundancy and vendors design for it — Heron tolerates two cell failures per phase with sub-cycle bypass. [DOSSIER heron-power v1] But ORNL flags a problem specific to wide-bandgap devices: series-stacked designs need a failed die to fail short and keep conducting for months, which press-pack silicon achieves and which SiC’s thermal stability, small die area and short short-circuit withstand make “an open research challenge”. [PRIMER ch.5.4] Status: pilot at the system, unsolved at the die. And the arithmetic nobody advertises: the one independent figure available says N+1 raises a modular multilevel drive’s MTBF by a factor of about 1.7 — a long way from the step change “five nines” implies. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

5.5 What the converter does better than iron

Five genuine advantages, and it is worth being precise about them because three of the five are commonly overstated. Grid behaviour: the MV-side active front end draws sinusoidal current at unity power factor, can supply reactive power, and can ride through voltage dips to a specified curve — NVIDIA asks for ride-through “surpassing current ITIC curve expectations by referencing ERCOT VRT requirements”, and a transformer can do none of it. [PRIMER ch.5.5] A native DC bus: batteries couple directly with no extra DC-DC stage, as can DC-coupled solar or fuel cells. [PRIMER ch.5.5] Bidirectionality: two active bridges around the MFT mean stored energy can flow back to the grid for demand response. [PRIMER ch.5.5] Footprint and speed: ORNL cites one third the weight and volume of an LFT-based chain, and Infineon’s own comparison is about 40× lighter and 14× smaller and roughly 50% faster to construct. [PRIMER ch.5.5] Sungrow claims 312 kW/m² — a vendor figure for its own product. [DOSSIER sungrow v9] Heron claims a 70% smaller footprint and a 24-week lead time against 46–48 weeks for a conventional lineup — its own model, and note that it is not the 128–210-week transformer lead time this document quotes elsewhere, which is for the utility service transformer rather than for the secondary lineup Heron is comparing against. [DOSSIER heron-power v1] Partial-load efficiency: idle cells switch off, and a real-time-simulator study put an SST at roughly 2% loss nearly flat across load against 7–13% for a UPS chain. [PRIMER ch.4.3]

The efficiency sentence to get right

NVIDIA’s headline numbers — up to 5% better end-to-end efficiency, 45% less copper, 85% more power through the same conductor, up to 70% lower maintenance and up to 30% lower TCO — are claims for the 800 V DC architecture against a legacy AC hall whose end-to-end efficiency NVIDIA puts at “less than 90%”. They are not claims for the SST against the MV rectifier, which NVIDIA rates at the same 98.5%. [PRIMER ch.4.3] The SST’s advantages in NVIDIA’s own framing are stage elimination, footprint and control. Saying “an SST is 5% more efficient” is the single most common error in this market and it is wrong in two directions at once.

6.2 Why 34.5 kV is the class that matters, in numbers

US utilities serve large customers at 12.47, 13.2 or 13.8 kV — the ANSI C84.1 15 kV class — or at 24.9 kV or 34.5 kV, while gigawatt campuses interconnect at 69–345 kV with their own substations. [PRIMER ch.6.1, fig.11] Large greenfield campuses in Texas and the Southeast trend to 34.5 kV because it moves more power on fewer, smaller feeders, and Northern Virginia campuses run 34.5 kV distribution behind 230 kV ties. [PRIMER ch.6.1]

Because a cascaded-H-bridge SST’s cell count scales roughly linearly with input voltage, the step from the 15 kV class to the 35 kV class multiplies cells, medium-frequency transformers, gate drivers and control channels by roughly 2.5×. [PRIMER ch.6.1] Analysis: the counts computed on the primer’s own assumptions bracket that figure rather than land on it — 13.8 → 34.5 kV is 15 → 35 cells, a 2.3× step, and 12.47 → 34.5 kV is 13 → 35, a 2.7× step. The primer’s 2.5× is the round number for the class step; the two exact ratios are what a bill of materials actually moves by. That is the whole argument, and it is worth seeing as a count rather than a multiplier.

With 1.7 kV devices a 12.47 kV design needs about 13 cells per phase and a 34.5 kV design about 35 — a 2.7× step in cells and therefore in capacitor banks, magnetics, gate drivers, sensors and failure points. Moving to 3.3 kV devices roughly halves both counts, which is precisely why Amperesand builds its front end on 3.3 kV silicon-carbide modules. [DOSSIER amperesand v2] On top of the count, the insulation class steps from 95 kV BIL to 150 kV, and a 35 kV interface needs roughly 100 kV of insulation inside the medium-frequency transformer itself. [PRIMER ch.5.3]

Three cautions that will otherwise cost you

345 kV is not 34.5 kV. Abilene’s Oncor interconnection is 345 kV transmission, and the campus distribution class is undisclosed. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] One decimal point turns a transmission fact into a fabricated service-class finding, and it is the single most likely error to introduce into this material.

The only named US AI-compute site with a disclosed class attached to an SST is at 12 kV, not 34.5 kV: Alderbuck Energy’s unit for the San Diego Supercomputer Center, hosted by San Diego Gas & Electric and funded by the California Energy Commission, announced 23 June 2026 and still to pass factory test and lab validation before installation. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Not everyone agrees 34.5 kV is ready. Hitachi Energy — the incumbent with the deepest MV transformer manufacturing base — is quoted urging caution specifically on compact 34.5 kV designs, on insulation and manufacturability grounds, and saying the technology “is not fully mature for every use case”. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] That is a trade-press paraphrase rather than a Hitachi release, but it is the most interesting single datapoint in the question: the company that would have to build the magnetics is the sceptic.

6.3 Programmes, buyers and what is genuinely energised

The strongest field evidence in the world is a research unit. A 1 MW solid-state transformer designed and built at NC State was tested on a real distribution feeder at EPRI’s power delivery laboratory in Lenox, Massachusetts, announced 18 August 2026, funded by the New York Power Authority and a DOE award, and claimed as “the first independently verified, megawatt-class solid state transformer validated on a live utility distribution feeder”. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The primer gives its class: a 1 MVA unit on a 13.2 kV feeder — the 15 kV class — using 15 kV SiC MOSFETs, run over three days supplying an EV load and energised more than ten times in June 2026. [PRIMER ch.5.6] Analysis: it therefore belongs in the ledger proper rather than in the undisclosed block, and it is the only field pilot in the table.

The largest committed volumes are all at the low class. Sungrow’s EnerNeo carries framework purchase agreements with HEC Technology for 30 MW across 2026–27 and with ZDATA for 100 MW, with a demonstration expected at the end of 2026 and deliveries in 2027–28 — and the product is specified 10 kV to 13.8 kV. [DOSSIER sungrow v9]

No SST has been evidenced energised at a named hyperscaler site. Microsoft states that solid-state transformers are “the next step” in its power transformation after Mt Diablo, naming no vendor, no site, no kV, no MW and no date. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] GE Vernova stated in April 2026 that a first product would be delivered to an unnamed hyperscaler in the autumn of 2026 followed by about six months of testing, and as of 23 September that delivery has not been reported. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Everything else at hyperscaler scale is ecosystem membership or an unnamed counterparty.

Analysis — what the ledger is actually telling you

Three facts, held together, define the commercial reality of this layer. The most commercially advanced SST in the world cannot serve the class that US hyperscale campuses take. The best-evidenced megawatt-class unit anywhere is a university research project. And the only named US site with a disclosed class is at 12 kV. A vendor arriving with a credible 34.5 kV-class product and a witnessed first article would not be joining a crowded field — it would be the first entrant in an empty one. That is the opportunity and the risk in the same sentence, because being first also means being the vendor who discovers what the standards bodies have not yet written.

7.1 The operations-and-maintenance chapter, which does not exist anywhere else

The framing that matters more than any number below

There is no field-service corpus for medium-voltage solid-state transformers because there is no installed base. Every serviceability figure in this section is either a vendor design claim about a product not yet shipped at scale, or borrowed from the nearest analogue with real field history — the cell-based MV drive, serviced by OEM field engineers for two decades. The honest position is that the O&M model is asserted, not demonstrated, and holding that distinction in a customer meeting is worth more than any of the individual claims. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Mean time to repair. The most concrete claim available is Amperesand’s: a power stage “can be swapped out in less than 20 minutes”, with modules “small and light enough for simple two-person replacement in minutes, using readily available lifts”. It is published in a case study by Wolfspeed, which supplies the silicon carbide inside the product, so both parties gain from the claim. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The comparison that puts it in perspective: GE Vernova markets the identical property for its MM7 modular multilevel drive — modules “light enough for one or two people to swap a faulty unit with a spare within a few minutes” — so swap-in-minutes is established MV-drive practice rather than an SST innovation. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Reliability arithmetic. No MV SST vendor publishes an MTBF or FIT number for the complete converter. Every reliability statement located is either qualitative — “five nines”, “excellent cosmic ray performance” — or a manufacturing metric such as Enphase’s 500 defects per million shipped, which is a production-quality figure and must never be relayed as a field reliability rate. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The one independent number available says N+1 redundancy raises a modular multilevel drive’s MTBF by a factor of about 1.7, and the same source warns that “extreme care should be taken when using these figures to compare vendors” — a drives consultancy with an interest in arguing vendor numbers are incomparable, which happens to point at a defensible conclusion. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Service life. The published SST design target located is “15+ year lifetime”. A large oil-filled transformer is specified and financed against a service life several times that. No standards body has defined a life class for an MV SST and no vendor publishes an end-of-life or mid-life-refresh plan, so an owner buying an SST is buying a mid-life silicon replacement into the capital plan — and nobody found has costed it. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Warranty and spares. Exactly one warranty term was located across the entire research pass: Enphase’s 10-year limited warranty on the IQ SST. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] No warranty, service-contract structure, response-time commitment or availability guarantee was published by Eaton, ABB, Hitachi Energy, Amperesand, DG Matrix or Heron Power in anything reachable. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Nor does any vendor publish a spares list, a recommended stocking level or a spare-cell price. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The structural point is worth carrying into a meeting: an oil transformer’s spares strategy is a bushing and a fan; a several-hundred-module SST’s spares strategy is an inventory of power electronics with its own obsolescence clock.

Firmware and cyber — a transformer that takes software updates. The frameworks exist and an MV-SST-specific profile does not: ISA/IEC 62443 is the product-side OT security standard and vendor platforms already aggregate access, configuration and firmware events for monitoring against NERC CIP and IEC 62443-3-3. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The regulatory teeth arrived from an unexpected direction. The stated national-security rationale for the FCC’s July 2026 action is precisely the firmware-update channel, and the companion waiver preserves qualifying Class I and Class II software and firmware updates on already-authorised covered devices only through at least 1 January 2029 — the right to patch a covered device is now a time-limited regulatory grant, not a vendor decision. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Whether NERC CIP attaches to a behind-the-meter hall-edge SST is addressed by no source located, and is an open question. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Technicians. The United States is reported to need more than 300,000 additional electricians while roughly 20,000 retire every year, with medium-voltage scope and switchgear commissioning named as the specific skills moving into the data-centre footprint — a widely repeated figure from a trade careers source that does not trace to a primary one. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Training money is being spent by Google with NECA and the Electrical Training Alliance, and the same source makes the honest point about it: a four-week credential is not a four-year apprenticeship, and an apprentice starting a five-year IBEW programme in early 2025 qualifies in 2030. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

7.2 Standards and listing — the three-year gap

The clearest single finding in the whole standards picture is a timing gap. NFPA 70E 2027 adds a new Article 310 for DC hazards with explicit thresholds — contact-thermal at power at or above 1,000 W, shock at or above 100 V DC and above 40 mA, arc flash above 150 V DC and above 1.2 cal/cm² — and an 800 V DC hall bus is over every one of them. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Meanwhile the NFPA Fire Protection Research Foundation’s project to develop a DC arc-flash hazard model was, in February 2026, still seeking sponsors at an estimated $3–4 million, with results aimed at the 2030 edition of 70E. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] So the hazard is named in 2027 and the calculation method arrives, at the earliest, in 2030. In between, operators energise 800 V DC busway with conservative table-based PPE, de-energised-work rules and per-project negotiation with the authority having jurisdiction. [PRIMER ch.6.4]

The listing envelope itself exists. UL 1741 with supplements SA and SB is the dominant US route and MV considerations were brought in by revision; UL 347A and UL 2877 cover MV power conversion from 1 kV up to 38 kV, which puts the 34.5 kV hall-edge case inside them. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Internationally IEC 62477-2 covers converters from 1 kV AC / 1.5 kV DC up to 36 kV AC / 54 kV DC, and Edition 2 went to a committee-draft-for-vote closing 13 March 2026. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] NEC 2026 adds a new Article 270 for medium-voltage grounding and bonding above 1,000 V AC, relevant precisely because SSTs push MV into the building footprint. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

The line to remember from PCIM Europe 2026

“Currently, we don’t have a specification, and currently we don’t have a UL certification for the 800-VDC bus. That is a major gap.” [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Related, and equally load-bearing: the server ecosystem was built around the SELV classification covering circuits below 60 V, so creepage and clearance at 800 V are unresolved in existing designs. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

7.3 Utility acceptance — the question has changed

Eighteen months ago the question was whether a utility would accept a converter at the service point. It is now whether any large load can prove it rides through — and a converter is the easiest thing to prove it with. The driver is not SST-specific; it is the load-loss events. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

ERCOT and the PUCT have turned ride-through into a numeric acceptance test through NOGRR 282, and PUCT rule 16 TAC §25.194 under Project 58481 implements Texas SB 6 at 75 MW and above, with study fees of $100,000 for 75–249 MW and $300,000 at 250 MW and above, financial security at $50,000 per MW of requested peak demand and a non-refundable interconnection fee of $50,000 per MW of contracted peak demand. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] NERC Project 2026-02 proposes CLO-001 for interconnection studies and modelling data, CLO-002 for operational data and communications and CLO-003 for protection coordination and disturbance monitoring, against a FERC-directed filing deadline of 31 December 2026. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] FERC opened a generic rulemaking on large-load interconnection at Docket RM26-4-000 and on 18 June 2026 issued Federal Power Act section 206 show-cause orders to all six RTOs and ISOs on their large-load and co-located-load tariffs. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Individual utilities have moved too: Dominion’s new GS-5 rate class for customers above 25 MW was approved in November 2025 for a January 2027 effective date with 14-year contracts [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]; AEP Ohio’s data-centre tariff took effect 23 July 2025 requiring large new customers to pay for a minimum 85% of stated monthly energy. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

What has not changed is the approved-equipment question. At 12.47–34.5 kV the service transformer is commonly utility-owned, and an SST displacing it must be accepted onto a utility’s approved-equipment list — a multi-year qualification with no visible US precedent, which is why every announced US SST pilot sits behind a customer-owned medium-voltage substation. [PRIMER ch.6.5]

7.4 The three obstacles nobody is working on

Worth stating plainly, because each is a question a careful buyer asks and no vendor can currently answer. Insurance and bankability of a novel MV asset: no insurer, reinsurer, broker or owner’s-engineer standard naming solid-state transformers was located in the research pass. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Part-load efficiency: every figure in the market is a peak or nominal number, and a data hall does not run at rated load. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Field-failure and availability data from an operating installation: none exists to find, because there is no installed base of MV data-centre SSTs to report on. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Analysis — where the leverage is

The technical obstacles all have named owners and most are at product or standardised. The operational ones mostly have nobody. That inverts the usual assumption about where a challenger has room: the engineering is crowded and the operations manual is blank. The first vendor to publish an overload curve, a part-load efficiency curve, an MTBF figure for the complete converter and a warranty with a response-time commitment gains a durable advantage at essentially zero engineering cost — because publishing what you already know is cheaper than inventing anything, and every one of those four is a question a buyer’s engineer asks in the first hour.

8NVIDIA and Oracle: what their programmes specify, and what their engineers will ask

Extends the primer’s chapter 2.2 and chapter 4.2 on NVIDIA; the primer does not cover Oracle at all. 8.1 NVIDIA — what is on the record

The architecture is published and consistent on its own terms, with one internal inconsistency worth knowing about. NVIDIA’s technical blog specifies converting 13.8 kV AC grid power directly to 800 V DC at the data-centre perimeter using industrial-grade rectifiers, then distributing via row-level 800 V DC busways over two conductors with DC-DC conversion in the compute rack. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] A different NVIDIA blog of the same date cites medium voltage “e.g., 35 kVAC” as the utility supply and puts AC-to-DC conversion “at the facility level”. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] NVIDIA’s own pages name two different MV classes. Ask which one a given site uses; do not assume, and do not assume they are unaware of the discrepancy.

The load profile is the most important number NVIDIA publishes for a converter vendor: rack utilisation swings from 30% to 100% in milliseconds. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] NVIDIA’s own answer to it is rack-side — 400 J per GPU of capacitor storage in Vera Rubin, “6× more rack-level energy storage” than the prior generation, Intelligent Power Smoothing and dynamic power steering. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The commercial consequence is that the real negotiation is not whether your converter can ride the swing, but how much of the ride-through is yours and how much is theirs.

The ecosystem roster is a weaker signal than it looks. NVIDIA’s October 2025 blog lists companies “building 800 VDC-compatible infrastructure”, which is a published alignment and not a qualification, a design win or a supply agreement; by August 2026 NVIDIA describes more than eighty manufacturers building to the specification without publishing the list. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Critically, that page does not break the roster into rectifier or SST sub-categories and names no vendor as an SST vendor — so any claim that a firm is “NVIDIA’s named SST partner” is unsupported by it. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The three tiers it does name are silicon (fourteen firms including Infineon), power system components (Bizlink, Delta, Flex, Lead Wealth, LITEON and Megmeet) and data-centre power systems (ABB, Eaton, GE Vernova, Heron Power, Hitachi Energy, Mitsubishi Electric, Schneider Electric, Siemens and Vertiv). [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

12Policy and supply chain

Four regimes bear on a China-headquartered vendor selling medium-voltage power conversion into US AI infrastructure, and they do not compose cleanly. Getting them right is worth more in a procurement conversation than any technical answer, because procurement is where this product line is most likely to be stopped.

12.1 The FCC Covered List — the binding one

On 28 July 2026 the FCC’s Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau added foreign-produced power inverters to the Covered List, implementing national-security determinations issued the previous day. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] The definition has two elements and a device must meet both: it must be “a bidirectional power device or system that converts direct-current electricity to alternating-current electricity, or alternating-current electricity to direct-current electricity”, and it must contain components “enabling remote communication, control, sensing, data collection, or monitoring”. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] There is no voltage or power threshold. A telemetered MV solid-state transformer is a literal instance of both elements.

Three things are commonly got wrong about it. It is not a China rule. “Foreign-produced” is defined by failure of the Buy American standard at 48 CFR §25.101(a) — generally US manufacture with domestic component costs above 65% for 2024–2028 — so it is a content test, not a nationality test, and a US-headquartered vendor building offshore is captured while a China-headquartered vendor building in the US in principle is not. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] It is prospective. Models authorised before 28 July may continue to be imported, marketed, sold and used. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] There is a route out, through conditional approval by the Department of War or DHS, with applications due by 1 January 2028 requiring ownership disclosure to 5% beneficial owners, a component-level bill of materials with country of origin, identification of who is responsible for software updates, and a time-bound US manufacturing plan with committed capital. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

And one thing has changed since the primer: an August 2026 update removed pure AC-to-DC rectifiers and off-grid inverters from the list and added a 45X safe harbour — so a unidirectional rectifier-only architecture may sit outside the rule where a bidirectional SST does not. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

12.2 Executive Order 14421 — and why it probably misses the hall edge

Executive Order 14421, “Declaring a National Emergency to Secure the United States Bulk-Power System”, was signed on 26 August 2026 under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. It covers transformers, inverters, circuit breakers, generation turbines, reactors and battery storage, plus associated components, software, firmware, digital services, maintenance services and remote-access capability linked to a covered foreign entity. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] Crucially for this product line, it applies to transmission facilities rated 69 kV and above and local distribution is excluded. DOE must issue implementing regulations within 120 days — by 24 December 2026 — and FAR revision recommendations within 180 days. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Analysis — the asymmetry to carry into a procurement conversation

A 34.5 kV behind-the-meter hall-edge converter is probably outside EO 14421, because it sits on local distribution below 69 kV, and inside the FCC Covered List, which has no voltage threshold at all. The two regimes were written for different problems and do not line up. Saying this accurately, and saying which one you think binds and why, is a stronger position than either claiming exemption or conceding exposure — and it is a question for counsel and the certification body, which is a strength to say out loud rather than a dodge.

12.3 Tariffs, the EU, and what Megmeet has actually said

A Section 232 tariff of 50% on semi-finished copper and copper-intensive derivatives took effect on 1 August 2025 — copper windings being the second-largest bill-of-materials line in both a line-frequency transformer and an SST. [PRIMER ch.6.2] Section 301 rates on certain Chinese imports rose from 1 January 2026, and a January 2026 Section 232 proclamation imposed an immediate 25% tariff on certain advanced semiconductor articles; no transformer-specific or power-electronics-specific line item was located, with corporate filings describing the exposure generically. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] In the European Union, the Commission moved in April–May 2026 to restrict EU funding for projects using inverters from high-risk countries, phasing in from 1 November 2026 with new contracts fully incorporating the restrictions from April 2027. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]

Megmeet’s own language on tariffs is direct: activating Thailand factory capacity can, to a large extent, avoid the US tariff problem. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] It also states that US sanctions and export-control regimes may involve certain of its customers and suppliers. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] No FEOC or Section 889 disclosure appears anywhere in its filings. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23] And on listings: no UL or ETL listing number is disclosed for any Megmeet data-centre product — the company claims UL, TÜV and CNAS laboratory accreditations, which are an in-house testing credential and not a listed product. [WEB, verified 2026-09-23]
