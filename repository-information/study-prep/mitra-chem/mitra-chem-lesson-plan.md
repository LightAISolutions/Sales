# Mitra Chem — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** the cathode powder — what lithium iron phosphate cathode active material is, how it is manufactured, why almost all of it is Chinese, and what it takes to build a plant that makes it in the United States.
**Assumed starting point:** high-school STEM. The CALB plan covers what happens when a storage cell gets very large and the Great Power plan covers the rest of the battery map; this one steps one link up the chain, to the powder both of those companies buy. It does not depend on either.
**What this is not:** a company crib sheet. Mitra Chem's funding rounds, directors and dates stay in the dossier.

**Suggested pacing.** Eight modules. Modules 1–3 are chemistry and manufacturing and hold together as one sitting of about ninety minutes. Modules 4–6 are policy and plant economics — the reason a cathode plant is a policy object as much as a chemical one — and take a second sitting. Modules 7–8 teach how to read a private company and a federal grant, and transfer to every private subject in the corpus.

**The one sentence to hold onto:** whoever makes the powder sets the cell's cost, its safety and — under the 2026 rules — its tax status; the hard part is not the chemistry but the tonnes.

## Module 1 — What a cathode does

**Concepts:** intercalation · cell voltage · specific capacity · why the cathode carries the cost.

Start with the mechanism. A lithium-ion cell stores energy by moving lithium ions between two host crystals; on charge they leave the cathode for the graphite anode and on discharge they return. Neither host is consumed — the ions slide into and out of gaps in the structure, which is what intercalation means — so the reaction repeats thousands of times.

Then the two things the cathode decides. Voltage: the cell voltage is the difference between the two hosts' electrochemical potentials, and since graphite sits near lithium metal, the cathode sets nearly all of it. Cost: the anode is cheap graphite; the cathode carries the lithium and, in nickel chemistries, the nickel and cobalt. Have the learner state why a "cheap cathode" and a "cheap cell" are nearly the same sentence.

**The arithmetic to install:** specific capacity in mAh/g times voltage gives Wh/g. LFP at about 155 mAh/g and 3.2 V stores roughly half a watt-hour per gram, so a kilowatt-hour of cells needs two to two-and-a-half kilograms of cathode powder once binder, carbon and practical losses are included. Every plant figure in the dossier converts through that ratio.

## Module 2 — Why iron phosphate, and what manganese adds

**Concepts:** olivine · thermal stability · the energy-density penalty · LMFP.

LFP's olivine crystal holds oxygen in strong phosphate bonds, so an overheated LFP cathode does not release oxygen the way a nickel-oxide cathode can. That is the whole reason grid storage — containers standing next to containers — chose it, and why it cycles for so long: the crystal barely changes shape as lithium comes and goes. The price is 3.2 V against NMC's 3.7 V and a lower energy density.

Teach the conductivity problem and its fix. Olivine conducts electrons and ions poorly, so the industry makes the particles very small and wraps each in a thin carbon coating. Ask the learner why that turns a chemistry business into a particle-engineering business — the answer is that the coating, the particle size and how the powder packs are decided in the factory, not in the formula.

**Then LMFP.** Swap part of the iron for manganese, whose reaction runs at about 4.1 V, and the same structure stores 15–20% more energy per kilogram at similar cost. The trade: manganese dissolves into the electrolyte and conducts worse, so LMFP is harder to make and to keep alive over thousands of cycles. That is the chemistry a carmaker asked Mitra Chem to develop, and the reason it has been "as early as 2026" for three years.

## Module 3 — How the powder is made, and what "on spec" means

**Concepts:** iron phosphate precursor · spray drying · sintering · hydrothermal and precursor-free routes · press density · D50 · cell qualification.

Walk the standard route: mill an iron phosphate precursor, lithium carbonate and a sugar into a slurry; spray-dry it into a free-flowing powder; sinter it for hours under nitrogen so the iron is not oxidised while the sugar chars into the carbon coat and the olivine forms; mill, classify, pack. Then the alternatives — hydrothermal synthesis in hot pressurised water for finer shape control, and the precursor-free route Mitra Chem's case study describes, which skips the intermediate.

Teach the three numbers that define "on spec": specific capacity (mAh/g), press density (g/cm³ in the rolled electrode — it sets Wh per litre and is the one number Mitra Chem's Sun Chemical release singles out), and D50 with its spread. Behind them sit impurities in parts per million, moisture, surface area and coating thickness.

**The two facts that explain a customer-less cathode company:** cell makers qualify a powder by building cells and cycling them for months, and repeat it for every plant and process change; and the numbers move with scale, because heat, mixing and drying in a tonne-a-day kiln do not behave like a kilogram-a-day reactor. Have the learner rewrite "from 1 kg/day to 1,000 kg/day in two years" as a claim about process transfer rather than about a plant, and "20× lower cost" as a claim with no denominator.

## Module 4 — Why the supply is Chinese, and what changed on 1 January 2026

**Concepts:** industrial clustering · the price war · 45X · prohibited foreign entity · the material-assistance cost ratio.

China makes almost all LFP cathode because the phosphate and lithium-carbonate industries, the cell makers and the decade-old plants are there, and because a 2023–2025 price war pushed the powder to a level at which Chinese producers themselves lost money. A Western plant starts at higher cost and zero volume.

Then the two policy instruments. 45X pays a US producer 10% of production cost. The prohibited-foreign-entity rules, from 1 January 2026, require a storage project to keep prohibited-entity content below a share of its equipment cost — the material-assistance cost ratio, 55% non-PFE in 2026 rising to 75% after 2029, with a steeper ladder for the manufacturer's own 45X.

**The arithmetic that makes the cathode the pinch point:** cells are about half of a storage system's equipment cost and the cathode is the largest cost inside the cell, so a US container with a Chinese cathode can fail the ratio. Provenance becomes a property with a price, set by how few alternatives exist — and in November 2025 the only other DOE-funded US LFP cathode plant was cancelled. Ask the learner why that is bad news as well as good for the survivor.

## Module 5 — Anatomy of a cathode plant

**Concepts:** tonnes to gigawatt-hours · capital cost per tonne · inputs · qualification lead time.

Take the Muskegon plant as the worked object. 15,000 tonnes a year at 2–2.5 kg per kWh is 6–7 GWh of cells — a fraction of one large cell plant, and the learner should say so before reading any headline about "doubling US capacity". Over USD 370 million for that capacity is roughly USD 25,000 per tonne-year; compare it with the USD 125 million reported two years earlier and ask whether that is a scope change or a cost overrun (the record does not say).

Then the inputs and the clock: lithium carbonate through a procurement agent, iron phosphate from a co-located partner, carbon, nitrogen, power; twelve to twenty-four months to build and as long again to qualify with customers, so a plant that breaks ground in 2027 sells spec-compliant volume in 2029 at the earliest.

**The point to install:** the ladder in Module 4 rewards a plant that exists; it pays nothing to one that is planned. Everything in the dossier's judgments follows from that.

## Module 6 — How a federal grant becomes a plant

**Concepts:** selected · obligated · outlaid · period of performance · cost share.

Three words that are routinely confused. Selected is a press release and creates no obligation. Obligated is a signed grant agreement that commits the money against milestones and is hard to cancel — cancelling means terminating a contract. Outlaid is money that left the Treasury against invoices.

Use the dossier's record: selected September 2024, obligated December 2024 for USD 100 million with a non-federal share of USD 260 million recorded, USD 2.25 million outlaid by July 2026, a recorded period of performance ending June 2026 with no visible extension. Have the learner explain why an award that had drawn 2% survived the 2025 cancellation rounds that killed peers, and why a period-of-performance end date without an extension is a question rather than a conclusion.

## Module 7 — Reading a private company from the outside

**Concepts:** Form D · amount sold versus amount targeted · implied valuation from a stake · silence as a signal · the Chesterton list.

A private US company owes the public a Form D when it sells shares under the common exemption: offering size, amount sold, investor count, first-sale date, directors and officers — and not price, valuation, revenue or profit. Three habits: add up what was sold across every filing rather than what was targeted; treat the absence of a later filing as the absence of evidence that a round closed; and triangulate through a listed counterparty's disclosure (USD 10 million for 3.3% implies a value near USD 300 million, labelled as arithmetic on someone else's number).

Then cadence. Nineteen press releases in three years and none since September 2024, with every material fact of 2025–2026 arriving through the SEC, a state government, a Korean shareholder or a reporter the company did not answer. Silence is not evidence of failure; it is evidence that the next fact will come from someone else and that status must be re-verified, not assumed.

**Practice:** build the Chesterton list from the dossier — the claims that travelled further than their sources ("joint venture", "confirmed as retained", "USD 242 million raised", "valued at USD 250 million") — and say for each who actually said it.

## Module 8 — The industry map, and where Mitra Chem sits in it

**Concepts:** upstream materials as an adjacent role · who buys cathode · who competes · why the transition matters economically.

Place the company on the chain. Above it: lithium producers (Albemarle) and phosphate chemistry (Sun Chemical). Beside it: the other pre-revenue US materials entrant on the anode side (NOVONIX), the Canadian pilot (Nano One), the Korean incumbent that is also its shareholder (L&F, supplying North America from Korea from 2026), and the exit (ICL). Below it: the cell makers who would buy — LG Energy Solution's Holland plant already making LFP storage cells with non-Chinese materials, Ultium Cells converting lines, American Battery Factory planning a plant — none of which has named Mitra as a supplier.

Close on the economics of the transition. If the material-assistance ladder holds, non-Chinese cathode is worth a premium that grows every year to 2029; if the ladder is repealed or the credits lapse, the premium disappears and the plant competes on cost against China, which it cannot. The whole investment case is a bet on a rule. That is a legitimate bet — most infrastructure is — and the learner should be able to say exactly which rule, which year, and which competitor already lost it.
