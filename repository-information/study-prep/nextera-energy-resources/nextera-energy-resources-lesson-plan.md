# NextEra Energy Resources — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** NextEra Energy Resources (the competitive generation arm of a holding company that also owns a regulated utility) · **Written:** 2026-09-07 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, plus the first buyer-side course in this corpus; no corporate-finance or utility-regulation background.

**What this teaches.** What changes about buying equipment and raising money when the buyer is very large — and specifically how two rules the first course states as though they were laws turn out to be facts about being small. Self-integration: buying cells and DC blocks rather than warranted systems, why access to scarce manufacturing capacity rather than margin is the real motive, and the interface risk that arrives with it. The seams, read as six concrete questions that get answered by one counterparty in one model and by several in the other. Forward booking as the purchase of a factory's calendar rather than of equipment, and why that converts a procurement decision into a position with inventory, working-capital and obsolescence consequences — including the odd result that a large owner sometimes appears in the market as a supplier to its competitors. The long-lead list, and the finding that the items gating a storage project are mostly ordinary grid equipment contested by every other connection on the network. The regulated-affiliate structure: what a rate base and an investment-grade parent buy a competitive developer, and the three costs — enforced separation, inherited regulatory risk, and internal competition for capital against a rate base with a better argument. The tax-appetite inversion, and how it reaches into the equipment decision by moving the eligibility gate inside the company. And a two-sided comparison of the very large owner against the specialist, in which several of the large owner's advantages turn out to be positional rather than earned.

**Why this gap.** The first course is emphatic that a buyer's counterparty is the integrator and that a developer cannot use its own investment tax credit, and both statements are true for almost every company in this corpus. Neither is true above a certain size, and nothing in the corpus explains the threshold or what crossing it costs. The owner-side guides elsewhere in the collection cover fund ownership, yieldcos, develop-and-sell and sponsor exits in depth; none covers the developer that sits inside a regulated utility group, which is a materially different animal.

**Its place in the portfolio-owner set.** Second of four. It takes the standardisation argument from the Plus Power guide and asks what the same scale does to procurement and capital, and it supplies the balance-sheet vocabulary the Terra-Gen guide uses when it asks who should hold a twenty-year obligation. It deliberately leaves the international dimension to the Lightsource bp guide.

## Module 1 — Buying cells instead of systems
What an integrator sells, in one paragraph, with the detail left to the integrator course. Three real motives for self-integration in ascending order: margin, control of the software layer that a supplier insolvency would otherwise orphan, and access — making your volume legible to the factory that allocates scarce capacity. The precondition, stated plainly: an engineering organisation large enough to determine, at its own cost, which supplier caused a shortfall.

## Module 2 — The seams
Six questions that arise on every plant and change owner rather than disappearing: missed energy, a defective cell batch, firmware and certification, who is the manufacturer of the assembled unit, spares across twenty years, and who is liable when it is genuinely unclear. Why one counterparty is worth a great deal when something is wrong and nothing at all when that counterparty is thin — and why self-integration is a good trade only for an organisation that can do the diagnosis itself.

## Module 3 — Buying the factory's calendar
Why the first course's late-ordering sequence is right for one project and impossible for a programme. Slot reservations, framework agreements and take-or-pay volume as the purchase of a calendar. The conversion of procurement into a position that can be right or wrong independently of any project. Inventory as working capital with an obsolescence clock, coverage above one as a deliberate bet, and surplus disposal into a competitor's supply chain as the correct response to a long position.

## Module 4 — The long-lead list
Order-to-delivery times for the items that actually gate a project. Two findings: the battery is usually not the long pole, and the two slowest items are shared with every other connection on the network, so their queue is not a storage queue and does not shorten when storage demand falls. Why this chart is the whole argument for forward booking.

## Module 5 — The regulated affiliate
Two businesses, two earning mechanisms — an allowed return on rate base against whatever a market pays. What the pairing buys: credit, permanence, tax capacity, shared scale. What it costs: codes of conduct and arm's-length dealing, because the standing risk is captive customers subsidising a competitive business; the regulated side's risks entering the group's credit; and a competitive arm bidding for capital against a rate base whose every prudent dollar earns an approved return for decades.

## Module 6 — The tax rule that scale inverts
Why a construction-heavy developer cannot absorb its own credit, and which kind of group can. What changes when it does — no discount, no partnership, no dependence on a buyer's quarterly appetite. The reach into engineering: eligibility diligence stops being a gate somebody else operates, which is faster and cheaper and moves the consequence of getting it wrong onto the group. The limit: tax capacity is finite and varies, so even large groups sell credits once it is used up.

## Module 7 — One battery, three owners
A fund with a life, a founder-owned developer funding its next project, and a regulated-utility affiliate with permanent capital. What each needs the asset to do and what follows in practice. The careful distinction from the dispatch guide's comparison: that one is about operating skill, this one about discount rates and horizons — and the two compound, because the cheapest capital and the best desk are rarely the same organisation.

## Module 8 — Large owner, specialist owner
Access before price, volume that underwrites a production line, fixed costs spread thin, and credit as a saleable product — against the obligation to keep buying, becoming the benchmark, being the obvious target of a rule change, and losing flexibility by necessity. The specialist's compensating freedom to take a position a large owner's process forbids, and why being acquired is its usual route into the other column.

## Pacing
Eight modules, roughly 15–20 minutes each. Modules 1 and 2 belong together and Modules 5 and 6 belong together; a reader short of time should take those two pairs. Module 4 is three minutes and changes how a schedule announcement reads. Module 7 is the one to hold in mind through the rest of the course, because it is the frame the closing guide returns to.
