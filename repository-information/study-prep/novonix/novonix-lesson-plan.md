# NOVONIX — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** the anode side of a lithium-ion cell — what graphite does, why making it is an energy problem, how a battery material gets qualified, and what happens to the economics when the policy scaffolding underneath a Western plant moves.
**Assumed starting point:** high-school STEM. The Albemarle plan covers the cathode side and the two are complementary, but neither depends on the other.
**What this is not:** a company crib sheet.

**Suggested pacing.** Seven modules. Modules 1–4 are the materials science and hold together as one session of about ninety minutes. Modules 5–7 are the commercial and policy mechanics; they are the more transferable half and are worth a separate sitting.

**The one sentence to hold onto:** the anode is the part of the cell nobody argues about, which is exactly why competition there is entirely about cost, consistency and where it is made.

---

## Module 1 — The electrode that never changed

**Concepts:** anode · cathode · intercalation · specific capacity.

Open with the asymmetry. Every battery chemistry argument — LFP against nickel-rich, cobalt content, sodium-ion — is an argument about the **cathode**. The anode has been graphite for thirty years.

Establish the mechanism: on charge, lithium ions move to the anode and **intercalate**, sliding into the gaps between graphite's stacked carbon sheets. Fully loaded the arrangement is one lithium per six carbons, which is where the theoretical 372 mAh/g comes from. Have the learner note that this is a *modest* number.

Then the real question: silicon stores roughly ten times as much, so why has it not won? Because graphite barely changes size while cycling — a few per cent — and silicon swells around 300%. A material that expands and contracts by that much every cycle grinds itself and everything touching it to powder.

**The reading rule to install:** treat every silicon-anode announcement as a question of *how much* silicon, not *whether*. Percentage additive, not replacement.

**The commercial consequence:** because both LFP storage cells and nickel-rich vehicle cells use graphite anodes, an anode supplier is exposed to total lithium-ion demand rather than to a chemistry bet. Ask the learner to contrast this with a cathode supplier's position.

## Module 2 — Natural and synthetic are different products

**Concepts:** natural graphite · synthetic graphite · petroleum coke · needle coke.

Teach these as two products, not two grades. The 2024 shipment split settles the question of which the industry prefers: of 2.11 million tonnes of anode material worldwide, artificial graphite was 83.2%.

**Natural:** mined, purified — typically by acid treatment, which is chemically messy — and rounded into spheres. Cheaper, lower embodied energy, more variable because every orebody differs, and that variability shows up as shorter cycle life in a hard-worked cell.

**Synthetic:** made by heating a carbon feedstock until its structure rearranges. Consistent because the process rather than the geology sets the structure. Expensive because the heat has to be paid for.

**Feedstock:** petroleum coke, a nearly pure solid carbon left at the bottom of an oil refinery. Cheap and abundant — which is why a graphite plant is often built near, or funded by, a refiner, and why a refiner turning up on the share register is a supply relationship rather than a portfolio decision. **Needle coke** is the premium grade whose carbon is already partly aligned, so it converts more readily; better material, tighter supply, higher price.

## Module 3 — The 3,000-degree step

**Concepts:** graphitisation · Acheson furnace · continuous processing · export controls on equipment.

The core of the module, and the core of the industry.

Petroleum coke is nearly pure carbon but *disordered* — small sheet fragments pointing every way, which lithium cannot move through usefully. Graphitisation heats it until the atoms have enough energy to rearrange into large, flat, aligned sheets.

Anchor the temperature: about 3,000°C, against roughly 1,500°C for melting steel. Sustaining twice the melting point of steel, in bulk, for days, is the whole cost. **The heat is effectively the product**, and the industry's economics are an energy-price argument dressed up as a materials one.

**The Acheson furnace**, essentially unchanged since the 1890s: pack the material in a long open trench, bury it in insulation, pass an enormous current directly through it for days, then wait days more to cool before unpacking. Have the learner identify the inefficiency before it is stated — it is a *batch* process, and each cycle heats the packing and insulation too, then throws that heat away.

**Continuous induction** as the alternative: material moves through continuously, heated by induction rather than by current through a packed bed. Three simultaneous gains in principle — heat-up energy spent once, throughput not gated by cooling, and steady process conditions instead of drift through a batch.

**The non-thermodynamic reason it matters.** Graphitisation furnaces, granulation machines and coating equipment are themselves subject to Chinese export controls. A furnace design from outside that chain is a *sourcing* claim as much as an efficiency claim — and sourcing rules that exclude prohibited foreign entities are what determine whether a customer's cells qualify for production credits. Make sure the learner sees that the technical choice and the policy position are the same choice.

## Module 4 — The first charge you never get back

**Concepts:** solid electrolyte interphase · first-cycle efficiency · tap density · impurity effects.

On the first charge, electrolyte in contact with the anode decomposes and forms a thin film across it — the **SEI**. It is electrically insulating but lets lithium ions through, so once formed it stops further decomposition. It is necessary.

The cost: forming it permanently locks up lithium. That is why a cell never returns quite all of its first charge, and the fraction it does return is the **first-cycle efficiency** — mid-90s of a per cent for good graphite.

Draw out why a fraction of a per cent is worth competing over: the lithium consumed is expensive and gone forever, so a higher figure means less lithium for the same delivered capacity, straight through to cost per kilowatt-hour.

Impurities hurt twice — they catalyse extra electrolyte decomposition, and metallic ones can eventually cause internal shorts. This is the practical reason a cell maker cannot switch anode suppliers on price: the material interacts with the electrolyte, the binder, the cathode and the formation protocol, and none of that transfers.

Introduce **tap density** briefly here: how much powder fits in a given volume once settled. An electrode has a fixed thickness, so denser powder puts more energy in the same cell.

## Module 5 — Qualification, and why a finished plant earns nothing

**Concepts:** qualification · pre-A / A / B / C samples · offtake agreement · conditions precedent.

The module that explains how a company can have a built factory, signed contracts and zero revenue.

Walk the ladder and, at each rung, ask what is being *proved* rather than what is being shipped:

| Stage | Size | Feedback | What is being proved |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-A | under 1 kg | under 2 months | that the chemistry works at all |
| A | 1–5 kg | under 2 months | that it works in *the customer's* cell design |
| B | 50–10,000 kg | ~6 months | that you can make it consistently |
| C | 5–30 t, on production equipment | 6–12 months | **that your factory can make it** |
| SOP | contract volumes | — | first revenue |

The C-sample is the conceptual key: the customer is qualifying the *plant*, not the powder, which is why the material must come off the real production line. Note that a six-to-twelve-month feedback window on the C stage alone sets a floor under how soon revenue can begin.

Then **offtake agreements**. For a plant that does not exist yet, the offtake is the document lenders read — which is why it is signed years before the first tonne. Teach the learner to read the *conditions*, not the volumes: qualification milestones by a date, compliance criteria, and — the one that bites a pre-revenue company hardest — a requirement to secure financing for the facility, failing which the customer may terminate.

*Worth dwelling on:* a terminated offtake is the clearest available evidence of how a qualification failure ends, and it is public information.

## Module 6 — When the customer is partly the government

**Concepts:** construction grant · Section 48C · 45X · conditional loan commitment · AD/CVD · tariffs.

A Western anode plant exists because policy is trying to make the economics work. Teach each instrument by **how it fails**, which is the part summaries omit:

- **Construction grant** — reimburses verified spending, usually against matching company funds. Paid in arrears, so it funds nothing up front; and an award can be *reviewed* after it is made.
- **Section 48C investment credit** — allocated in advance, earned when the plant is placed in service *by a deadline*. If construction is late, a certified credit simply lapses. This puts a construction schedule and a tax asset in direct conflict, and it is the mechanism by which conserving cash can cost more than it saves.
- **45X production credit** — pays per unit produced, so it is worth nothing to a plant that has not started. It also carries the sourcing tests that decide whether the *customer's* cells qualify, which is the supplier's real selling argument.
- **Conditional loan commitment** — very large, long-tenor, at a rate no private lender offers. But *conditional*: until conditions precedent are met, none of it is available. A company can hold a headline commitment and be unable to spend a dollar.
- **Trade duties** — the important structure. **Two independent agencies must both agree.** One sets the rates; the other must separately find the domestic industry is being harmed.
- **Tariffs** — blunter, unconditional, and set by policy that can change.

*Exercise:* given only the headline value of each instrument, have the learner rank them by how much cash they actually make available in the next twelve months. The ordering is usually the reverse of the headline ordering.

## Module 7 — Two mechanisms that decide outcomes

**Two structures worth learning properly, because both generalise far beyond this industry.**

**A trade case has two gates.** In February 2026 Commerce set final duties on Chinese anode material — 93.5% antidumping for named companies, 102.72% China-wide, 66.82–66.86% countervailing, above 160% stacked. Every rate was conditional on an injury finding. In March 2026 the trade commission voted 2-1 that the imports do not materially retard the establishment of a US industry, so **no order issued and none of the rates ever took effect**.

The consequence is legible in the accounts four months later: the assumed selling price in the company's own impairment model was cut from US$10–12/kg to US$7–11/kg, naming the Commerce ruling as the reason, against operating costs of US$6–8/kg. Have the learner compute what a 20–30% cut to assumed price does to a margin of that width. **The lesson: a two-gate process is not decided at the first gate, however loudly the first is announced.**

**A convertible floor price inverts.** A convertible debenture lets the lender take shares instead of repayment — attractive to a company with no revenue, because the debt may never need cash. The lender protects itself with a **floor price**: below it, the lender will not convert, and a clause instead requires the company to redeem part of the balance **in cash**, often repeating at intervals while the price stays low.

Make the learner articulate the inversion: a structure designed to avoid cash repayment becomes a cash demand precisely when the share price is lowest and equity hardest to raise. **The general rule: find the floor, and find what happens below it.** The conversion price is the upside and appears in the announcement; the floor clause is the downside and usually does not.
