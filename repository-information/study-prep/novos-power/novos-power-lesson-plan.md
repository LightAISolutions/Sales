## Module 1 — Reluctance and the gap
A coil on an iron or ferrite core pushes magnetic flux around the core the way a battery pushes current around a wire, and reluctance is the magnetic resistance. Iron and ferrite have very little; air has thousands of times more, so a spacer of a millimetre dominates the loop's total reluctance and the coil's inductance — turns squared over reluctance — falls in proportion to the gap. Teach why anyone weakens a magnetic circuit on purpose: a gapped core stores energy (flux across air costs energy; flux in iron almost none) and carries far more current before it saturates, which is what an inductor is for; a transformer passes energy through and wants the largest magnetizing inductance it can get, so it has no gap. Then the two numbers a gap moves: magnetizing inductance (falls — more current wasted setting up the flux) and leakage inductance (the field that links one winding but not the other, a small series inductor — a nuisance at 60 Hz, a design element at kilohertz). Everything a 'variable airgap' could mean lives in those two quantities.

## Module 2 — What a variable gap could do, and what Novos has not said
Inside every SST cell the isolation stage is some form of dual-active bridge; the power a given phase shift moves is inversely proportional to the inductance in series with the transformer. Small inductance: high peak power, coarse control and heavy circulating current at light load. Large inductance: fine light-load control, a ceiling on peak power. A fixed value is a factory compromise for one expected load — and a GPU hall offers none, stepping between light and full several times a second (DG Matrix guide, Module 4). An inductance that rises when light and falls when heavy would hold soft switching and control authority across the whole swing; a mechanically moved gap or a magnetically biased core are the two classical variable inductors, and both appear in the patent literature for resonant and dual-active-bridge converters. State plainly that this is the guide's reading, not a disclosure: the CTO's abstract says only 'a novel megawatt-scale, medium-voltage power conversion system based on a Variable Air Gap Solid-State Transformer architecture', no patent or paper is visible, and his published research is wireless power transfer — coupled coils across a large gap, the extreme case — which makes the reading plausible without confirming it. Two test questions for any future explanation: which inductance is varied and what actuates it; and what the variation buys that a wider switching-frequency range would not.

## Module 3 — What '1000x faster' would have to mean
Line up every element between the utility and the GPU by how it answers a load step. The 60 Hz transformer answers passively — its leakage inductance sags the voltage and nothing corrects it. A rectifier or UPS front end corrects through a control loop whose bandwidth is a fraction of its switching frequency (silicon IGBTs, low kilohertz): milliseconds, with DC-link capacitors covering the gap. A battery follows in milliseconds — fine for an outage, coarse for a waveform. A silicon-carbide SST cell switches at tens of kilohertz and corrects in tens of microseconds, its own capacitors covering the first microseconds. A capacitive sidecar at the rack answers the first pulse with stored charge and no loop at all. Work the pulse itself: 500 kW over 20 ms is 10 kJ, repeated several times a second, so at 1–50 MW the question is how much energy the unit holds and where. Then read the claim: 'faster than' a device with no loop is not a comparison, so the questions are the baseline (transformer, UPS loop or grid), the switching frequency, the closed-loop bandwidth, and whether the unit includes rack-side storage or ends at the hall edge.

## Module 4 — Air cooling at 13–48 kV: an insulation problem before a thermal one
Two things must be kept apart inside a medium-voltage converter: the semiconductors' heat and the voltage's electric field. Liquid cooling carries the heat away in a sealed loop and can help with the field, because a dielectric fluid or a potted cell tolerates a stronger field than open air; air cooling puts heat and field in the same medium, and air breaks down at roughly 3 kV per millimetre in clean, dry conditions, less with dust, humidity or altitude. Teach insulation coordination: the basic insulation level (95 kV at 15 kV class, 150 kV or more at 35 kV) sets the clearance through air and the creepage along surfaces, and those distances grow faster than the working voltage — which is why a 35 kV air-insulated cubicle is the size of a wardrobe and why cascaded-cell SSTs give each cell its own insulation system. So 'proprietary insulation' is the interesting phrase in SemiAnalysis's line, not 'air cooling': a design that held the stack apart in air at useful density would avoid the coolant loop, pump, leak risk and maintenance the liquid-cooled rivals carry. The evaluable questions: the design BIL, the insulation material, how the stack meets creepage and clearance at the rated kV, and the ambient and altitude ratings. Close on the odd number: 48 kV is above the 38 kV where UL 2877 stops and 46 kV is a subtransmission class, while the company's later post says '35 kV line' — read 48 kV as an envelope and 35 kV as the likely design point.

## Module 5 — Reading a pre-product company: the readiness ladder
The technology readiness level scale — NASA's nine rungs, used by DOE and defense buyers — exists to separate a proven principle (1–3) from a validated prototype (4–6) from a demonstrated and qualified product (7–8) and a system proven in operation (9). Place each piece of the record on the rung it actually proves. The CTO's seminar abstract is a formulated concept: TRL 2–3. Spec claims (≥98.5% peak efficiency, 1–50 MW, DC 400–1,500 V, 13–48 kV input) are targets, not measurements, and prove no rung. Pitch-competition wins (FedTech Crucible Ignitor; UC San Diego's Golden Ticket) and an IEEE showcase slot are commercial signals — a jury judges a pitch, not a unit. Lineage claims ('incubated by LACI and FedTech'; 'DOE and NASA-funded research') dissolve on inspection: LACI's cohort pages carry the CEO's prior company, and the DOE and NASA work is the CTO's general portfolio rather than an SST project. SemiAnalysis's 'reports peak efficiencies over 98%' is the company's briefing relayed by an analyst who thanks it in the acknowledgments. Two-to-ten employees, no round, a four-page site with an empty blog and a 'five generations' roadmap with no dates is pre-seed. Then the comparison that gives the ladder its scale: Heron (USD 140M, factory), Amperesand (USD 80M, Reno line) and DG Matrix (USD 60M, a shipping 480 V unit pre-certification) sit at TRL 5–7 and none has a listed medium-voltage unit — Novos must reach the rung the siblings reached two to three years and USD 60–140M ago. Teach the developer to build this table for any deep-tech pitch: evidence, rung proven, what would move it up.

## Module 6 — Where VASST would slot in, and what it would displace
Take the description at face value and trace the power path. A 13–35 kV feeder enters the unit directly, so the line-frequency transformer — the long-lead item behind the whole speed-to-power argument — is gone, as it is for Heron and Amperesand and is not for DG Matrix's shipping 480 V unit. A regulated DC output in 400–1,500 V feeds the 800 V DC bus NVIDIA has specified, so the rectifier and UPS are gone too. 'High redundancy and hot-swap capability' says modules replaceable live, as in every cascaded-cell design. At 1–50 MW the range spans a hall block to a small campus. The stated applications beyond the hall — EV fast charging, microgrids, storage, on-site generation, defense installations — are the list Amperesand and DG Matrix each carried before a data-center customer existed, because a medium-voltage-to-DC converter is a platform; FedTech's 'modular, rapid deployment at bases to deliver real-time voltage and frequency regulation during grid stress' is the microgrid version of the same box. What the record does not let anyone place is scale and form: no unit rating, cell count, enclosure, weight or footprint, so the unit cannot be compared with Heron Link's 4.2 MW block or DG Matrix's 400 kVA skid on any axis except claimed efficiency. Run the DG Matrix guide's datasheet exercise with the first column empty.

## Module 7 — The industry map: four startups, one roster, no listed unit
Who buys: the hall-edge block is specified by the hyperscaler or its design-build engineer and bought through the electrical contractor, and NVIDIA's 800 V DC lists are the shortlist most start from — the incumbents (ABB, Eaton, GE Vernova, Hitachi Energy, Mitsubishi Electric, Schneider Electric, Siemens, Vertiv) and one startup, Heron Power. Novos is on none, and appears in one analyst piece it briefed. Who competes: Heron owns roster placement and the largest raise; Amperesand the platform argument and a production line; DG Matrix the only shipping unit, at low voltage, with its medium-voltage product dated 2028+; all three name certification targets and none has completed a data-center SST listing; the incumbents hedge with their own solid-state programs and with the transformer-rectifier unit, which is what most 800 V halls will be built with first. Why the transition matters and why it is slow: the prize is the transformer lead time, the electrical room and two conversion stages of losses; the price is forty-year iron traded for actively controlled semiconductors with no field record, and a buyer who will not install an unlisted unit at scale needs a certified product — one to two years and millions per variant. What Novos would have to show to matter, in order: a topology and prototype rating; a measured efficiency curve; a certification target and an NRTL engagement; a pilot with a named counterparty; capital sufficient for a certification campaign. As of September 2026 the record contains none of the five.
