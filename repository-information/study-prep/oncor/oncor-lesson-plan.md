# Oncor — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach how electricity gets bought and delivered in a market where the utility that connects a data center owns no power plants — what a transmission-and-distribution utility is and what it earns a return on, why an energy-only market means nobody is obliged to build the supply, how a gigawatt of load actually connects and what the 2025 statute changed about that, why a Texas large-load figure is meaningless until you say whether it counts requests, approvals or metered load, how a wires rate case works with no fuel line in it, who pays for a substation against who pays for a backbone, why the state chose its highest transmission voltage for the Permian Basin, and — the question the three earlier utility plans never had to ask — who builds the generation and where storage enters when the utility is forbidden from buying either. Starting from high-school STEM and from the Dominion lesson plan, which this one does not repeat. No company trivia: founding dates, executives and share counts stay in the dossier. Generated 2026-09-03 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1). Companion: the in-app guide (Profiler → Oncor → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**Prerequisite.** The Dominion Energy lesson plan (`study-prep/dominion-energy/`) teaches the revenue-requirement equation, the IRP → CPCN → RFP sequence, the rate case, the anatomy of a large-load tariff, the interconnection study and the RTO layer with PJM as the example. Do that plan first. The Southern Company plan (the elected commission, the grid with no RTO) and the Entergy plan (the holding company, formula rates, the one-customer certificate) are useful second and third — this plan is the fourth regulatory story and the only one where the chain is genuinely cut in half rather than rearranged.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min — restructuring, and who decides what), Module 2 in one sitting (~30 min — the energy-only market and its consequences for a load that wants a date), Module 3 in one sitting (~45 min — the connection sequence, SB 6 clause by clause, and the three-numbers discipline), Module 4 in one sitting (~40 min — the wires rate case, the postage stamp, 4CP and the 765 kV decision), Module 5 last (~30 min — who builds generation, where storage enters, and Oncor against the chain), then the flashcard and self-test passes in the app.

## Module 1 — The wires-only utility: what is left when a state takes the utility apart

**The single idea:** restructuring split one company into three businesses, and the one that keeps the monopoly, the regulator and the rate base is the one that owns no power plants.

1. **What restructuring did.** Before 1999 a Texas utility looked like Dominion or Georgia Power: it owned the plants, the wires and the customer, and a commission decided what it could build and charge. The state then separated the three. Generation was sold to merchant companies competing in a wholesale market. The retail relationship went to competitive retail electric providers, which buy energy wholesale and resell it with a bill. What remained — poles, lines, substations, meters — became the regulated transmission and distribution utility, the TDU. Oncor is the largest, serving the Dallas–Fort Worth region and a band of Texas reaching the Panhandle and the Permian Basin.
2. **What that does to the revenue-requirement equation.** The Dominion plan wrote it as costs plus depreciation plus taxes plus rate base times the allowed return. Nothing about the equation changes; what changes is what is in the rate base. For a TDU it is wires only. There is no generating plant to certify, no fuel to buy, no purchased-power line and no prudence fight about a plant. Earnings grow by building transmission and distribution, and by nothing else — which is why a wires utility greets a gigawatt of new load the way a merchant generator greets a scarcity price. Every connection needs a substation, and a substation is rate base.
3. **Who decides what.** Five actors, and none of them is a utility that generates. The **commission** sets the wires utility's rates, issues the certificate for a new transmission line, and since 2025 writes the rules for connecting very large loads. The **grid operator** reviews large-load requests against the wider system, keeps the queue, plans the regional network, runs the market and orders emergency load shed. The **legislature** wrote the 2025 large-load statute and funds a public loan program for new gas plants. The **wires utility** studies the request, builds the substation, signs the interconnection agreement and earns on what it builds. The **retail providers and merchant generators** sell the energy and decide, privately, whether a plant is worth building.
4. **The row to memorize.** A regulated monopoly that connects the load, is obliged to deliver whatever energy someone else sells, and is forbidden from owning the generation. That combination is what Modules 2 and 5 are about.

**Self-check:** a campus signs a fifteen-year power contract in Texas. Name the counterparty for the energy, the counterparty for the delivery, and the body that approves the plant that will serve it. (A competitive retail provider or a generator directly; the wires utility; nobody — inside ERCOT no plant is certified.)

## Module 2 — The energy-only market: nobody is obliged to serve the load

**The single idea:** with no capacity market and no obligation to serve, supply arrives because scarcity prices make it worth building — which is a poor fit for a customer that needs megawatts on a date rather than at a price.

1. **Three market designs, side by side.** The Dominion plan taught PJM: a capacity market buys accredited capacity three years ahead, so the reserve margin is procured before it is needed. The Southern plan taught the other extreme: no market at all, a utility certifying its own reserve margin in a resource-plan docket. Texas is the third design. Generators are paid only for the energy they sell; when supply is tight an administrative curve pushes the price up toward a system-wide cap, and that scarcity revenue is the entire signal to build. Nobody buys capacity in advance and no commission finds a plant needed. The planning reserve margin is a number the operator publishes, not a duty anyone holds.
2. **Why that matters to a gigawatt of load.** In Virginia or Georgia the utility that connects the load also has to serve it, and the plant that serves it is certified against that obligation. In Texas the wires utility must deliver whatever the customer's retail provider has bought, and whether a plant exists to sell it is answered by a price. A campus arriving in a tight region raises the scarcity price for everyone, which is supposed to summon the next plant — and, when new gas takes four years and every other campus is arriving on the same clock, the state has intervened twice: a public loan fund for new dispatchable generation, and a statute that treats the largest loads themselves as a reserve the operator may call on.
3. **Where storage comes from here.** A grid battery in ERCOT earns on energy arbitrage and on ancillary services the operator buys — regulation, responsive reserve, the newer fast-frequency products. Those revenues built the largest battery fleet outside California with no certificate anywhere in the story, and the fleet is now big enough that the ancillary markets it grew up on have saturated, pushing new projects toward energy revenue and longer durations. Note what this means for the seller: the buyer is a developer or an IPP making a market bet, and the decision is made at their bid stage against a price forecast, not in a docket.
4. **The load as a resource.** An energy-only market runs closer to its margin by design. The state's answer for the largest customers is to make them part of the reserve — a customer with backup generation can be told to run it, and one without can be told to curtail, before the operator cuts power to homes. That turns a campus's electrical design into a compliance question: how fast it can shed, how much on-site generation it has, how much storage it carries to ride through a call.

**Self-check:** why is there no capacity market clearing price to quote a Texas developer, and what replaces it as the signal to build? (The market is energy-only; scarcity pricing during tight hours, administratively supported up to an offer cap, is the entire investment signal.)

## Module 3 — Connecting a gigawatt: the sequence, the statute, and the three numbers

**The single idea:** the connection process is the whole regulated story in ERCOT, and in 2025 the state rewrote its terms because free requests had produced a queue nobody could plan against.

1. **The sequence.** A request to the wires utility naming site, megawatts and an in-service date, with proof of site control and a study fee. A screening study and a system impact study: what the load does to the network, which lines and transformers overload, what substation and feed it needs, and what the upgrades cost. Submission to the grid operator, which studies it against the wider transmission system and, since 2026, in batches. An interconnection agreement, with security posted and curtailment terms accepted — this is the point at which a request becomes a signed agreement in anyone's count. A certificate for any new line the corridor needs. Construction of the substation and feed, against transformer backlogs. Then approval to energize, and a phased ramp hall by hall.
2. **What the 2025 statute changed, clause by clause.** A threshold at 75 MW defining the class. A study fee of at least $100,000, scaled in the proposed rule to $300,000 above 250 MW. Financial commitment — security, contributions in aid of construction, advance payments — which the proposed rule prices at $50,000 per MW of security plus a non-refundable $50,000 per MW interconnection fee, with forfeited security split 80 percent to the transmission provider's rate base and 20 percent refunded on withdrawal. Disclosure of whether the same load has asked other utilities. Disclosure of on-site backup generation capable of half the site's demand, with fuel, run-hours and ramp time. Authority for the operator to direct deploy-or-curtail. A requirement that the curtailment equipment be installed before interconnection, for loads connecting after the end of 2025. A competitively procured demand-reduction service for loads of 75 MW and up. Commission review of co-location arrangements pairing an existing generator with a new load. And an order to re-examine the transmission cost allocation by the end of 2026.
3. **Read the statute and the rule as one instrument.** The statute sets the powers; the implementing rule sets the prices. The rule was published for comment in March 2026 and had not been adopted by the date of this plan — so every figure in the previous paragraph beyond the statutory minimum is proposed, not final. That distinction is exactly the kind of thing a seller quoting a fee schedule needs to get right.
4. **The three numbers.** This is the discipline the whole plan exists to install. A **request** is a form. An **approval to energize** is the operator's finding that the system can carry the load. **Observed peak** is what connected loads actually drew. In 2026 the operator was tracking requests in the high hundreds of gigawatts, had approved about nine gigawatts to energize, and observed under four gigawatts of actual peak from those approved loads — against an all-time system peak in the low nineties. The gap is structural rather than dishonest: developers submit at several utilities to keep options open, campuses energize in halls over years, and a request describes a build-out decade. But it means the request count measures interest, not demand.
5. **What the state did about the gap.** The fee, the security and the duplicate-request disclosure make a request cost something. The batch process replaces serial studies with clustered ones so a corridor's requests are modelled against each other. And in 2026 the state paused the process outright to audit the projects moving through it — the strongest possible statement that the number had stopped being trusted.

**Self-check:** you are handed "the Texas data-center pipeline is 438 gigawatts." What three questions do you ask before using it? (Is that requests, approvals or metered load? Whose count — one utility's queue, the operator's tracked total, or a batch-eligible subset? And as of when, given the queue tripled in a year?)

## Module 4 — The wires rate case, the postage stamp, and the backbone

**The single idea:** a wires utility's cost of service has no fuel line and three mechanisms that carry new investment into bills between cases — and the transmission half of it is paid for by everyone in the market, which is why Texas builds backbone faster than regions that argue about who benefits.

1. **The case itself.** Narrower than Dominion's and rarer: it sets the transmission-and-distribution cost of service on a historical test year, and the utility then lives on that base for years. What it resets is the important part — the allowed return, the capital structure and the depreciation rates that every interim mechanism afterwards uses. It is also the one docket where the cities the utility serves, the industrial customers and the consumer advocate can argue the whole picture at once. Oncor's 2025 case is the worked example in the dossier: filed asking for roughly $834 million and a 10.55 percent return, settled and ordered in April 2026 at about a $560 million increase and a 9.75 percent return, with new rates effective that June.
2. **The three mechanisms between cases.** *Transmission cost of service*: every transmission owner has a TCOS the commission sets, recovered not from its own customers but from every distribution utility in the state, and updatable between rate cases as lines enter service. *The distribution cost recovery factor*: an annual filing under a statutory formula recovering new distribution plant. *The system resiliency plan*: a multi-year hardening, wildfire-mitigation, flood and cyber package approved up front and recovered through its own rider. Between them most of the capital plan reaches rates without a contested case, so earnings track rate base with months of lag rather than years.
3. **The postage stamp and 4CP.** ERCOT pools the cost of all transmission and allocates it across all load in proportion to each utility's share of the four summer coincident peaks. Two consequences. A line built in West Texas is paid for by customers in Dallas and Houston — nobody has to prove who benefits, which is why the state builds backbone quickly. And a flat, always-on load draws its full share at every one of the four peak intervals, so it pays a full share — which gives on-site storage that shaves precisely those intervals a direct, calculable value, and is why the statute ordered the whole allocation re-examined.
4. **The 765 kV decision.** Transmission voltage is a throughput choice: the state's workhorse has been 345 kV, and 765 kV carries several times the power over the same right-of-way with lower losses per megawatt-mile, at higher cost per mile and with long-lead transformers from a short list of manufacturers. In 2025 the commission chose it for the Permian Basin — three import paths, roughly $17.2 billion for the extra-high-voltage lines plus several billion in local improvements, anchoring a statewide expansion of thousands of miles and a figure in the thirties of billions.
5. **Who builds it, and what that costs politically.** Each line is a separate certificate case with landowners as parties. Oncor took the two western segments — approved in August 2026 at roughly $2.24 billion and $1.70 billion for 242.6 and 181.6 miles — after organized landowner opposition, a finding that about 1,650 additional property owners had not been notified of route changes, and public calls from senior state officials to deny the applications pending legislative reform. Another segment is a fifty-fifty build with a river authority; the segment reaching south went to a different utility with a municipal partner; a four-sponsor eastern backbone extends the network toward the coast.
6. **The seller's read.** Extra-high-voltage transformers, breakers, series compensation and terminal substations are ordered years ahead against manufacturer backlogs — the transformer supply chain and a transmission plan are the same story told from two ends. And because the recovery runs through TCOS on the postage stamp, the backbone reaches bills within months of energizing, which is the mechanism the state's own critics attack when they argue the same reliability could be bought by building generation near the load instead.

**Self-check:** a 765 kV line energizes in West Texas in December. When does it start earning, and from whom? (Within months, through an interim transmission cost-of-service update, recovered from every load in the market on the postage-stamp rate — not from the customers nearest the line.)

## Module 5 — Who builds the generation, where storage enters, and Oncor against the chain

**The single idea:** the rung that does not exist is the one the earlier plans spent the most time on — and mistaking its absence is the classic error.

1. **The chain, rung by rung, with the strikeouts.** *The plan:* no generation IRP; the equivalents are the operator's regional transmission plan and long-term load forecast plus the utility's own capital plan — read for corridors and substations, not purchase orders. *The customer contract:* an interconnection agreement with the wires utility plus a separate retail contract, two counterparties and two documents; the campus's electrical design is settled here. *The certificate:* a CCN for a transmission line only. *The solicitation:* none — this is the rung that does not exist. *The rate order:* a wires cost of service plus the three interim mechanisms. *Interconnection:* the large-load process with its fees, security and batching, plus a separate generator queue for merchant plants and batteries. *The regional layer:* an energy-only market and a postage-stamped transmission plan.
2. **Who actually builds supply.** Four kinds of company, all of them already in this corpus. The merchant developer building a gas plant or battery on expected market prices and hedges. The independent power producer signing a PPA or tolling agreement with the campus and building against it. The hyperscaler or its developer building behind the meter — turbines, engines, fuel cells, storage inside the fence, connected as a private use network or not at all. And the bridge-power vendors whose entire market is the gap between a campus's opening date and the grid's.
3. **Where storage enters.** Two sales, neither at the utility. Utility-scale: the counterparty is the developer or IPP, and the decision is made at their bid stage against a market forecast. On-site: the counterparty is the campus and its EPC, and the decision is made in the electrical design — sized to ride through the operator's curtailment calls, to shave the four coincident peaks that set the transmission charge, and to buffer the load swings the operator has begun to regulate. One narrow exception exists: the state allows a wires utility to lease or procure a small amount of storage for reliability where a wires solution cannot arrive in time, and every such proposal is argued as market intrusion.
4. **The scale, in context.** The generator queue tells its own story: roughly 2,000 active interconnection requests totalling about 453 GW, of which storage is the single largest category at about 178 GW, ahead of solar at about 163 GW, gas at about 61 GW and wind at about 48 GW. Against that, a wires utility's own storage authority is a rounding error — which is the point.
5. **Where it fails, in one line each.** The date moves, and here nobody is even obliged to build the supply behind it. The queue is not the load. The operator can call the load itself. The allocation reopens by statute. The backbone arrives after the campuses it was planned for, which is the entire market for bridge power. And the last one: sold to the wrong desk — the wires utility buys transformers, breakers, conductor and substation packages, and never a battery, a turbine or a fuel cell.

**Self-check:** a battery seller asks for a meeting with Oncor's resource planning team to discuss its storage RFP. What is wrong with the request, and where should the meeting be? (There is no resource plan and no storage RFP; the utility may not own generation. The meeting is with the merchant developer or IPP bidding a project into the market, or with the campus and its EPC for a behind-the-fence system.)

## Whole-plan self-test

1. What does a transmission and distribution utility own, and what does it earn a return on? (Poles, lines, substations and meters; its rate base is wires alone.)
2. In an energy-only market, what replaces a capacity auction as the signal to build a plant? (Scarcity pricing during tight hours, supported administratively up to an offer cap.)
3. Name the three large-load numbers and say which one has ever bought equipment. (Requests, approvals to energize, observed metered load — only the last.)
4. What is the study fee and security structure the proposed Texas rule sets? (Study fees of $100,000 for 75–250 MW and $300,000 above it plus actual costs; $50,000 per MW of security and a non-refundable $50,000 per MW interconnection fee, with forfeited security split 80/20.)
5. What must be installed before a qualifying large load interconnects, and why? (Equipment enabling remote curtailment — an authority without the physical means to exercise it is unenforceable.)
6. Explain the postage-stamp rate and name one thing it makes easy and one thing it makes contentious. (Transmission cost pooled and allocated across all load: easy to build backbone without proving who benefits; contentious because a household pays for a line built for a distant cluster.)
7. Why does a flat load pay a full share of transmission cost, and what does that make valuable? (It draws at full strength during all four coincident peaks; storage that shaves precisely those intervals.)
8. What does a CCN certify in ERCOT, and what does a CPCN certify in Virginia or Georgia? (A transmission line's route and need; a generating plant into rate base.)
9. Which three mechanisms carry a wires utility's new investment into rates between full cases? (Interim transmission cost of service, the distribution cost recovery factor, the system resiliency plan rider.)
10. Who are the four kinds of company that build generation for a Texas data center, and which one is the wires utility? (Merchant developer, IPP, the campus behind its own fence, and bridge-power vendors — none of them is the wires utility, which is forbidden from owning generation.)
