## Module 1 — The seconds belong to the UPS: what "uninterruptible" has to mean

**The single idea:** the UPS owns the interval nobody else can — from the last milliseconds a server can coast on its own capacitors to the moment a generator is carrying the load — and everything about the machine follows from having to fill that interval *without a decision*.

1. **Where the UPS sits on the clock.** A server power supply rides roughly 10–20 ms on its capacitors — its **hold-up time**. A standby engine needs about ten seconds to crank, stabilize and take load, and the standard for emergency systems (**NFPA 110**) classes the usual data-center system by that ten-second **transfer time**. Between those two numbers is a gap three orders of magnitude wide that only stored energy can fill. The UPS is that stored energy plus the machinery to deliver it as clean AC — and, in practice, it also owns the *minutes* an operator wants in hand before trusting that the engine will start.
2. **Three things the machine must therefore do.** (a) Deliver the full load *instantly*: there is no time to detect, decide and switch, so the store must already be "on the bus" or the switching must be finished inside the hold-up window. (b) Produce a waveform the servers accept — voltage, frequency and shape within tight limits — regardless of what the supply upstream is doing. (c) Be removable for service without dropping the load, which means a path *around* it: the **static bypass**, automatic through a solid-state switch when the unit overloads or fails, manual through a maintenance bypass when a technician needs it out. **While the load is on bypass it is unprotected.** That sentence is the origin of the first row in the failure map.
3. **The classification that makes promises comparable.** **IEC 62040-3** grades a UPS by how much of the supply's misbehavior reaches the load: *VFD* (voltage and frequency dependent — the supply passes straight through until it fails and a switch acts), *VI* (voltage independent — the voltage is corrected but the frequency is the grid's), *VFI* (voltage and frequency independent — the load is fully isolated from the supply). Data centers buy VFI. A full code such as VFI-SS-111 adds letters for the output waveform in normal and stored-energy modes and digits for how far the output may deviate during a mode change, a linear load step and a non-linear load step — so a rotary machine and a static unit can be held to the same yardstick.
4. **The dial, in one paragraph.** A static UPS in **double conversion** rectifies everything to DC and rebuilds it as AC continuously — the load never sees the raw supply, and the price is a few percent of every megawatt-hour as heat. **Eco-mode** runs the load on the raw supply through the bypass with the conversion idling, lifting efficiency toward 98–99 %, and bets that the unit can detect a failure and transfer back inside the hold-up time for *every* kind of fault. The Eaton guide teaches the debate; carry two facts forward from it: efficiency and protection trade against each other in a static machine, and a rotary machine sits at a different point on the dial by construction — it conditions the supply all the time through a spinning mass rather than through switching.
5. **Sizing words.** UPS ratings are quoted in kVA and kW; the ratio is the **power factor** the machine is designed for, and modern IT loads run near unity, so the kW number is the one that matters. **Autonomy** is how long the store carries rated load — seconds for a flywheel, minutes for batteries — and it is *insurance*, not runtime: it is sized to beat the generator's start with margin, and a longer autonomy is protection against the engine failing its first attempt, bought in cabinets and floor space.

**Self-check:** a hall dropped for 40 ms while a UPS module was being swapped and one row of servers rebooted. Name the mechanism. (The load was on bypass — raw supply, no stored seconds — when the supply dipped for longer than the hold-up time. Nothing failed except the assumption that maintenance mode is safe mode.)

## Module 2 — Two ways to store the seconds: chemistry or spinning steel

**The single idea:** the UPS problem is a *power* problem wearing an *energy* costume. The amount of energy needed is tiny; the rate at which it must be delivered is enormous; and the two stores available — a battery string and a flywheel — are opposite personalities on exactly that axis.

**Do the arithmetic once.** Energy is power multiplied by time. Carrying a 1 MW hall for 15 seconds needs 1 MW × 15 s = 15 MJ, which is about 4.2 kWh — the energy in a few laptop batteries' worth of cells, delivered at the rate of a small town's demand. Carrying the same hall for ten minutes needs forty times more, about 170 kWh, before the margins engineers add for aging, temperature and end-of-discharge voltage. So the *seconds* store and the *minutes* store are different objects: the first is sized by power and the second by energy.

**The battery.** A string of cells on the UPS's **DC link** — for decades **VRLA** lead-acid, increasingly lithium-ion (**LFP** for its thermal stability). Its personality: energy is cheap per kilowatt-hour so minutes are affordable; power is limited by how fast the chemistry can be drawn down (the **C-rate**), so a seconds-only battery is oversized for its energy; every discharge and every warm day costs **cycle life** and calendar life; **state of health** has to be tested rather than read; and a room of cells is a fire-code object under **NFPA 855** with spacing, suppression and ventilation. It fails gradually and then suddenly — one weak cell in a series string limits the string. Its gift is the minutes: time for an operator to confirm the generators, to attempt a second start, or to shut the hall down in order.

**The flywheel.** Energy stored as rotation: **E = ½ I ω²** — half the moment of inertia (how much mass, how far from the axis) times angular speed squared. Doubling the speed quadruples the energy, which is why one design school spins a small composite rotor at tens of thousands of rpm in a vacuum on **magnetic bearings**, and the other spins a heavy steel mass at the synchronous machine's own speed and simply accepts the friction. Its personality is the mirror image of the battery: huge power for a short time (energy divided by seconds, not hours), essentially unlimited cycles, no chemical aging, no temperature sensitivity worth mentioning, and a **state of charge** you read directly from the tachometer — if it is spinning at speed, it is full. Its cost is that the seconds are all you get, that the rotor must be kept spinning (a standing loss), and that it is a precision rotating machine with bearings and a maintenance calendar.

**Which seconds do you actually need?** This is the real design question, and honest engineers give two answers. *Answer one:* about ten to fifteen seconds with margin — enough to cover a generator start whose reliability is engineered high (redundant starters, a second engine already cranking, or a diesel on the same shaft as the store), so autonomy beyond that buys nothing but cost. *Answer two:* several minutes — because the first start sometimes fails, because a fuel or control fault presents late, and because an orderly shutdown of a hall is worth more than an abrupt one. The industry's split between flywheel-backed and battery-backed UPS is, at bottom, a split between these two answers. A hybrid — a flywheel to take the hit and a small battery behind it — exists precisely because each answer is right about something.

**Consequences you can predict from the physics.** No battery room and no fire-code review for a flywheel, but a rotating machine to inspect. No replacement cycle for the store, but a bearing cycle. A store that is *always* proven (rpm on the display) versus one that must be discharge-tested. A footprint that stays constant against one that grows with the minutes bought. And on a hot day in a hot climate, a store that does not care against one whose life halves for every ten degrees.

**Self-check:** a design brief asks for 1 MW of UPS with 30 seconds of stored energy and asks whether a battery or a flywheel is "bigger". Answer in energy and in power. (Energy: 30 MJ ≈ 8.3 kWh — trivially small for either. Power: 1 MW — the battery must be sized for a very high C-rate and will carry far more energy than the brief needs; the flywheel is sized for exactly this. Neither answer is "bigger" — they are answers to different questions.)

## Module 3 — Static and rotary: the anatomy of the two machines, and the diesel on the shaft

**The single idea:** a static UPS makes clean power by *switching* — chopping DC into a sine wave thousands of times a second — while a rotary UPS makes it by *spinning a magnet*. Everything they differ on (waveform, fault current, efficiency curve, voltage class, maintenance, failure mode) follows from that one difference.

**The static UPS.** Three **conversion stages** in a row: a **rectifier** turns the supply into DC; the **DC link** carries it, with the battery hanging on the same bus so it can take over without any switching event; an **inverter** built on **IGBT**s (or, in the newest units, **SiC** switches) chops the DC back into AC. Around them: the **static bypass**, the controls, and fans. It is built at **low voltage** (400 or 480 V), so a large hall needs many units and a transformer above them. It is modular — power modules of a few hundred kilowatts hot-swapped into a frame — and it scales by paralleling frames to a few megawatts and then multiplying systems. Its efficiency is very high at full load in **double conversion** (mid-to-high nineties) and higher still in **eco-mode**, and it is *lowest at part load*, where the fixed losses of switching and magnetics dominate — which matters because redundancy parks units at half load. Its output is a synthesized sine wave; its **fault current** is limited to a little above its rating by the very switches that make it, so protection downstream of a static UPS has to be designed around a *soft* source; and its store is a battery room with everything Module 2 said about one.

**The rotary UPS.** A **synchronous machine** — an AC machine whose rotor turns in exact step with the grid frequency, 1,500 rpm for a four-pole machine at 50 Hz and 1,800 rpm at 60 Hz — fed from the supply through a **coupling choke**, with the critical load connected at the machine's terminals and a **flywheel** on or coupled to its shaft. In normal operation the machine runs as a motor and, at the same time, as a **synchronous condenser** for the load: its **excitation** is adjusted continuously to hold the output voltage, it supplies the reactive power the load needs so the utility sees a corrected **power factor**, and because the load is fed from a spinning magnet through a large inductance rather than straight from the wires, **harmonics** on the supply do not reach the load and the load's own distortion does not reach the supply. When the supply fails or sags, the choke-side breaker opens and the flywheel drives the machine as a generator: the output is a sine wave *by construction*, the machine delivers **fault current** several times its rating for the first cycles from its stored magnetic energy, and there is nothing to switch because the machine was already feeding the load. Its costs are the mirror image of its gifts: bearings and a maintenance calendar, standing losses that make it less efficient than the best static units especially at part load, noise and mass, and a store that gives seconds only. Its structural advantage is that a wound machine can be built for **medium voltage** directly, which is where the largest campuses want their UPS to sit.

**The diesel on the shaft — DRUPS.** Put a diesel engine on the same shaft line as the rotary machine, joined by a clutch, and the flywheel now covers the *engine's* start rather than a separate generator's. Normal operation is the rotary UPS above. On a supply failure the flywheel takes the load instantly, the engine starts and runs up to speed in a few seconds, the clutch engages, and the engine is carrying the hall well inside the flywheel's window — with no **ATS**, no separate **gen-set**, no synchronizing of a remote engine onto the bus, and no battery. When the supply returns, the machine is **synchronized** back to it, the choke breaker closes, the clutch releases and the engine cools and stops. One machine does the work of UPS, transfer switch and generator for its block, which is the whole appeal at forty-megawatt scale. The price is that the engine is now *in* the critical path: the flywheel's seconds are the entire margin for a start, the clutch is a mechanical element that must engage under load, the engine carries an emergency-class air permit like any standby diesel, and taking the machine out for service means the load goes to bypass or to the ring in Module 4.

| Dimension | Static UPS | Rotary UPS | Diesel rotary UPS (DRUPS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| How the clean wave is made | Switching (IGBT / SiC inverter) | Rotating magnet (synchronous machine) | Rotating magnet |
| Stored energy | Battery string on the DC link — minutes | Flywheel — seconds | Flywheel — seconds, covering the engine's start |
| Fault current | A little above rating (switch-limited) | Several times rating for the first cycles | Several times rating |
| Harmonic isolation | Depends on mode; the rectifier itself is a harmonic load | Inherent — the choke and the machine decouple supply and load | Inherent |
| Voltage class | Low voltage; transformer above it | Low or medium voltage | Low or medium voltage |
| Efficiency shape | Highest at full load; falls at part load; eco-mode higher still | High and flat-ish but with standing losses; worst at light load | As rotary, plus the engine is idle in normal operation |
| Store maintenance | Battery replacement cycle; fire code; SOH testing | Bearings and rotor inspection; SOC is rpm | Bearings plus a diesel to test and permit |
| What else you still buy | Gen-sets, ATS, paralleling gear, battery room | Gen-sets, ATS, paralleling gear | Nothing for the block — engine, transfer and UPS are one machine |
| Failure signature | Load on bypass; eco-mode transfer that misses; a weak string | Bearing or excitation fault; the ring as a shared element | Engine fails to start or clutch to engage inside the flywheel's seconds |

**Self-check:** why can a rotary UPS feed a downstream protection scheme that a static UPS of the same rating cannot? (A rotating machine dumps several times its rated current into a short circuit from its stored magnetic field, so the nearest breaker sees a large fault and trips fast; an inverter's switches cannot survive that and limit the current to a little above rating, so selective coordination downstream must be designed around a soft source.)

## Module 4 — Redundancy without doubling: topologies, the isolated-parallel bus, and the stiff source

**The single idea:** a UPS block's reliability is decided by how the units are *arranged* at least as much as by how good each unit is — and the arrangements differ in whether the redundancy lives in a switching event that must work or in a bus that shares by physics.

**The vocabulary.** *N* is the equipment the load needs. **N+1** adds one spare. *2N* duplicates everything down two independent paths, and 2(N+1) duplicates with a spare in each — the most expensive and the most forgiving. **Uptime Tier** III demands **concurrent maintainability**: any component can be taken out for planned work without dropping the load, which is a statement about paths, not about equipment quality. Tier IV demands fault tolerance — survive any single *unplanned* failure.

**Two cheaper-than-2N families that live in a switch.** In a *block-redundant* or **catcher** design, several UPS systems each feed their own block and one spare system stands ready; if a system fails, static transfer switches move its block onto the catcher. In a **distributed redundant** design ("three-to-make-two"), each load is fed from two of several systems through its own dual-input equipment, so any one system can fail. Both reach N+1 for less than doubling. Both place the redundancy in a switching event — a static transfer switch or a load's input selector — that must detect the failure and act inside the hold-up time, every time.

**The isolated-parallel bus — redundancy by physics.** Join the UPS units in a *ring* through **coupling chokes**. In normal operation each unit feeds its own load through its own path, exactly as if it stood alone — the units are *isolated* from each other's loads. If one unit fails, the ring lets its load flow to *all* the other units at once, each taking a small share through its choke — the units are *parallel* when they need to be. There is no dedicated spare to switch to and no switching event: the choke ring shares by impedance the instant a unit stops contributing, and the surviving units each pick up a fraction rather than one of them picking up everything. One spare unit across a very large block gives N+1; the block can be tens of megawatts on one bus; and a fault on one unit's output is limited by the chokes from pulling the whole ring down. This is the shape in which rotary UPS is sold at scale, and it is the idea most worth being able to draw from memory: a circle of machines, a choke between each pair, each with its own load hanging below it. Its exposures are the ones any shared element has — the ring itself and its controls are common to every unit on it, so the protection and the operating procedure for the ring are where the engineering effort goes.

**Why the stiff source matters downstream.** **Selective coordination** — arranging breakers so a fault opens only the nearest device upstream — depends on the fault current being large enough, fast enough, to separate the breakers' time-current curves. A rotating machine is a *stiff* source: it delivers a large **fault current** for the first cycles from its stored magnetic energy, so downstream protection behaves as it would on utility power. An inverter is a *soft* source that limits current to protect its own switches, so on a static UPS the fault-current picture changes the moment the load is on the inverter rather than on bypass, and the coordination study must cover both states. The same stiffness is why a rotary machine's output waveform barely notices a large non-linear load stepping on or off, and why its **power conditioning** — voltage, harmonics, power factor — is continuous rather than a mode.

**The voltage-class argument.** A UPS at **low voltage** needs a transformer above it and many units per hall. Moving the UPS to **medium voltage** deletes a **conversion stage** and lets a single machine cover a much larger block — one of the live arguments in gigawatt campuses. A wound synchronous machine can be built for medium voltage directly, which is the rotary family's natural home at scale; the static family's answer is a solid-state MV UPS with its own isolating reactor and coupling transformer, taught in the ABB guide. Both are ways of saying the same thing: at a hundred megawatts, the UPS is becoming substation equipment.

**Self-check:** draw an isolated-parallel ring of five units and then remove one. Where does its load go, through what, and what did *not* have to happen for that to work? (To the other four, a quarter each, through the chokes; nothing had to detect, decide or switch — the sharing is impedance, not logic. Now name the shared element: the ring and its protection.)

The trade is dependence: Piller's AI-era volume is a function of Bergen's engine sales, of the permits those plants can get (Vineland), and of whether campuses keep buying a flywheel and a battery together or decide one will do.
