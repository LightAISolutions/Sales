# Pinnacle West (APS) — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach how a regulated utility serves a data-center boom by buying capacity it does not own — the tolling agreement and the all-source RFP as the utility's battery specification; what a load-factor tariff is and why a flat load earns its own rate schedule; what a formula rate changes and why every consumer party in Arizona opposes it; how to read a committed and an uncommitted queue against a system peak; what converting a retired coal unit to gas buys and does not buy; how degree days, weather normalization and purchased power explain a quarter; and what relicensing a nuclear plant to 80 years removes from a capital plan — starting from high-school STEM. ## Module 1 — The toll buyer

**The single idea:** a tolling agreement gives the utility the right to charge and discharge a battery it does not own, for a fixed fee, for twenty years — so the utility signs a contract, and the developer signs the battery purchase order.

1. **The instrument.** Under a toll the developer builds, finances, owns and operates the plant; the toller pays a fixed fee and decides when the battery charges and discharges. The toller takes the market risk (the value of the energy it shifts), the owner takes the availability risk (the plant must be there when called). A power purchase agreement with storage sells energy and capacity instead; build-transfer moves ownership to the utility at completion; self-build keeps it from day one. Four routes, four owners, four buyers of the equipment.
2. **The procurement.** APS runs an all-source RFP every year or two — 2022, 2023, 2025 — and signs the winning bids. From the 2023 solicitation it signed about 7,300 MW: roughly 2,650 MW of solar, 3,610 MW of storage, 500-plus MW of wind and 520 MW of gas. Of the storage, about 150 MW (Agave) is APS-owned. The rest is tolls and PPAs with Recurrent Energy (Papago, 1,200 MWh), Strata Clean Energy (Scatter Wash, Justice, White Tank) and developers not yet named. The 2025 RFP's awards were still being negotiated in August 2026.
3. **The specification.** APS never publishes a battery datasheet; it publishes RFP terms. The 2023 RFP asked for 365 equivalent cycles a year at a 50 percent average state of charge — one full cycle a day, resting half-full — and those two numbers are what the bidder's warranty, augmentation plan and financing model have to cover. A seller reading an APS toll is reading the spec.
4. **Why the toll is bankable.** GridStor bought White Tank (100 MW / 400 MWh) from Strata with the toll attached and closed $220 million of financing in September 2026. The lenders priced twenty years of payments from an investment-grade utility that recovers the toll in rates — not a merchant revenue forecast. That is why Strata's business, as its own dossier records, is to win the toll and sell the project with the contract on.

**Self-check:** APS has 3,610 MW of storage under contract and 150 MW in rate base. A seller wants the next gigawatt. Who is the account? (The developers bidding into the 2025 all-source RFP — the toll structure puts the OEM decision with them at bid stage.)

## Module 2 — The load-factor tariff

**The single idea:** load factor is average demand divided by peak demand; a load that runs flat uses the plant sized to its peak every hour, so it is cheaper to serve per kilowatt-hour and earns its own rate schedule.

1. **The physics of cost.** Plants and wires are sized to a customer's peak; a customer at 30 percent load factor uses that capacity a third of the time, one at 92 percent almost all of it. Same energy, very different cost per kilowatt-hour. APS's XHLF schedule (in force since March 2024) prices that: for customers above 5,000 kW whose load factor held at 92 percent or better for nine of the last twelve months, a demand charge of $18.750 per kW and an energy rate of $0.04210 per kWh.
2. **What travels with the price.** An Electric Supply Agreement fixing contract length, minimum and maximum monthly loads and special charges; a minimum bill for the term; above 15,000 kW, contributions in aid of construction, a maintenance contract and a share of replacement-facility cost. What the sheet lacks: an exit fee and a collateral formula — the two terms every other 2025–2026 large-load tariff in the corpus carries.
3. **What is being added.** The June 2025 rate case proposes about a 45 percent increase for extra-large users, higher minimum bills and collateral, and long-term contracts covering infrastructure cost. The "subscription model" APS began describing in May 2026 is the contract form: individually negotiated XHLF agreements at cost of service, with negotiated term, collateral and exit fees, the customer's option to finance or accelerate key equipment, and front-of-the-meter or self-supply options — WEC's bespoke resource, by contract instead of by tariff.

**Self-check:** a customer above 5,000 kW at 88 percent load factor asks for XHLF. What does the schedule say, and what would qualify it? (No — 92 percent in nine of twelve months is the test; flattening the load, not signing an ESA, is what qualifies it.)

## Module 3 — The formula rate and the queue

**The single idea:** a formula rate resets prices every year from projected costs, closing the lag between a utility's investment and its recovery — and a committed queue is a claim on the system peak, not on energy.

1. **Regulatory lag.** A rate case sets prices from a historical test year (APS's current rates rest on June 2022) and leaves them until the next case; a utility growing rate base 7–9 percent a year earns below its allowed return in between. In the second quarter of 2026 APS's revenue rose $97 million and its operating expenses rose $99 million — lag in one quarter. The Formula Rate Adjustment Mechanism in the 2025 case would reset rates annually by class from projected costs.
2. **Why it is contested.** The consumer office called it "one, two, three or four increases on an annual basis" and asked for a separate data-center class instead; Commission Staff proposed a 9.55–9.80 percent return against the 10.70 asked; the Attorney General argued for about 3 percent at a 6 percent return. Every party accepts the large-load class; none accepts the annual reset. The hearing ran 31 days to July 7, 2026; the judge's recommended order is due in late November and the Commission's vote by December 31.
3. **Reading the queue.** Committed: about 4.5 GW, under signed agreements and ramping. Uncommitted: about 20 GW, in negotiation under the subscription model. The 2025 system peak: 8.6 GW. A flat load adds nearly its full size at the peak hour, so 4.5 GW of committed load is more than half a system peak of new capacity to hold — before the 20 GW behind it. The 2035 forecast peak on APS's own page is 12.0 GW against 8.7 in 2025.
4. **What moves and when.** The committed figure has read "about 4.5 GW" from August 2025 to August 2026; the uncommitted queue converts only when the subscription contracts have a priced basis, which is the December decision. Meanwhile commercial and industrial sales — the ramp — grew 12.7 percent in the quarter and 13.6 percent year to date. When the committed number moves, a contract was signed; when the C&I number moves, one started to draw.

**Self-check:** why would losing the formula rate cost APS more than a lower return would? (A lower return trims the earnings on each dollar of rate base; lag delays the earnings on every new dollar for years, across a plan growing 7–9 percent a year.)

## Module 4 — Cholla, the desert summer and the nuclear decision

**The single idea:** the fastest firm capacity a utility can add is on a site it already owns; a quarter's numbers separate into weather and growth; and a relicensed nuclear plant fixes the shape of everything else that gets bought.

1. **Brownfield gas.** APS retired its last Cholla coal unit in March 2025 and in July 2026 said it would convert two retired units to up to 380 MW of gas by 2029 at up to $440 million, mostly spent in 2027–2028. The site brings an interconnection, a switchyard, transmission, water, rail and permits — no interconnection queue — at a fraction of a greenfield combined cycle's capital. The cost: capped output and an older cycle's efficiency. The 2,000 MW proposal near Gila Bend, from 2030, is the greenfield answer, and it depends on the Desert Southwest pipeline (1.5 Bcf/d, late 2029), on which APS is the anchor customer.
2. **Degree days.** A cooling degree day counts each degree the day's average temperature sits above 65°F. APS's second quarter of 2026 had 619 against 578 — 7 percent more cooling load from weather. Total retail sales rose 9.6 percent; weather-normalized sales rose 5.6 percent. The gap is the weather; the 5.6 is the growth to plan on.
3. **Purchased power.** With 6,257 MW owned against an 8.6 GW peak, APS covers the gap with contracted plants and market purchases: 5,962 GWh generated and 4,925 GWh bought in the quarter, purchases nearly half of supply. Palo Verde ran at 86 percent for the quarter (a refuelling outage) and 92 percent for the half; coal at 64 percent.
4. **The nuclear decision.** APS operates Palo Verde (three units, about 4,200 MW) and holds 29.1 percent — 1,146 MW, its largest block of firm capacity. It will file in late 2027 to extend each unit to 80 years (2065–2067), as Duke did for Oconee and Robinson and WEC did by contract at Point Beach. The effect is identical: a gigawatt of baseload stays, the plan does not grow to replace it, and what is still needed is capacity for the peak — brownfield gas, tolled batteries, the 2 GW program — and energy from the solar PPAs.

**Self-check:** a plan shows 380 MW at Cholla in 2029 and 2 GW from 2030. What sits between Redhawk (2028) and 2030 if the pipeline slips? (Only Cholla — the 2 GW program's fuel arrives in late 2029; the tolled batteries and market purchases carry the peak in between.)

## Module 5 — The map and where it fails

**The single idea:** APS is a toll buyer with a queue larger than its peak, a rate case deciding both its return and its large-load class, and one named customer.

1. **Who buys what.** Storage tolls: the developers bidding into the RFPs (Strata, Recurrent, GridStor, others) — the seller's account. Owned storage and solar: APS, but small (about 4 percent of the 2023 storage). Cholla, Redhawk and the 2 GW program: APS, turbine OEMs undisclosed. Transmission: APS at a FERC formula rate, $300–400 million a year rising, with a potential $1 billion Four Corners–Cholla line — and the "key equipment" a subscription customer can pay to accelerate. Large-load contracts: the customer, with front-of-the-meter or self-supply options.
2. **The failure modes.** The queue does not convert if the Commission narrows XHLF or rejects the formula rate in December. The customer mix is one fab — TSMC at "over 1 gigawatt", the data-center campuses unnamed. Tolls are financed against APS's Baa1 rating and its recovery of the toll in rates; a disallowance or a downgrade changes what the next RFP's bidders can finance. Fuel arrives in 2029 and the plants in 2030.
3. **Arizona's frame.** The five-member elected Commission holds exclusive rate authority and is building a statewide large-load framework in parallel (workshop April 2026: ESAs assigning costs, standard mechanisms, "bring-your-own-capacity"); the legislature paused the data-center tax exemption for three years. Statewide, two dozen operating data centers above 2,000 MW and 10,600 MW planned; SRP, next door, has 59 large-load customers at about 7,000 MW.

**Self-check:** the December order approves the large-load terms but rejects the formula rate. What does APS keep doing, and what changes? (It keeps buying capacity by toll — the cheapest route for its balance sheet — and the owned-gas program becomes harder to carry at the same credit metrics.)
