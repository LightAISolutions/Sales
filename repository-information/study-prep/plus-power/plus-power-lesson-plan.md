# Plus Power — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Plus Power (a standalone-storage specialist operating a fleet of near-identical large batteries across several US markets) · **Written:** 2026-09-07 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, plus the first buyer-side course in this corpus; no fleet-engineering or programme-management background.

**What this teaches.** What changes when a company stops producing projects and starts producing a repeatable way of producing them. A reference design as a frozen set of *decisions*, and the rule that separates what a fleet may freeze from what every site still decides for itself — decisions freeze, measurements do not. Why repetition is worth more in permitting, lending and insurance than in procurement, because a permit and a technical review are examinations of an argument rather than purchases of a thing. What a network can force a standard block to change — grid-forming control and black start on an island system, damping and model validation at a weak point, cold-weather duty, thermal derating, fire-code footprint — and the observation that none of it is a different battery. The serial build curve: why it falls quickly, why it flattens well above zero, and why it resets at a border. The owner's build-organisation decision, which is the contractor course's bargain seen from the other chair. Concurrency, not capital, as the binding constraint on fleet growth, and the warranty clock that starts before energisation. A pipeline as a portfolio of options in which the discipline is killing them early. And the replicated defect — the only failure mode in this course that cannot exist above one project.

**Why this gap.** The first buyer-side course follows one project through its whole life, and everything in it is correct for one project. Nothing in the corpus asks what the second, tenth and thirtieth cost, or what a company has to become to build them. The contracting guide comes closest with a single card weighing one supplier against several, but that card is about counterparty and price risk; it does not touch design standardisation, serial execution, concurrency, or the fact that a standardised fleet installs any error everywhere at once.

**Its place in the portfolio-owner set.** First of four, and the entry point. It establishes the claim the other three elaborate — that above a handful of projects the unit of production is a process — and hands on three threads: the supply-chain consequences of scale to the NextEra Energy Resources guide, the question of what a standard design does at a border to the Lightsource bp guide, and the operating bill for the diversity it argues for to the Terra-Gen guide, which closes the course.

## Module 1 — What actually repeats
The reference design as frozen answers rather than an identical plant. Three benefits in ascending order of importance: procurement discount, engineering not repeated, and the evidentiary one — a reviewer re-examining an argument already accepted. The rule that bounds all of it: decisions can be frozen, measurements cannot, and the failure mode is letting a measurement quietly become an assumption.

## Module 2 — The layers, and what each site still owns
Power block, protection and controls, fire and life safety, civil works, grid connection, and records. Reading the last row as the one that is pure decision, is never forced to differ, and is nonetheless the layer most often left to each project team — which is what makes fleet-wide analysis impossible a decade later.

## Module 3 — The same block on five different networks
Island systems and why the absence of large spinning machines converts an inverter into a grid-forming machine with black-start duty. Weak connection points, system strength and the commissioning stage in which an operator must accept a validated model. Winter-peaking duty and the inversion of a site's own energy consumption. Desert derating as a sizing decision. Dense urban fire review as a footprint decision. The unifying observation: what changes is control software, ratings margin, auxiliary systems and evidence — never the cells.

## Module 4 — The serial curve, and where it resets
Where the time saving comes from and why it is a finite stock. What governs the schedule once that stock is exhausted — the queue, the utility's own work, the factory calendar. Why crossing into a new jurisdiction resets the counter, and the pricing error that follows from budgeting a new market off a mature-market schedule.

## Module 5 — Employ the builders or hire them
The owner's side of the contractor course's bargain. What an in-house delivery organisation keeps and what it costs to feed. What a contracted builder transfers and what leaves with the crew. The hybrid every large owner actually runs, and the rule that explains where it draws the line: keep what you must learn from, buy what you merely need done.

## Module 6 — Concurrency
Why the last phase of a project is the one that cannot parallelise, and which shared resources cause it — commissioning engineers, operator witnesses, inspectors, energisation crews. Energisation slots as the real planning unit. The warranty and guarantee clocks that commonly start at delivery rather than at commercial operation, which turns a scheduling problem into a balance-sheet one. Which part of the limit can be bought back and which part cannot.

## Module 7 — The pipeline as options
Each position as an option whose value comes from uncertainty rather than expectation, and why that makes holding more positions than you intend to build arithmetic rather than optimism. Queue time as the scarce input, so positions must be bought before they can be valued. The kill decision, and development staff as the resource it actually returns. Why a high conversion rate is not evidence of judgement, and why the timing of kills is the more informative measure.

## Module 8 — The replicated defect
The failure mode standardisation creates. Correlation, so identical sites fail on the same trigger rather than independently; and contractual correlation, so the damages fall due together. The four defences — change control, staged rollout, root-cause capability, spares depth. The corollary that a deliberate second design lineage is a hedge against correlated failure, and the careful distinction from the supplier-diversity argument in the contracting guide, which is about a company rather than about a fault.

## Pacing
Eight modules, roughly 15–20 minutes each. Modules 1 and 8 are the conceptual pair and should be read together — they are the two faces of the same decision, and a reader short of time who reads only those will still have the argument. Module 3 is the most technical and is the one to read before any of the integrator guides. Module 6 is the one most likely to change how a reader reads a growth announcement.
