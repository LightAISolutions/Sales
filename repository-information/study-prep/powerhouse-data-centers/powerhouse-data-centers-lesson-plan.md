# PowerHouse Data Centers — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach **powered land and the utility-first campus** — the layer under every landlord in this corpus: what powered land is and why the substation rather than the acreage is the asset, the entitlement toolkit (annexation agreements, planned unit developments, by-right zoning, rezonings, the telecommunications-hotel classification, extraterritorial-jurisdiction release, community benefit agreements, moratoria) and what each instrument buys from which body, the two utility contracts behind a gigawatt campus (the state electric service agreement and the federal transmission security agreement) and how one of them was cancelled after the council had voted, the GP-fund model in which a developer's own fund supplies 5–10% of the equity and its pipeline counter is the product it sells to partners, and the one completed cycle — build a powered shell, lease it for thirty years, sell it to the tenant — that prices the model. PowerHouse Data Centers is the worked example because it is the purest position-seller in the corpus: a division of a private fund manager, one building delivered and sold, "140 data centers" and "~10 GW" in planning, and a 1.8 GW flagship in Joliet that was annexed 8–1 in March 2026 and lost its ComEd transmission agreement in July.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **Aligned** plan names power-first siting and the queue problem; **STACK** teaches land-and-power banking as one rung of a developer's ladder; **QTS** records a USD 12 billion site lost to a village vote; **Excelsior** teaches the general partner, the limited partner and carried interest inside a fund. The **Compass** plan (same session) teaches the sponsor's capital stack and the pipeline ladder; the **EdgeCore** plan opens the lease and the limited-recourse loan. This plan sits under all of them and repeats none.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** Powered land is an option on grid capacity attached to dirt, and the substation is where the option lives. Entitlement and power are separate contracts with separate counterparties, so a campus can be fully approved and have no power. And a GP-fund developer's balance sheet is small, its control is contractual, and its disclosed numbers are partners' programme sizes and projected campus costs.

**One boundary, held throughout.** PowerHouse publishes no accounts, no lease terms beyond ABX-1's thirty years, no construction loan and no tenant for eleven of thirteen campuses; the City of Joliet's own notice, the FERC docket, Hillwood's newsroom and all Data Center Dynamics coverage could not be read, and the brief's domains for the company were wrong (it publishes at powerhousedata.com and americanrepartners.com). Where the plan gives arithmetic (USD 2.6 million per MW for ABX-1) it says so.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min). Module 2 with the Joliet staff memo open (~35 min). Module 3 with the Utility Dive FERC/ComEd articles (~35 min). Module 4 with the New Project Media interview and the Fund IV release (~30 min). Module 5 (~20 min). Then flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — What powered land is, and why the substation is the asset

**The single idea:** a parcel with a secured grid connection is worth what a tenant will pay to skip the queue; the land is cheap, the megawatts are the asset, and the substation is their physical form.

**The catalogue.** ABX-1: a 300 MW gas-insulated substation with Dominion on ten acres, energised in phases (15, 7, 8, 60 MW). Arcola Grove: a "first of its kind" GIS substation shrunk from four acres to 2.4 to sit 468 feet from homes; planning commission 7–2 against, supervisors 5–4 for. PowerHouse 95: three 300 MW substations planned. Circle T: an Oncor 350 MW substation "funded by PowerHouse". Grand Prairie: a 1.8 GW switchyard, "first 500 MW tranche approved through ERCOT". Joliet: a 25-acre switching station to be conveyed to ComEd. Louisville: an LG&E switch station. Carlisle: 450 MW substations per campus.

**Why GIS.** Gas-insulated switchgear is enclosed, quieter and about a third the footprint of an air-insulated yard — the difference between a substation that fits a suburban parcel and one that does not; the cost is SF6 and a planning fight.

**Who pays decides who owns the position.** Utility-built under tariff: the capacity belongs to the utility's plan and the developer holds a queue position. Developer-funded: a dedicated asset to market and pledge. PowerHouse's SVP Data Center Utilities came from Dominion for this work.

**Exercise:** for each PowerHouse campus in the dossier, record the utility, the substation size and who funds it; mark which have a signed service agreement on the public record (few).

## Module 2 — The entitlement toolkit

**The single idea:** the developer chooses the instrument that minimises the number of elected people who must say yes, and pays for the yes with a package rather than an abatement.

**Instruments and PowerHouse's use of them.** Annexation with an annexation agreement (Joliet: 795 acres, 8–1, USD 100 million community benefit, developer-funded upgrades, a 150,000 gallon-a-day water cap with a triple penalty, Tier 4 generators, no incentives). Planned unit development (Joliet's 24-building preliminary plan; challenged in Will County). By-right zoning (Westlake's first Circle T building; EdgeCore's Louisa site). Rezoning and special exception (Arcola Grove, LEGI-2023-0071). The telecommunications-hotel classification (Louisville: planning commission 6–1, "No Metro Council approval required"; then a council moratorium proposal from which the project is exempt). Extraterritorial-jurisdiction release (Uhland, Texas: 500 acres petitioned out of the city into a county that cannot zone). Community benefit agreements and donations (Joliet's USD 100 million — Hillwood's cheque; Compass's USD 27.6 million in Ellis County).

**What follows the vote.** Litigation over process (Joliet's Open Meetings Act suit, hearing 8 September 2026) and moratoria that close the door behind the project that got in (Louisville; Merrillville, Indiana, June 2026–May 2027).

**Exercise:** for a hypothetical 500 MW site in each of Virginia, Texas, Illinois and Kentucky, pick the instrument PowerHouse would reach for first and name the body that decides.

## Module 3 — The two utility contracts, and how one was cancelled after the vote

**The single idea:** a gigawatt campus signs a retail electric service agreement at the state commission and a transmission security agreement at FERC; the council's yes governs neither.

**The Joliet sequence.** December 2025: ComEd files transmission security agreements for a batch of data-center customers (PowerHouse Hillwood Holding, QTS, Equinix, Aligned among them); the Illinois Attorney General protests. 11 March 2026: FERC accepts five. 19 March: Joliet annexes 795 acres 8–1. 23 March: the Illinois Commerce Commission approves ComEd's large-load tariff — USD 1 million base deposit plus USD 500,000 per 100 MW above 200 MW, developer-funded substations and transmission, deposits retainable if a project fails. 24 July: ComEd tells FERC it has cancelled PowerHouse Hillwood's agreement ("security deposit timing") and cancels the pending retail agreement. 14 August: PowerHouse Hillwood asks FERC to reject the cancellation and sues ComEd in the Northern District of Illinois.

**Read the deposit against the model.** A GP-fund developer reserves capacity years ahead of a tenant; the rule charges for exactly that, and a forfeitable deposit is large next to a 10% equity cheque. Whether the deposit was late, disputed or refused is not on the record; that it became the breaking point is.

**What survives.** The annexation agreement and its USD 100 million of promised payments; the PUD; the residents' lawsuit (hearing 8 September 2026). A campus can be fully entitled and unpowered — the inverse of a by-right site with no permit.

**Exercise:** draw the four contracts around Joliet (annexation agreement, PUD, retail service agreement, transmission security agreement), name each counterparty and mark which are in force on 5 September 2026.

## Module 4 — The GP-fund model: 10% of the equity, 100% of the pipeline

**The single idea:** the developer supplies the general partner's sliver and the work; the institutions supply the money; the pipeline counter is the product the GP sells to the next partner.

**The structure in the company's words.** "90/10 ... AREP contributes 10% of the equity while large institutional partners provide 90%"; Blue Owl "95/5"; Fund IV "a USD 300m GP fund with over USD 9bn total buying power" for "12-to-15 new PowerHouse projects" (Katz, New Project Media). Fund IV closed at USD 309 million, 80% to PowerHouse, "100% deployed as common equity", "predominantly repeat" investors. Fund III: USD 63 million (2022), financing Arcola.

**The partners.** Programmatic: Harrison Street (USD 1 billion in 2022 to "USD 3.2 billion committed" by December 2024 across NoVA, Dallas, Reno); Blue Owl/Chirisa ("USD 5 billion", two USD 750 million tranches, CoreWeave at Chesterfield). Single-site: Provident (Grand Prairie), Hillwood (Joliet — "PowerHouse Hillwood Holding, LLC"; the USD 100 million is Hillwood's), Poe (Kentucky), Town Lane (Charlotte), Pennsylvania Data Center Partners (Carlisle). Commitment figures are capacity, not cash.

**Economics.** Fees plus a carried interest on profit above the partner's preferred return (Excelsior's plan has the fund version). Buying power is the project cost 10% controls. The pipeline counter — 4.1 GW (December 2024) → 5.9 → 6.1 → 8.1 (January 2026) → "~10 GW" (September 2026) against one delivered building — is compiled per release, not maintained as a ledger.

**Watch the drift.** PowerHouse is absent from the August 2025 USD 4 billion Blue Owl/Chirisa/Machine Investment Group close that includes Chesterfield; Harrison Street counts Arcola in its own platform. A low-confidence judgment, not a finding.

**Exercise:** rebuild the "who owns what" table for Joliet, Chesterfield and ABX-1 from the sources; note which party made each dollar announcement.

## Module 5 — The one completed cycle, and what it prices

**The single idea:** ABX-1 is the model end to end — entitle, power, build a shell, lease long, sell to the tenant — and its sale price puts a number on a powered shell.

**The cycle.** Harrison Street JV announced January 2022; topped out March 2023 (DPR); leased to CyrusOne for 30 years in May 2023 on a 300 MW Dominion substation; delivered October 2023; sold to CyrusOne for USD 154.1 million in December 2024 (Commercial Observer; the release gives no price).

**Price per MW.** About USD 2.6 million per MW for a 60 MW powered shell, versus about USD 18 million per MW for Compass's turnkey, operating, hyperscale-leased ABS pool. The difference is the fit-out, the operating platform and the lease — the product ladder in dollars.

**The exit the GP needs.** A stabilized asset with a thirty-year credit-tenant lease is what a partner's capital and preferred return are returned from and what the promote is earned on; Chaudhry's 2027–2028 "big buying opportunity" for stabilized credit-tenant assets is the company saying when the rest of its portfolio should reach the same point.

**Exercise:** apply the ABX-1 per-MW price and the Compass per-MW appraisal to a 120 MW Arcola shell and a 120 MW turnkey Chesterfield building; state what each figure assumes and why neither is a valuation of PowerHouse.

**Where it fails (for the whole plan):** entitled but unpowered; a counter that is not a portfolio; tenants mostly unnamed; a GP cheque that is small; schedules that slip silently; a deposit rule that targets the model.
