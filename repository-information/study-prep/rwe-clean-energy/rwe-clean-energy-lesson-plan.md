# RWE Clean Energy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach **what a completed grid interconnection is actually worth, and the two very different things an owner can do with one**. Specifically: why repowering a 2006 wind farm to almost the same nameplate is worth doing — capacity factor, hub height, rotor area, a restarted 30-year life and, above all, a connection you keep; what the One Big Beautiful Bill Act's safe-harbour rules force a developer to evidence and by when, and why buying turbines early hedges the tax clock and the tariff regime in one purchase; why RWE co-locates storage with solar in America and runs it standalone in Europe, in its own words; how iron-air storage works chemically and why 50 MW of it sits beside 1,500 MW of lithium-ion on one Colorado site; what a simple-cycle peaker is, why poor fuel efficiency is the point, and how an interconnection right won for renewables converts into firm gas capacity; and how to read a segment report that deliberately merges the country you care about into a larger one.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **ENGIE** plan (same session) teaches the ancillary-services revenue stack and the farmdown; the **AES** plan (same session) teaches construction labour, robots and the tax-credit machinery. This plan is the **asset-renewal and technology-choice** plan: what you do with a site you already have, and how you choose between chemistries and between prime movers. The **Blattner** and **McCarthy** dossiers give the contractor's view of the projects named here.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** The scarce good in American power is a connected point at which generation may legally exist, and it is technology-neutral in a way most readers do not expect. A battery's configuration — co-located or standalone, four-hour lithium or multi-day iron-air — is a decision about which market it is being paid by, not about which technology is better. And when a listed European parent merges its American business into a multi-continent segment, you must know which disclosure exists and which does not before you quote anything.

**One boundary, held throughout.** RWE Clean Energy, LLC was renamed **RWE Americas, LLC** in March 2026, confirmed by the leadership CVs on RWE's own site, while the legal entity persists in the parent's shareholdings list. RWE publishes no US profit measure, no US-only operating storage MW or MWh, no US development pipeline in gigawatts, no dated US employee count, no PV module supplier, and neither the term "FEOC" nor "domestic content" anywhere in its first-party material. Four different US capacity bases circulate and none reconciles to another. The widely repeated "931 MW of US storage under construction" is RWE's own figure from a press release dated 2 October 2024, and the three Texas projects behind it were commissioned by the end of 2025 — this plan treats it as stale and says so. The domains us.rwe.com and rwecleanenergy.com do not resolve; americas.rwe.com is the live site.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 with a turbine manufacturer's power curve open (~30 min). Module 2 with RWE's Annual Report 2025 risk chapter (~30 min). Module 3 with the Stoneridge and Crowned Heron releases side by side (~25 min). Module 4 with an iron-air technical primer (~30 min). Module 5 with a simple-cycle heat-rate table (~25 min). Module 6 with the Annual Report's segment and goodwill notes (~30 min). Then flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — Repowering: why you demolish a working wind farm

**The single idea:** the valuable part of a twenty-year-old wind farm is not its turbines.

**The worked case.** Forest Creek, Texas, online 2006: 54 Siemens Mark II turbines at 2.3 MW on 400-foot towers, 124.2 MW. Repowered 2025: every original tower, turbine and component decommissioned, new foundations, **45 GE Vernova turbines at 2.82 MW**, 126.9 MW. Nine fewer machines, two megawatts more nameplate.

**Where the gain actually is.** Capacity factor. Twenty years bought larger rotors, taller hubs and better control. A larger swept area starts generating at a lower cut-in wind speed and reaches rated power sooner; a taller hub sits in steadier, faster wind. The same site converts more of the hours between calm and rated.

**The other two gains.** Life — RWE states the repower extends operating life "for an additional 30 years". And eligibility — a repower can restart tax-credit qualification, which is why repowering clusters when credit rules change.

**The gain that decides it.** You keep the interconnection, the land rights, the access roads, the collection system and the substation. A greenfield equivalent must win a queue position whose ERCOT median has stretched to 4.1 years, where only about 27% of large projects that exit the queue ever operate.

**The disposal problem.** Blades are thermoset composite designed never to come apart. RWE routes decommissioned blades, rotors and nacelles through Wanzek Construction (a MasTec Renewables company) for repurposing or recycling.

**Exercise:** for a 100 MW site, assume the old fleet ran at a 32% capacity factor and the repowered fleet runs at 42% on the same nameplate. Compute the additional annual MWh and, at USD 30/MWh, the additional annual revenue. Then state what fraction of a USD 130m repower cost that recovers per year.

## Module 2 — The clock, from a European parent's side

**The single idea:** RWE's US retrenchment and its reversal were both group capital decisions, and the thing that changed between them was certainty, not favourability.

**The rules RWE states.** New wind and solar must be commissioned by end-2027 to remain eligible; safe-harbour rules preserve credits for later completion "if they can prove by mid-2026 that construction on them has commenced"; safe-harboured assets must operate within four years of the construction start. RWE's position: "As all the investment projects we will be implementing in the coming years have safe harbour status, they remain eligible for these tax credits."

**The mitigation, and why it is two hedges in one.** "We responded quickly to the shift in energy policy by purchasing components early, enabling us to secure important state funding." Early purchase evidences a construction start *and* pre-empts tariffs — a 15% EU rate from August 2025 ruled unconstitutional in February 2026 and replaced by a 10% global tariff for 150 days, 50% on steel and aluminium since June 2025, punitive duties on Southeast Asian solar. RWE also pushes tariff risk into contracts: it reaches "agreements with suppliers and power purchasers that oblige them to pay a share of any additional levies".

**The capital arc.** November 2024: US offshore investment delayed. March 2025: EUR 10bn (about 25%) cut from the 2025-2030 programme, hurdle rate raised from an average 8% to above 8.5%, savings to a EUR 1.5bn buyback; Elliott discloses about 5% days later. March 2026: EUR 17bn committed to the US to 2031, capacity 13 GW → 22 GW. The chief executive credited the OBBB with providing the "clarity" to resume — a bill that *cut* credits.

**The onshore/offshore distinction RWE draws.** "We consider our onshore solar and wind projects to be less exposed to such risks. The necessary federal approvals have continued to be granted for these investments." The pause was an offshore judgment applied to a group budget; 2025 was RWE's largest US commissioning year ever (2 GW across 15 projects in seven states).

**Exercise:** write the sequence of events that would have to occur for a project safe-harboured in mid-2026 to lose its credits anyway. There are at least three.

## Module 3 — Co-located or standalone: a decision about which market pays

**The single idea:** the configuration follows the revenue, and RWE runs both because it operates in two markets that pay for different things.

**RWE's own statement.** "In the USA, we often build storage facilities and solar farms together to optimise the timing of photovoltaic feed-ins to the local grid. Conversely, our large-scale batteries in Germany and other European markets are usually operated independently."

**What co-location buys.** A shared point of interconnection. The battery charges from midday output the line cannot carry and discharges in the evening when the line is empty — raising the energy a connection delivers without enlarging it. Shared land, shared substation, shared permitting.

**What co-location costs.** Flexibility. Charging patterns and sometimes grid-charging rights are constrained by the interconnection agreement and historically by tax-credit rules requiring charging from the paired plant.

**The US fleet, read this way.** Co-located: Stoneridge (200 MW solar with 100 MW / 200 MWh, commissioned 26 November 2025), the two 9.9 MW / 5 MWh Texas Waves units at Inadale and Pyron, Iron Horse in Arizona (10 MW / 2.5 MWh with 2.4 MW of PV, dispatched by Tucson Electric Power). Standalone: Crowned Heron (150 MW / 300 MWh, Fort Bend County, online March 2025, 92 batteries on 15 acres, stated 20-year life), Cartwheel (150 MW / 300 MWh), Ventasso in California (50 MW, published as both 200 MWh and 220 MWh in RWE's own releases). Westside Canal 2A (119 MW) was sold to San Diego Gas & Electric, closing July 2026.

**Exercise:** for a 200 MW solar plant on a 200 MW interconnection, clipping 8% of annual generation at midday, compute the energy a co-located 100 MW / 200 MWh battery could recover. Then state what that same battery could earn standalone on a $35/MWh average spread, and which configuration you would choose in ERCOT versus in Germany.

## Module 4 — Two chemistries on one site: Planet Rock

**The single idea:** lithium-ion and iron-air are not competitors; they are complements sized to different markets, and a hybrid site is a portfolio decision.

**The asset.** Planet Rock, Pueblo County, Colorado — 1,550 MW on 180 acres a mile from the Tundra Substation, phased with a first 550 MW operating in 2029. **1,500 MW of four-hour lithium-ion plus 50 MW of iron-air.**

**Lithium-ion.** Energy-dense, high-power, expensive per kilowatt-hour of storage, excellent for moving energy a few hours thousands of times. At very long durations the economics break, because storing a hundred hours means buying a hundred hours of cells and cells are the cost.

**Iron-air.** Stores energy by reversibly oxidising iron — rusting it with oxygen from ambient air to discharge, reducing it back to metallic iron to charge. Active materials are iron and air: abundant, cheap, non-flammable. In exchange it is bulky, slow and round-trip inefficient. Useless for the products lithium serves; potentially decisive for multi-day storage lithium cannot reach.

**Why 50 against 1,500.** Large enough to learn from and to prove interconnection and controls; small enough that being wrong about the multi-day market is survivable. That ratio is the shape of a well-made technology bet.

**The rest of the book, and the number to remember.** Conventional four-hour lithium: Fourth Quarter (500 MW, four-hour, on the retired Dickerson coal site in Maryland), Wythe County (52 MW / 208.8 MWh — a clean 4.02 hours), Erie Canal (230 MW), Elliot (200 MW), White Clover (200 MW), Basecamp (170 MW), Covina (110 MW, operations 2027). RWE assumes a **10-15 year useful life for battery facilities against 25-35 years for solar** — the batteries in a co-located project will be replaced once or twice inside the solar plant's life.

**Exercise:** state which of the following a 50 MW iron-air unit can and cannot sell: energy arbitrage on a daily spread, responsive reserve, regulation, multi-day firming, capacity accreditation. Justify each from the chemistry.

## Module 5 — The pivot: peakers, and how an interconnection converts

**The single idea:** a grid connection is not permanently tied to the technology that won it, and that is the entire logic of RWE's gas announcement.

**Simple cycle.** A gas turbine — effectively a jet engine driving a generator. Air compressed, fuel burned, hot gas expanded through a turbine, exhaust up the stack. Roughly 30-40% of the fuel's energy becomes electricity.

**Combined cycle.** The exhaust heat raises steam in a heat-recovery boiler driving a second turbine: 55-64% efficiency, considerably more capital, slower to build and slower to start.

**Why you would ever choose the inefficient one.** Duty cycle. A plant running 6,000 hours a year lives on fuel efficiency, so combined cycle earns its capital back. A plant running 200 hours a year barely burns fuel, so what matters is low capital cost and a fast start. That is a peaker.

**RWE's announcement, read precisely.** Fifteen gas peaking projects across MISO, WECC, PJM and ERCOT; more than 3 GW of gas targeted by 2035; grid connections secured for up to 10 GW; a USD 300 million turbine reservation; "energy campuses" co-locating generation, storage and infrastructure, sold as "speed to power". The plan is to "leverage already-secured grid interconnections to add firm capacity on an accelerated timeline".

**What the strategy actually is.** Turbines can be ordered. Interconnection positions take years to win, and one secured for wind or solar can — subject to the operator's process — host a different resource. RWE is converting queue positions it won as a renewables developer into firm capacity it can sell to load today. The name changed in the same quarter.

**Exercise:** for a 200 MW simple-cycle unit at 35% efficiency running 300 hours a year against a 200 MW combined-cycle unit at 58% running 5,000 hours, at USD 4/MMBtu gas, compare annual fuel cost per MWh and state at roughly what capacity factor the combined-cycle capital premium starts to pay.

## Module 6 — Reading a segment report that hides your country

**The single idea:** when a company merges the business you care about into a larger reportable segment, know which disclosure survives.

**The structure.** RWE runs "RWE Clean Energy" as an *operating* segment and merges it into the *reportable* segment "Onshore Wind/Solar" with Europe and Australia, on the stated ground that they "have comparable economic features". There is no US profit and loss.

**Where to look instead.** *Capacity* — the audited installed-capacity table by country: 11,177 MW of US renewables at 31 December 2025 (5,432 MW onshore wind, 5,745 MW solar), excluding storage. *Value* — the goodwill note, where RWE Clean Energy is a separate cash-generating unit: goodwill EUR 1,351m from EUR 1,528m, after-tax discount rate raised 5.25% → 6.25%, terminal growth 1.50% → 2.00%, with a ten-year detailed planning period against three for other units "due to the growth business". RWE attributes the discount-rate rise to a method change — discounting in dollars rather than converting first — so read it as a currency-basis change, not purely a risk repricing. *Write-downs* — an impairment in the USA within Onshore Wind/Solar with a recoverable amount of "EUR 0 billion", plus intangibles recorded for California battery development projects; EUR 132m of most significant segment impairments.

**The segment numbers you do get.** Onshore Wind/Solar adjusted EBITDA EUR 1,502m (FY2024) → EUR 1,740m (FY2025) → EUR 507m (Q1 2026) → EUR 1,016m (H1 2026), with H1 gains "offset by negative effects arising from the conversion of US dollars to euros".

**The four capacity bases.** RWE's ~13 GW marketing figure including storage; the audited 11,177 MW excluding it; an EIA-860M-derived 15,266 MW across 166 plants counting nameplate differently; and a generation-based ranking (26th of 5,343 US utilities, 3rd of 749 by wind generation) that is not a capacity measure at all. No source reconciles any pair.

**Exercise:** using only the goodwill note, state what RWE's own model implies about the US business's expected cash flows in 2025 versus 2024, and list every reason other than deterioration that could explain a lower goodwill balance.
