# Salt River Project — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach how a public-power utility serves a data-center boom without a commission — what public power is and why its rate case is an election; how a utility with no equity finances a $12.9 billion program on tax-exempt revenue bonds and manages to coverage instead of a return; what an energy storage agreement is and why the developer, not the utility, picks the battery; how a minimum bill keyed to the customer's own forecast shifts the ramp risk; why the siting certificate is the one gate a state body holds over a public-power plant; what an eight-hour battery buys and what a boiler conversion is priced against; and how federal direct pay turned a PPA buyer into an owner — starting from high-school STEM. Companion: the in-app guide (Profiler → Salt River Project → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The Pinnacle West plan taught the toll buyer inside a commission-regulated utility. This plan teaches the same buying pattern inside a utility with no commission: SRP contracts almost all of its storage the way APS does, but its Board writes the large-load terms in one vote, faces an election instead of a rate order, and has begun to own. Read the two together and the Phoenix metro splits into an investor-owned West Valley and a public-power Mesa side with different clocks.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min — public power and revenue bonds), Module 2 in one sitting (~30 min — the energy storage agreement and the RFP), Module 3 in one sitting (~25 min — E-67 and the LCIP), Module 4 in one sitting (~30 min — the siting certificate, eight hours and the conversions), Module 5 last (~20 min — direct pay, the map and where it fails), then the flashcard and self-test passes in the app.

## Module 1 — Public power: no shareholders, an elected board, tax-exempt debt

**The single idea:** a public-power utility is owned by the public it serves, prices itself through a board vote in place of a rate case, and borrows on bonds repaid only from electric sales.

1. **The entity.** SRP is two organizations: the Salt River Project Agricultural Improvement and Power District, a political subdivision of Arizona formed in 1937 that runs the electric business, and the Salt River Valley Water Users' Association from 1903. A political subdivision is a unit of government — like a county or an irrigation district — with its own legal existence and its own power to borrow. There are no shareholders and no dividend; SRP's own words are "does not have investors."
2. **What replaces the rate case.** Fourteen directors plus a president and vice president are elected by landowners inside SRP's boundaries, mostly one acre–one vote. Arizona law (A.R.S. 45-1720) removes SRP's rates and service from the Arizona Corporation Commission. Prices change in a pricing process: the Board voted in February 2025 to raise base prices 4.0 percent and cut the fuel clause 1.6 percent from November 2025, and in March 2026 to cut 3 percent for the summer from a fuel surplus. No testimony, no order, no appeal.
3. **The money.** SRP sells revenue bonds — debt repaid only from the system's income, with interest exempt from federal tax. Its targets are bond metrics: debt service coverage of 4.2 times in FY2025, a 46.2 percent debt ratio, a general-fund cash target. Its ratings (Aa1, AA+) sit above every investor-owned utility in the corpus. The one financial lever the ACC holds is bond authorization — $6.4 billion approved in September 2024.
4. **The disclosure.** There is no earnings call and no consensus. The documents are the audited annual statements (fiscal year to April 30), the official statement for each bond sale — the 2025 Series C statement is 160 pages and carries the load forecast, the power-source table and the contract list — and the Board's public packets. A public-power dossier is written from those, and says so.

**Self-check:** APS's large-load terms wait on a commission vote by December 31, 2026; SRP's took effect in November 2025. What explains the gap? (A pricing process is a board vote; a rate case is a contested proceeding with a schedule the utility does not control.)

## Module 2 — The energy storage agreement and the all-source RFP

**The single idea:** SRP has more than 1,570 MW of batteries and owns 25 MW of them; the rest are under agreements in which the developer builds, finances, owns and operates the plant and SRP dispatches it — so the developer picks the supplier.

1. **The instrument.** An energy storage agreement is a toll: SRP pays for the right to charge and discharge for the term and takes the market risk; the owner takes the availability risk. Sierra Estrella (250 MW / 1,000 MWh) and Superstition belong to Plus Power; Pediment (250 MW / 1,000 MWh) to Aypa; Flatland (200 MW / 800 MWh) to EDP Renewables under a 24-year agreement; Eleven Mile, Sonoran, Storey and Saint pair storage with solar under PPAs with Ørsted and NextEra. The operating list sums to 1,573 MW.
2. **Who chose the equipment.** Where SRP has disclosed the battery it was Tesla Megapacks — at Bolster (SRP-owned) and at Sierra Estrella, Superstition and Flatland (developer-owned). Pediment's, Eleven Mile's and NextEra's suppliers are not on SRP's record. The account for a battery seller is therefore the developer bidding into SRP's all-source RFP, not SRP's desk.
3. **The RFP.** SRP runs one every year or two (2022, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). The 2026 solicitation asked for up to 2,900 MW of carbon-free resources — including wind on new interstate transmission — plus peaking capacity including storage and gas, in tolling, build-transfer or asset-acquisition form, with a 25 MW minimum, a $1,500 per MW community contribution, proposals due April 29, 2026 and a short list in November. The 2023 and 2024 rounds drew 144 proposals from 68 developers.
4. **The counterparties in the corpus.** Aypa, Plus Power, Invenergy (SunDog, 200 MW of solar plus 200 MW of four-hour storage, March 2026), NextEra (3,000 MW of solar at 500 MW a year from 2029) and Pattern (600 MW of SunZia wind) are covered; EDP, Ørsted, BrightNight, Clenera, ESS, CMBlu and Energy Dome are not. Strata Clean Energy, often paired with SRP in trade press, holds no SRP contract — its Arizona batteries are APS's.

**Self-check:** SRP has 1,573 MW of batteries and 25 MW in its own name. A seller wants the next 500 MW. Who is the account, and by when? (The bidders into the 2026 RFP; the short list is November 2026.)

## Module 3 — E-67 and the Large Customer Integration Process

**The single idea:** SRP's large-load plan makes the customer's own forecast the basis of its minimum bill and makes the customer pay for its dedicated upgrades upfront.

1. **Applicability.** E-67 is mandatory for any new account of 20 MW or more, or one SRP expects from the customer's forecast to reach 20 MW within five years. The 2023 sheet's 90 percent load-factor test — APS still uses 92 percent — is gone.
2. **The minimum.** Each new large-load account carries a minimum billing demand of 80 percent of the customer's forecasted demand: it pays for four-fifths of what it said it would use whether or not it uses it. SRP may lower the figure where the customer pays for load-serving resources or for job creation. SRP sets a maximum load from the same forecast and has no obligation to serve beyond it; a customer who wants resources faster or dearer than SRP otherwise would must agree in writing to reimburse the cost.
3. **What the sheet does not carry.** Term, collateral and termination fees sit in the Agreement for Electric Service, which is not public. That is why Prime Data Centers' account of "15-year contracts" and collateral "in the hundreds of millions" cannot be checked against SRP's record — the dossier states it as unreconciled.
4. **The upgrades and the pipeline.** The Large Customer Integration Process (2025) requires dedicated substations and lines to be funded upfront — Meta paid for its own 230 kV line and substation in Mesa; EdgeCore lost a building to an SRP switchyard. SRP told the ACC in April 2026 it has 59 large-load customers and a nearly 7,000 MW combined load; that is requested load, not served — the whole system peaked at 9,072 MW on July 24, 2026 and data centers were 441 MW of the 2025 peak. SRP's own planning figure is a peak above 14 GW by 2035 with large business above 39 percent, from 12 percent.

**Self-check:** a customer forecasts 200 MW, ramps to 120 MW and stalls. What does it pay demand charges on under E-67? (160 MW — 80 percent of its forecast — unless SRP reduced the minimum for upgrades it funded.)

## Module 4 — The siting certificate, eight hours and the coal conversions

**The single idea:** the ACC cannot set SRP's prices but can stop its plants above 100 MW; SRP's next batteries are eight hours long; and its coal sites become gas because the conversion was cheaper than long-duration lithium.

1. **The certificate.** A Certificate of Environmental Compatibility is Arizona's siting approval for a plant above 100 MW or a line at 115 kV or more. The Power Plant and Transmission Line Siting Committee hears the case and recommends; the five elected commissioners approve, amend or reject. In 2022 they rejected SRP's 820 MW Coolidge expansion; it was built at 575 MW with twelve GE LM6000 units and a 30 percent capacity-factor cap.
2. **Marigold.** Approved by SRP's Board on September 14, 2026 for Stanfield in Pinal County: 600 MW of solar, 400 MW of eight-hour batteries, 675 MW of gas, a 230/500 kV substation and four miles of 230 kV line; construction January 2027, online 2032. Only the gas plant (Case 268) and the lines (Case 267) need the certificate; the committee sat September 21–25 and the ACC's vote is expected in November 2026. Residents and the Sierra Club contested air quality in a non-attainment area. The solar and the batteries can proceed whatever the vote.
3. **Eight hours.** A four-hour battery — 250 MW, 1,000 MWh — covers the evening ramp. SRP's E-67 on-peak window runs 5–10 p.m. and its record peak came at 5–6 p.m. after a 117-degree day, on a system with more than 1,560 MW of solar that fades at sunset. Aypa's second project (250 MW / 2,000 MWh, December 2028) and Marigold's 400 MW are eight-hour: twice the cells and enclosure for the same inverters and interconnection, worth it when the window is five hours long and the plan adds 6,000 MW of solar by 2035.
4. **The conversions.** Coronado (762 MW) and Springerville 4 (415 MW) will burn gas from late 2029 on a new lateral; the ACC granted the certificates in March 2026. SRP priced Coronado's conversion at about $300 million less than a new gas plant and about $1.2 billion less than long-duration lithium batteries. The long-duration pilots — ESS's iron-flow 5 MW / 50 MWh, CMBlu's organic flow, Energy Dome's 19 MW / ten-hour CO2 battery under a Google-co-funded toll — are a few megawatts on a research budget.

**Self-check:** the ACC rejects Case 268 in November 2026. What is Marigold then? (A 600 MW solar plus 400 MW / eight-hour storage project with no firm leg, and the 2035 plan needs 675 MW from the 2026 RFP instead.)

## Module 5 — Direct pay, the map and where it fails

**The single idea:** federal direct pay let a tax-exempt utility become an owner; SRP is a toll buyer that has started to self-build; and its plan fails at the vote, the certificate, the forecast and the disclosure.

1. **Direct pay.** Clean-energy credits reduce income tax, and a public-power utility pays none, so for two decades SRP bought renewables through PPAs with developers who could use the credit. The 2022 federal law let tax-exempt entities take it as cash. SRP's official statement says this "gave the District a new opportunity to diversify development methods": Copper Crossing's 55 MW of solar (April 2026) is the first owned plant; Marigold's storage and solar are carried as both capital and PPA cost; a 1–2 GW pumped-storage project at Apache Lake is self-build. The 2025 phase-downs and FEOC rules apply to direct-pay projects too, and SRP has not quantified them.
2. **Who buys what.** Storage tolls: the developers in the RFPs — the seller's account. Owned storage: SRP, small but growing (Bolster, Marigold, Apache Lake). Gas: SRP, with GE named only at Coolidge and Marigold's design not selected. Large loads: the customer, under E-67 and the LCIP. Long-duration pilots: the vendors, under tolls Google co-funds.
3. **The failure modes.** The Board is 7–7 on gas after an August 2026 appointment, with half the seats up in 2028. The ACC has rejected an SRP gas plant before. SRP itself told the Commission that large customers cannot forecast their ramps. Every srpnet.com HTML page returned 403 to this session; the workshop slide behind the 59-customer figure was never read; no financial statement after July 31, 2025 was found; the E-67 contract terms are private.
4. **Arizona's frame.** The state paused new data-center tax-exemption applications from July 2026 to June 2029; HB 2756 sends SRP's semiannual extra-high-load-factor reports to its own Board while giving the ACC tariff authority only over investor-owned utilities; APS and SRP share Palo Verde (20.4 percent for SRP), the Desert Southwest pipeline (late 2029) and a nuclear siting study.

**Self-check:** the ACC approves Marigold's certificate and the 2028 election flips the Board back to a gas-skeptical majority. What is already committed, and what is not? (Marigold's gas has its certificate and a January 2027 construction start; the 2026 RFP's gas bids and any later plant would face the new Board.)
