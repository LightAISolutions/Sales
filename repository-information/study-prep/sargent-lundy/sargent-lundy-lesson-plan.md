# Sargent & Lundy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach **the three engineers on every power project and what each one is for** — the owner's engineer who advocates, the independent engineer who verifies for the lender, and the EPC's engineer of record who delivers — then the technical due-diligence sequence from feasibility to construction monitoring and what a 30, 60 and 90 percent design review actually asks; how engineering firms are paid and the risks they never carry; campus power for a data centre as an owner's-engineer problem — grid prospecting, the mock system-impact study, the point of interconnection, islanded and bridging generation, and what 99.995 percent means in minutes; and why a hyperscaler weighing a reactor hires a nuclear architect-engineer before it talks to a reactor vendor. Sargent & Lundy is the worked example because it holds all three seats on different projects and does so with no equity, no construction risk and no published revenue.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** This is the second half of a two-part lesson. The **DNV plan** is the first half — what "bankable" means and who decides it, what sits inside the independent engineer's report, what a certificate and a recommended practice prove, where degradation test data comes from, reliance and liability, and the eight ways an IE fails. Nothing here repeats it; where this plan touches the IE it points there. The **Burns & McDonnell**, **Black & Veatch**, **Kiewit** and **Bechtel** plans teach EPC delivery from the contractor's side; this plan sits on the owner's side of the same table. The utility plans (Dominion, Southern, Entergy, Xcel, AEP, Oncor) own the large-load tariff and the regulatory argument; this plan owns the engineering that precedes the filing.

**Three ideas carry the plan.** An engineering firm sells judgment *on someone else's behalf*, and every role is defined by whose. A design review is a question asked at the last moment it is still cheap to answer. And in the data-centre chain the scarce input is the energisation date, so the engineer who sells time — the mock study, the queue conversion, the reused interconnection — sells the most valuable thing.

**One boundary, held throughout.** Sargent & Lundy publishes no revenue, names no data-centre client, and does not use the words "employee-owned" that others attach to it ("privately owned and led by engineers" is its formulation). The plan works from what is verifiable — its own 640-page site, client and regulator statements, NRC inspection reports, ENR ranks — and says so where the record stops.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min). Module 2 with a real design-review log if you can get one (~40 min). Module 3 (~25 min). Module 4 — the data-centre module — is the one that pays (~40 min). Module 5 (~25 min). Then flashcards and self-test in the app.

## Module 1 — Three engineers, one project

**The single idea:** the owner's engineer, the independent engineer and the EPC's engineer produce different documents for different masters, and the rules that keep them apart are the point.

**Who hires whom.** The owner hires the owner's engineer under a professional-services agreement to advocate for it. The lender appoints the independent engineer (the developer pays) to verify the plan for the money. The EPC contractor's engineer stamps the construction drawings as engineer of record under the EPC's lump-sum or turnkey contract. In a third party's compression: "IEs identify issues. OEs help solve them."

**Why the owner needs its own.** The contractor's obligation is the contract, not the owner's outcome; the owner's engineer reads the drawings with the contractor's level of knowledge and pushes back before steel is ordered. Its fee is "a small fraction of total project cost", typically time-and-materials, and is argued to be repaid through control of "the schedule, scope management, change orders, and overall project controls."

**The rule that keeps them honest.** The owner's engineer must not bid the EPC it helped specify — owners should write in "a provision that precludes the owner's engineer from bidding on any related EPC contract." The conflict is per project, not per firm: the same company can be owner's engineer on one job and engineer of record inside an EPC consortium on another, as Sargent & Lundy is (Ørsted's Eleven Mile battery; Entergy's Orange County station).

**Self-check:** an EPC offers owner's engineering "at no charge." What is lost? *(The advocacy — an engineer paid by the contractor to check the contractor is the contractor's engineer under another label.)*

## Module 2 — The due-diligence sequence and what a design review asks

**The single idea:** each review stage asks a question that is cheap to answer then and expensive to answer later.

**The sequence.** Feasibility and siting; interconnection prospecting and application; basis of design and the 10 and 30 percent concept "to provide a basis for contracting"; EPC specification and bid review; award. Then the EPC's packages at 30, 60 and 90 percent and issued-for-construction, each reviewed by the owner's engineer; vendor submittals approved (approval, not the purchase order, releases the factory slot); factory acceptance tests witnessed; construction monitored; site acceptance tests, energisation, commissioning and performance tests overseen; turnover.

**What each review is for.** 10 percent: is this the right plant on the right site? 30 percent: does the architecture — layout, single-line, major equipment — meet the requirements and the interconnection? 60 percent: do the studies close (short circuit, protection coordination, load flow, thermal) and do the submittals match the specification? 90 percent: is it constructible and coordinated, and do the test procedures actually prove the guarantees? A concept objection raised at 90 percent is a field change.

**The or-equal trap.** A submittal offers an "equivalent" product; the reviewer approves it against headline ratings; the difference surfaces at site. "It is the clause by which a second and third vendor get into a bid — and the clause all three of them argue about."

**Factory versus site.** The factory acceptance test proves the machine in the shop; the site acceptance test and the integrated test prove the installation. A programme that budgets travel for the first and none for the second has proven half the plant.

**Self-check:** a review log shows zero rejected items at 60 percent. Comfort or warning? *(Warning — a 60 percent review with two weeks to turn comments becomes a rubber stamp, and the tell is a log with nothing rejected.)*

## Module 3 — How engineering firms are paid, and what they never carry

**The single idea:** fees for judgment; no equity, no construction risk, no performance guarantee.

**The contract.** Professional-services agreements — hourly, lump-sum fee, or a mix. Professional liability capped at a multiple of the fee. Overruns, delays and shortfalls sit with the EPC under its lump sum, backed by liquidated damages. The DNV plan's account of reliance letters and caps applies to the owner's engineer and the engineer of record as much as to the IE.

**Why an engineer-owned firm partners to build.** With no external balance sheet, a firm "owned and led by engineers" cannot absorb a lump-sum overrun. So it takes EPC scope only in a consortium or joint venture with a constructor: TIC on Orange County, PCL in White Oak Power Constructors, Boldt in Westside Energy Partners, EC Source in the AEP joint venture, Kiewit on carbon capture. The engineer designs and manages; the constructor builds and carries the risk. The one disclosed exception — an investment in NuScale in 2019 — is a bet on a design the firm wrote, announced precisely because it is rare.

**How to read a firm that publishes no numbers.** ENR rank (design revenue compiled by a third party from self-reported surveys — comparable relative position, unpublished absolute figures): No. 23 to No. 22 overall, No. 5 to No. 2 in battery storage in one year, No. 1 in nuclear. Headcount statements: "4,000 employees worldwide" (2024), "more than 1,500 openings" (2026). Real estate: a headquarters 60 percent larger than the one it left. "Company-record annual revenue, nearly doubled its backlog and workforce" since 2023 — with the base year being the current CEO's first.

**Self-check:** a proposal from an architect-engineer says it will "deliver the plant as EPC." Next question? *(Who is the constructor, and whose balance sheet backs the lump sum.)*

## Module 4 — Campus power: the owner's engineer between the hyperscaler and the utility

**The single idea:** the scarce input is the energisation date, and the owner's engineer's product is time.

**Grid prospecting and the mock study.** Transmission planners run the utility's own kind of models — power flow, contingency, short-circuit — on public and purchased network data to rank candidate points of interconnection by headroom and likely network-upgrade cost, so that the large-load application is filed at a site the engineer already believes will pass: "rather than waiting for opaque and costly study results." During the utility's study the engineer manages milestones, answers technical queries, negotiates the interconnection agreement and looks for cost-sharing on upgrades.

**Sizing the application from the campus design.** The megawatts requested depend on how much of the campus peak on-site generation and storage will serve; the large-load tariff's minimum bills and credit support depend on the megawatts requested. So the campus layout — medium-voltage systems, cooling, backup generation — is designed to size the application, not after it.

**When the grid says not yet.** Behind-the-meter and bridging generation: gas turbines in simple or combined cycle, reciprocating engines, eventually a reactor. The engineering questions become "interconnection complexity, emissions permitting, fuel availability, technology fit, and lifecycle cost"; for islanded sites the plan must "accommodate forced outage scenarios" with redundancy and black-start capability. Air permits become the critical path — which is why the firm's data-centre conference talks are about permitting.

**The number the lease is written on.** Availability. 99.9 percent is 8.8 hours a year; 99.99 percent is 53 minutes; 99.995 percent is 26 minutes; 99.999 percent is 5 minutes. Per component, availability is MTBF divided by MTBF plus MTTR; series components multiply it down, parallel ones up; a Monte Carlo model runs thousands of simulated years of random failures and repairs to see how often the campus falls short of its service-level agreement. An islanded gas plant at 99.995 percent needs spare units, fuel security and a maintenance plan that never coincides with a forced outage, because turbine forced-outage rates are measured in percent.

**Self-check:** a developer wants to file next month. First deliverable? *(The mock system-impact study — the site and the megawatts are its outputs, and filing before it means waiting for the utility to say no.)*

## Module 5 — Nuclear as an engineering product, and where it fails

**The single idea:** a hyperscaler evaluating a reactor is buying a firm the regulator has already examined.

**Standard plant, site-specific design.** An SMR is designed once as a standard plant so regulators review it once; each site then gets a site-specific design for its soil, seismic conditions, flooding, cooling water and grid. Sargent & Lundy does both — reactor-building standard plant for GE Hitachi's BWRX-300; site-specific detailed design for TVA at Clinch River.

**The licensing package.** A construction permit under 10 CFR Part 50 rests on an environmental report, a preliminary safety analysis report and siting studies, all written by the architect-engineer. Dow and X-energy's Xe-100 plant filed in March 2025 on the firm's paper; the NRC's environmental finding followed in August 2026. And the regulator inspects the engineer: the NRC's 2025 vendor inspection sampled the firm's Palisades, BWRX-300, Clinch River and Dow-site packages and found "No findings of significance."

**Behind the meter, mechanically.** A data centre on the plant's side of the meter buys from the generator through a land lease and a generator-output interconnection, metered separately from the grid. The engineering is a switchyard, metering and protection studies; the argument is regulatory and belongs to the utility plans.

**Where it fails.** The owner's engineer who wants the EPC. The seam between the owner's engineer and the independent engineer — two scopes, not double coverage; the fix is a scope matrix both sign. Reviews under schedule pressure. The or-equal that is not. Factory test witnessed, site test assumed. The mock study that assumed today's grid — headroom lost to another applicant before the real study; the answer is a second site. Queue arbitrage that trips the material-modification rules (FERC Order 845's "equal or better" test). Standards described rather than named — this firm's material never cites NFPA 855, UL 9540A, IEEE 2800 or PRC-029 even though its people sit on the committees; ask which edition and who has applied it. Confidentiality that hides the track record — every data-centre client is "Confidential"; references, not releases, are the check.

**Self-check:** two engineers, one project, and nobody reviewed the augmentation layout. What went wrong? *(A seam. Each assumed the other had it; a signed scope matrix is the fix.)*
