# SoftBank Group — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** SoftBank Group Corp. (TSE: 9984), with SB Energy, DigitalBridge, the OpenAI stake and the Stargate programme · **Written:** 2026-10-04 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1) · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no finance background.

**Purpose:** teach the machinery that decides where a holding company this large is, and is not, a buyer of power equipment:
- how a holding company is valued (net asset value) and funded (loan-to-value, margin loans, stake sales);
- what a large minority stake decides, and why investment gains say nothing about orders;
- what Stargate is — a programme with funders — and who owns each campus;
- what an investor buys when it buys a fund manager, and why equity value and enterprise value differ;
- how a foreign owner is screened, and how a security agreement can reach the supplier list;
- why the landlord, the utility and the generator at one campus can be three different companies;
- the buying-authority test applied to every holding, and the deal ladder applied to every transaction.

No company trivia: founding dates, executives, share counts and deal sizes stay in the dossier. The in-app guide (Profiler → SoftBank → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The **BlackRock** plan teaches the purchase of a manager (GIP) and the deal ladder; Module 4 here applies both to DigitalBridge. The **MGX** plan teaches Stargate from a fellow funder's side. The **SB Energy** plan, written the same day, teaches the one SoftBank company that buys equipment, and should be read next.

**Suggested pacing (about two hours):** Module 1 (~20 min — the foundation), Module 2 (~15 min), Module 3 (~10 min), Module 4 (~20 min), Module 5 (~15 min), Module 6 (~15 min), Module 7 (~15 min — the most important for a seller), then the flashcard and self-test passes in the app.

## Module 1 — What a holding company is, and how it keeps score

**The single idea:** SoftBank does not sell products; it owns stakes, and it is judged by what they are worth and how much it has borrowed against them.

1. **Holding, not operating.** A holding company owns shares in other companies. SoftBank describes itself as a pure holding company: its subsidiaries make chips, run a phone network or build power plants; the parent decides where money goes.
2. **Net asset value (NAV).** Add up the value of every holding — listed shares at market price, private stakes at estimated value — and subtract net debt. SoftBank reported ¥40.1 trillion at 31 March 2026 and ¥72.3 trillion at 30 June 2026. The jump came from higher values for OpenAI and Arm, not from selling anything.
3. **Loan-to-value (LTV).** Net debt divided by the value of the holdings. SoftBank aims to stay below 25% in normal times and never above 35%; it reported 13.0% in June 2026. Because the denominator moves with share prices, LTV can fall without a yen of debt being repaid — and rise just as fast in a market fall.
4. **How the money is raised.** Three ways appear in SoftBank's record: **margin loans** secured on pledged shares (72.0% of Arm's shares were pledged), **selling a stake** (its whole NVIDIA holding in October 2025), and **bridge loans** repaid later from bonds or sales.
5. **The NAV discount.** The market usually values a holding company below its NAV, allowing for its debt, costs and the tax on any sale. Buying back shares or selling a stake at full value narrows the gap.

**Self-check:** Arm's share price rises 20% and SoftBank borrows nothing new. What happens to its loan-to-value? *(It falls — the same debt is now a smaller share of more valuable holdings.)*

## Module 2 — What a 13% stake decides

**The single idea:** a minority shareholder, however large, does not choose a company's suppliers.

1. **Minority against control.** Control means the power to direct a company's decisions — normally a voting majority and the board. SoftBank had invested USD 64.6bn in OpenAI by 1 October 2026 for about 13%, and calls itself 'a minority shareholder of OpenAI' with 'only a limited ability to influence' it.
2. **Why the profit is so large.** SoftBank reports under IFRS, which carries such a stake at its estimated current value. Each funding round that prices OpenAI higher becomes an investment gain: ¥6.7 trillion of SoftBank's FY2025 profit came from OpenAI. The gain is real for SoftBank's balance sheet and says nothing about purchases.
3. **Where SoftBank does control.** SB Energy is the contrast: SoftBank designates six of its nine directors and expects to keep more than half the votes after the IPO, which makes SB Energy a *controlled company* under exchange rules.

**Self-check:** A news story says SoftBank is OpenAI's 'biggest backer'. Does that make SoftBank the right first call for selling switchgear to an OpenAI campus? *(No — find the campus developer; at two campuses that is SoftBank's SB Energy, which is the controlled buyer.)*

## Module 3 — Stargate: funders, operators and developers

**The single idea:** Stargate is an investment programme; the buildings belong to whichever developer builds each campus.

1. **What was announced.** On 22 January 2025, a new company intending to invest USD 500bn over four years in AI infrastructure for OpenAI. Initial equity funders: SoftBank, OpenAI, Oracle and MGX — SoftBank with 'financial responsibility', OpenAI with 'operational responsibility'. No funder has disclosed its commitment.
2. **Who builds.** Oracle's sites are developed by partners such as Crusoe and Vantage; SB Energy develops Milam County, Texas and PORTS-Pike, Ohio; G42 builds Stargate UAE. Each developer runs its own procurement.
3. **How to read a release.** 'Part of Stargate' tells you the customer is OpenAI; it does not tell you who signs for equipment. Look for the named developer and owner of the site.

**Self-check:** Which company buys the medium-voltage switchgear for a Stargate campus? *(The developer that owns or builds that campus — never 'Stargate' itself.)*

## Module 4 — Buying a manager, and the two prices of a deal

**The single idea:** SoftBank bought DigitalBridge, the firm that runs the funds — not the data centres, towers and fibre networks those funds own.

1. **How a fund manager works.** A manager (the general partner) raises money from limited partners — pension funds, sovereign funds, insurers — invests it, and earns a management fee plus a share of gains (carried interest). DigitalBridge reported USD 40.2bn of fee-earning equity at 30 June 2026.
2. **How little of its own money is in.** Its 10-Q gives commitments of 0.03% to 0.72% of each fund, plus 1.43% to 4.29% as a general-partner affiliate in the flagship funds. Vantage, Switch and DataBank belong to the funds and to co-investors such as Silver Lake, PSP, GIC, ADIA, AustralianSuper and IFM.
3. **Equity value against enterprise value.** SoftBank put the price at about USD 3.1bn for all the common stock (USD 16.00 a share) and the enterprise value at about USD 4.0bn. Equity value is what the shares are worth; enterprise value adds debt and preferred stock and subtracts cash. A buyer pays the first; the second measures the whole business. Never state one as the other.
4. **Nine months from signing to closing.** Signed 29 December 2025; stockholders approved 23 April 2026; all regulatory approvals received 22 September; completed 30 September 2026. DigitalBridge's own purchase of ArcLight was signed in May 2026 on condition that the SoftBank merger closed first.

**Self-check:** After the purchase, who decides which transformers Vantage buys? *(Vantage's own management, under the governance of the DigitalBridge-managed funds that own it — SoftBank only through the manager.)*

## Module 5 — A foreign owner, and the security agreement

**The single idea:** a Japanese parent owning US energy and data-centre assets is screened, and the conditions of that screening can reach the supplier list.

1. **CFIUS.** The US Committee on Foreign Investment reviews foreign purchases of US businesses that touch security-sensitive assets, including power and data centres. DigitalBridge's purchase needed its clearance.
2. **The National Security Agreement.** Clearance can come with a binding agreement. SB Energy's S-1 says it operates under one that requires government approval of certain vendors.
3. **Origin rules for equipment.** US clean-energy tax credits exclude equipment tied to a 'foreign entity of concern' (FEOC). A developer chasing those credits screens battery and inverter supply chains.

**Self-check:** Your product wins on price. What else should you check before SB Energy can buy it? *(Whether its origin or software needs approval under the buyer's security agreement, and whether it affects the project's tax-credit eligibility.)*

## Module 6 — Who buys what at one AI campus

**The single idea:** the high-voltage lines, the power plant and the equipment inside the fence can each belong to a different company — even inside one group.

1. **Three layers.** Transmission brings power to the site; generation produces it; inside the fence, substations, transformers and switchgear step it down and distribute it to the data halls.
2. **PORTS-Pike, Ohio.** The utility AEP Ohio builds new 765 kV transmission and SB Energy, as the customer, pays for it. About 9.2 GW of gas-fired generation is planned beside the campus, developed by 'an affiliate of SoftBank that is not our subsidiary', in SB Energy's words — so the turbines or engines are that affiliate's purchase, not SB Energy's. Inside the fence, SB Energy buys high-voltage transformers and switchgear itself.
3. **France.** SoftBank's plan for up to 5 GW includes a Dunkirk facility of its own to manufacture enclosures and one run by Schneider Electric to integrate data-centre power modules — prefabricated electrical rooms built in a factory. It is an announced plan, with no order or volume stated.

**Self-check:** You sell reciprocating engines for on-site generation. Is SB Energy the buyer at PORTS? *(Not for the planned gas generation — that is a separate SoftBank affiliate. SB Energy's S-1 does say its own backup-generation engines are not yet chosen.)*

## Module 7 — The two tests a seller applies

**The single idea:** before treating an investor as a customer, ask whether it controls a buyer (the buying-authority test) and how far each deal has really gone (the deal ladder).

1. **The buying-authority test.** Does the company, or a platform it controls, sign for batteries, medium-voltage switchgear, generation or solid-state transformers? Applied to SoftBank: SB Energy passes; the DigitalBridge platforms pass only through their own management; OpenAI, Arm, Ampere and Graphcore, Stargate and the unclosed ABB Robotics purchase do not.
2. **The deal ladder.** Announced plan (France) → registration filed but not priced (SB Energy's IPO) → signed but awaiting approvals (ABB Robotics) → conditional signing (ArcLight) → closed (DigitalBridge, Ampere). Only the last rung moves money and control.
3. **Door or directory.** SoftBank is a door at SB Energy and a directory everywhere else: the map tells a seller which platforms and developers to approach, but each signs for itself.

**Self-check:** A press report says SoftBank 'has bought' a company, but neither party has announced completion. Which rung do you record? *(The highest rung a primary source confirms — usually 'signed' — and say the report is unconfirmed.)*
