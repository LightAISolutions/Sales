# Southern Company (Georgia Power) — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach how a vertically integrated utility in an elected-commission state certifies a decade of load — what changes in the regulated chain when the regulator runs for office, how Georgia's statute folds the integrated resource plan and the certificate into one three-year cycle, why the utility's own load forecast is the whole argument when there is no grid operator above it, what a large-load rule looks like when its terms are filed under seal, what a rate freeze holds still and what it does not, and what fourteen years of Vogtle did to the buyer's appetite for anything first-of-a-kind — starting from high-school STEM and from the Dominion lesson plan, which this one does not repeat. No company trivia: founding dates, executives and share counts stay in the dossier. Generated 2026-09-03 from the Profiler dossier (profileVersion 1). Companion: the in-app guide (Profiler → Southern Company → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**Prerequisite.** The Dominion Energy lesson plan (`study-prep/dominion-energy/`) teaches the revenue-requirement equation, the IRP → CPCN → RFP sequence, the rate case, the anatomy of a large-load tariff, the interconnection study and the PJM layer. Do that plan first. This plan assumes it and changes one variable at a time: the regulator is elected, there is no RTO, the certificate is statewide and decade-sized, and the buyer has a nuclear overrun in living memory.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min — the elected commission and the missing RTO row), Module 2 in one sitting (~40 min — the certification cycle and the pipeline arithmetic, done by hand), Module 3 in one sitting (~35 min — the rule, the freeze, the fuel line), Module 4 in one sitting (~30 min — Vogtle as the risk memory), Module 5 last (~30 min — the four channels and who picks the equipment), then the flashcard and self-test passes in the app.

## Module 1 — The elected commission, and the grid with nobody above it

**The single idea:** change two variables in the Dominion chain — the regulator runs for office, and there is no grid operator above the utility — and the sequence stays the same while its timing, its exposure and the location of every decision change.

1. **The elected commission.** Georgia's Public Service Commission has five members, one from each of five districts, elected statewide in partisan elections to staggered six-year terms. They decide every rate, plan, certificate, rider and prudence review Georgia Power brings them. An appointed regulator answers to a record; an elected one answers to a record and a ballot. The rate case that raises the residential bill lands in a campaign; the load forecast that doubles the system becomes a town-hall question; a cost cap on a nuclear plant is a promise to voters. The dockets are as long and the testimony as technical as anywhere — but affordability reaches the docket faster, and the composition of the body deciding the next case can change on a Tuesday in November.
2. **What it looked like in 2025–2026.** Six bill increases in three years became the issue in a November 2025 special election; two incumbents lost their seats by roughly 60–40 margins to challengers who campaigned on affordability and data-center cost-shifting — the first change of party on the commission in almost twenty years. The commission that certified nearly ten gigawatts in December 2025 by a unanimous vote was, two months later, a commission that denied reconsideration of that same order three to two. The 2026 general election puts two more seats on the ballot, with grid overbuilding for data centers a stated issue for candidates in both parties. Learn to read the election calendar beside the docket calendar: a three-year rate freeze approved four months before an election, a certificate settled by the outgoing majority, a fuel-clause investigation voted down by the new minority — these are one negotiation, not three.
3. **No RTO.** The Southeast never formed a regional grid operator. Southern Company's operating companies are their own **balancing authority** — the company's control centre matches generation to load every few seconds across three states, dispatches its own plants, holds its own reserves, keeps its own generation queue and builds its own transmission. Since 2022 it can trade fifteen-minute blocks with neighbours through **SEEM**, the Southeast Energy Exchange Market — a bilateral trading platform, not a market operator: no central dispatch, no capacity auction, no queue.
4. **Three consequences for a buyer of capacity.** *No capacity price:* in PJM an auction clears three years ahead and tells everyone what an accredited megawatt is worth; in Georgia the value of a megawatt is whatever the IRP says the system needs to hold its **planning reserve margin**, and the commission decides that in the docket. *No outside queue for the utility's own plants:* a certified self-build joins the company's plan; an independent developer connects through the company's FERC transmission tariff. *The firm resources are the company's own:* gas, nuclear, hydro, coal — so a reserve-margin shortfall is answered by certifying plant, never by bidding into an auction.
5. **The channels narrow.** There is no merchant lane. A battery cannot be built on speculation and sold into a Georgia capacity market because there is none. It enters as company-owned plant — certified, in rate base, built by an EPC the company selects — or under a PPA the company signs with a developer after an all-source RFP that the commission reviews with an independent evaluator. Both lanes start in the IRP and pass through the certification docket; both are decided by the same five people.
6. **The forecast is the whole argument.** With no regional operator publishing an independent load forecast, the commission's staff and the intervenors are the only check on how many gigawatts of data-center demand are real. That is why Module 2's discipline — keeping the tranches of a pipeline apart — matters more here than in a PJM state, where the RTO's forecast and the utility's can be compared.
7. **Who decides what, in one table.** The commission: rates, the triennial IRP, every certificate with its cost cap and construction monitoring, the large-load rule, riders, the fuel clause, the prudence review — a docket with an order, decided by vote of five. The ballot: who the five are. The General Assembly: the IRP statute, the data-center tax exemption, cost-allocation bills, election timing. The utility as balancing authority: second-by-second balancing, the reserve target it proposes, its own queue and transmission plan. FERC and the NRC: wholesale and transmission tariffs; reactor licences and uprates. The missing row is the point — in Virginia the capacity price and the regional transmission plan land on the utility from above; in Georgia every one of those decisions is the commission's, which concentrates authority and political exposure in one elected body.

**Self-check:** a battery developer with a PJM-style merchant business plan asks how to sell into Georgia. Give the two lanes that exist and the one that does not, and say which document each lane starts in. (Company-owned project via the certification docket, or PPA via the all-source RFP — both start in the IRP; the merchant lane does not exist because there is no capacity market to sell into.)

## Module 2 — Certifying a decade: the cycle, the certificate, the pipeline in tranches

**The single idea:** Georgia's statute folds the plan and the certificate into one three-year cycle, so a single order can carry ten gigawatts — and the whole argument inside it is how much of the utility's pipeline to believe.

1. **The cycle.** Every three years Georgia Power files a twenty-year IRP; the commission rules on it; and within months a separate certification docket asks to certify the specific plants and purchases the plan called for — Georgia's CPCN, though the state does not use the name. A mid-cycle update (2023) can certify too. The 2025 sequence: plan filed January (8,200 MW of load growth over six years), approved July with the forecast revised to about 8,500 MW; certification requested July 31 (about 9,900 MW); stipulation with staff December 10; certificate December 19, five to nothing, all 9,885 MW at each project's certified cost.
2. **What a certificate carries.** Three things. A **certified cost**, which works as a **cost cap** — spend above it is the utility's to justify in a later prudence review. **Construction monitoring** — periodic commission review of schedule and spend while the plant is built, adopted for Vogtle and kept for everything since. And entry into **rate base** on completion, which is the economic point. The December 2025 order added a fourth: a **customer-benefit condition** (at least $556 million a year of downward residential pressure in 2029–2031 from large-load revenue) and a **shareholder backstop** for those three years if the loads do not arrive.
3. **What was in it.** Three combined-cycle blocks at existing plant sites (about 1,480 MW at Bowen, 1,450 at Wansley, 760 at McIntosh); ten stand-alone batteries at eight sites (3,022 MW) and two solar-plus-storage projects (350 MW); 2,821 MW of purchases from third parties and affiliates — about $16.3 billion of company-owned capital. Roughly 60 percent gas by capacity. Close to half of the ~22 GW Georgia Power deploys today, in one order.
4. **What was argued.** Staff had recommended certifying about a third and called the rest speculative — only about 1,900 MW of the load was backed by executed contracts when the request was filed — then settled for the whole request in exchange for the backstop. Two months later the reconstituted commission denied reconsideration three to two; environmental and faith groups sued in March 2026 to decertify at least the McIntosh block. The certificate stands while the case runs. That sequence — unanimous certification, contested reconsideration, litigation — is what an elected commission changing hands mid-cycle looks like from a seller's chair.
5. **The pipeline in tranches.** *Pipeline* is every request received; *committed* (Georgia's word for a signed request for service) is a customer that has signed something short of a service agreement; *contracted* is load under an executed agreement with a minimum bill and collateral, submitted to the regulator. Only the third is bankable. Southern's parent publishes a four-rung ladder every quarter — contracted, finalizing, late-stage, prospective — with a written definition of contracted; between Q3 2025 and Q2 2026 the contracted rung went from 8 GW to 17 GW while the prospective rung stayed at 75 GW-plus and was never counted as load.
6. **Do the arithmetic.** 50 GW of pipeline, 10 GW committed, 3 GW contracted, a 17 GW peak. The 3 GW can be financed against; the 10 GW is a forecast with deposits behind it; the 37 GW is a queue the utility itself keeps, with no RTO to compare it to. A plan that certifies 8 GW is betting the committed tranche mostly converts; one that certifies 3 GW is betting it mostly does not. Neither is wrong; each is a judgment about a conversion rate the commission will defend at the next election.
7. **The seller's timing.** The Yates turbines were ordered around the 2023 update, two years before the plan that justified them — lead times had stretched to five years or more. The 2024 battery portfolio's supplier and its two EPCs were named a year before certification. The next storage solicitation (500 MW) closes its short list in December 2026 with contracts in January 2027; the next capacity request (2,000–6,000 MW for 2032–2033) is certified in mid-2027. The certificate makes the money recoverable; the purchases were decided in the plan and the solicitation, by the utility's engineers and the bidding developers.

**Self-check:** a commission certifies 9,885 MW when executed contracts support about 1,900 MW. Name the two instruments in the order that answer the stranded-cost objection, and say for how long each runs. (The customer-benefit condition — $556 million a year of downward pressure — and the shareholder backstop; both cover 2029–2031, three years, against plants that run forty. That gap is the reconsideration dissent.)

## Module 3 — The rule, the freeze and the fuel line

**The single idea:** Georgia binds a data center with a commission rule plus a confidential contract rather than a published rate class; a rate freeze holds one line of the bill still while the others keep moving; and the cost-allocation fight migrates from docket to docket.

1. **The rule.** Adopted unanimously January 23, 2025, effective February 1, written out of the 2023 plan-update docket. Any new customer above 100 MW can be served on terms beyond the standard tariff: contract lengths moved from five to fifteen years, minimum billing requirements, payment for upstream generation, transmission and distribution as construction proceeds, termination payments, collateral — and every such contract goes to the commission at least thirty days before it is signed. The company's own framework, from its investor decks: at least fifteen-year terms for data centers, minimum bills covering at least 100 percent of annual incremental cost to serve, termination payments tied to the remaining incremental cost, collateral by creditworthiness — more than $21 billion of it by 2028.
2. **Against Virginia's GS-5.** Same shape, different transparency. GS-5 publishes its numbers — 85 percent minimum on contracted transmission-and-distribution demand, 60 percent on generation, a 14-year term (four-year ramp plus ten). Georgia's percentages of demand live in contracts filed under seal; the company states the categories, not the numbers. Treat any specific Georgia minimum-bill percentage in trade press as unverified.
3. **The first tested contract.** OpenAI's ~3,200 MW Effingham County campus cleared the thirty-day window in August 2026 on a staff statement, not a vote, with added protections: no recovery of a revenue shortfall from residential customers on early termination, notice to the commission within fifteen days of any large-customer cancellation, semiannual public reporting on the portfolio. The lesson: Georgia's protections are negotiated contract by contract, so one deal's terms are not the next one's rule.
4. **The freeze.** July 1, 2025, unanimous: base rates flat for 2026, 2027 and 2028, after six bill increases in three years and four months before the special election. What it holds still is the rate-case line. What keeps moving: the **fuel clause** (pass-through, no return), storm-cost recovery (a separate case), Vogtle's recovery (its own order). Deferred costs accumulate as regulatory assets for a base rate case that must be filed by July 2028 — the same year the backstop begins, which is why the certificate had to say something about 2029–2031.
5. **The fuel line.** May 2026: a fuel-and-storm stipulation cut the typical bill by about $4 a month, capped hedging, stretched Helene storm amortization to 67 months — and, three to two, declined the two new commissioners' motions to investigate whether large customers were raising everyone else's fuel cost. Staff had put that effect at several percent of the monthly fuel bill. A separate hearing on recovering a regulatory asset only from large customers was due by December 2026. The cross-subsidy argument does not live in one docket in Georgia: rate case → certificate → fuel clause → the next election.

**Self-check:** a Georgia data-center developer asks what its minimum bill will be. What can you tell it from public sources, and what can you not? (Fifteen-year term, minimum bill at 100 percent of incremental cost to serve, termination payments, collateral by creditworthiness, thirty-day commission review — from the rule and the decks. The percentage of contracted demand: not public; it is in the sealed contract.)

## Module 4 — Vogtle: the risk memory

**The single idea:** fourteen years from certificate to second unit, a cost more than doubled, one bankruptcy, one monitoring regime that outlived the project and one recovery order that split the overrun — every later decision this buyer makes is legible against it.

1. **The sequence.** Certified 2009 at about $14 billion for all owners, with financing costs charged during construction (**CWIP** in rate base — customers paying for a plant before it produces anything, which lowers financing cost and moves construction risk to customers). Construction monitoring from the start, semi-annual, numbered past twenty-nine. First-of-a-kind design changes, module fabrication problems, a contractor that had never built the design in the United States. The reactor vendor's bankruptcy in 2017; the owners took over with a new constructor and the commission voted to continue. Testing found more problems; the pandemic slipped the schedule again; co-owners invoked cost-sharing provisions.
2. **The outcome.** Unit 3 commercial July 31, 2023; Unit 4 April 29, 2024. Roughly $35 billion for all owners. Georgia Power's net investment: $10.670 billion excluding about $440 million of capitalized AFUDC. The December 2023 recovery order, unanimous: customers pay about $7.6 billion of Georgia Power's remaining cost, about $9 a month on a typical bill; about $3 billion of the overrun stays with shareholders — the prudence review every certificate promises, delivered.
3. **The operating record.** Company: availability above 95 percent since commercial operation; expected site output above 30 million MWh a year; first refueling outages in the autumn of each unit's second year. Regulator: a White finding on Unit 3 for two 2024 shutdowns traced to an over-conservative safety-system design, closed to Green in March 2025. Capacity factors for the two new units alone are not published.
4. **What it did to the appetite.** The 2025 plan and certificate contain gas, batteries, purchases and uprates of the older units — Vogtle 1 and 2 (54 MW) and Hatch — and no new reactor. The chief executive's July 2026 sentence: Southern is 'not going to be next' to build an AP1000 unless others share the overrun risk. A seller offering anything first-of-a-kind to this buyer is selling to a commission and a company that have already lived through one.
5. **The instruments it left behind.** Construction monitoring as standard practice; the certified cost as a cap; the 2025 backstop; the rule that every large-load contract is filed before signing. These are institutional memory, not policy innovation.

**Self-check:** why did the 2025 certificate include 54 MW of uprates at forty-year-old reactors and zero megawatts of new nuclear, when the same company just finished two new units that run at 95 percent availability? (Because the buyer's risk appetite was set by the overrun and its recovery order, not by the operating record; uprates use a site, a licence and operators that already exist, and the certified cost is a cap the company has learned to respect.)

## Module 5 — Southern against the chain: the four channels, who picks the equipment, who competes

**The single idea:** the December 2025 certificate opened four procurement channels with four different buyers of record; a fifth and sixth run on their own calendars; and in only one of them does the utility itself choose the battery.

1. **Self-build gas.** Bowen, Wansley, McIntosh combined-cycle blocks (2029–2030) and the Yates simple-cycle units (three Mitsubishi Power M501JAC machines, ~1.3 GW, 2027–2028, certified in the 2023 update). The utility's own engineering and procurement selects the turbine OEM and the EPC; the IRP tested the self-build against third-party bids in an all-source RFP with an independent evaluator. Equipment reserved before certification; McIntosh self-performed by the company's own construction team. Seller's entry: turbine and balance-of-plant supply to the utility's project teams, years before the certificate. The combined-cycle turbine OEM is not yet disclosed.
2. **Utility-owned storage.** Mossy Branch (Wärtsilä, 2024); the 765 MW portfolio (Robins, Moody, Hammond, McGrau Ford I and II — EPCs Burns & McDonnell and Crowder; supplier Tesla Megapack 2XL per trade press, unnamed by the company); Twiggs (200 MW, 2027) and Wadley (260 MW, 2027); 3,022 MW more certified in December 2025 at Bowen, Hammond, McIntosh, South Hall, Thomson, Wadley, Wansley and Yates; Alabama Power's Gorgas (150 MW, 92 Tesla Megapacks, 2027). The utility signs the supply agreement and hires the EPC. Seller's entry: a supply agreement with the utility, or subcontract to its EPC — the one lane where the utility, not a developer, picks the BESS.
3. **Purchases.** 2,821 MW of PPAs in the certificate — a 930 MW twenty-year contract from 2030, five 25-year contracts totalling 646 MW from 2027, affiliate pieces from Southern Power (732 MW) and Mississippi Power's Plant Daniel (a 750 MW coal-and-gas PPA extended in part through 2029). The bidding developer or generator chooses its own equipment; the utility chooses the bid; the commission approves the contract. Seller's entry: the developer, never the utility.
4. **Uprates and upgrades.** Vogtle 1–2 (54 MW), Hatch engineering, McIntosh +268 MW, coal at Bowen and Scherer kept to at least 2034, hydro modernization, Southern Power's Franklin and Wansley turbine uprates (400 MW, 2029–2031). OEM and owner's-engineer work on the federal or the plant's own clock.
5. **The fifth and sixth channels.** The 500 MW storage RFP (short list December 2026, contracts January 2027, service by 2031) — the developer lane for Georgia storage, with Ascend Analytics as evaluator. The 2026 customer-identified-resource program — a large customer brings its own clean energy above 3 MW under a 75/25 savings split; no capacity credit, no displacement of planned gas. Neither had a winner at the time of writing.
6. **Who picks the battery — the two lanes.** *Utility-owned:* one buyer known in advance, supplier named before the certificate, warranties direct to the utility, the allowed return earned on it; costs filed under a confidentiality rule, slower, one qualification list. *Developer's bid:* many buyers, price-driven, faster to lock the bill of materials, politically protected because the commission wants the self-build tested; but the utility earns nothing on a PPA and argues for the self-build wherever the numbers are close, a losing bidder has no merchant fallback in a state with no capacity market, and a bid price can expire while a contested certificate runs.
7. **Gigawatts on the clock.** Georgia Power deploys ~22 GW today; the accepted forecast adds 8,448 MW by winter 2030/31; 9,885 MW certified, 6,804 of it owned; Georgia Power's contracted large load 12.5 GW of the parent's 17; 32 committed customers at 15,600 MW; the next solicitation up to 6,000 MW. Off the chart: the 75 GW-plus prospective pipeline. The two figures that are load are the contracted 12.5 GW and, with the company's definition attached, the committed 15.6 GW.
8. **Where it fails, in one line each.** The date moves — the utility keeps its own queue and builds its own wires across 330-plus parcels. The commission changes hands mid-cycle. The tranche evaporates — 14.3 GW of projects exited in a year. The certified cost becomes the cap. The freeze ends in the year the backstop begins. No price signal — no capacity market, no merchant lane. Sold to the wrong desk — the commission's staff decide whether a plant is needed, never which battery is in it.
9. **Who competes.** For the certificate's gas: one J-class OEM at Yates and, by inference only, at the other three sites. For owned storage: whichever integrator the utility qualifies — Wärtsilä once, Tesla (per trade press) four times, unnamed for the ten December projects. For PPAs: every developer that can bid an all-source RFP in a state where the contract is the only revenue. For the customers: Dominion's GS-5 and Georgia's rule are the two Southeastern templates hyperscalers compare when choosing a state.

**Self-check:** a battery integrator has one week in Atlanta. Rank the three meetings — Georgia Power's generation procurement team, a developer preparing a bid for the 500 MW RFP, and the commission's public-interest staff — by likelihood of producing a purchase order, and say why the third is last. (Procurement team first — the owned lane is real and the utility picks the battery; the developer second — it picks the hardware for the PPA lane; staff last — they decide need, never equipment.)

## Whole-plan self-test

1. What two variables does this plan change from the Dominion chain, and what stays the same? (The regulator is elected and there is no RTO; the sequence plan → certificate → solicitation → rate order is unchanged.)
2. Who sets the value of a megawatt of capacity in Georgia, and through which document? (The commission, through the IRP's planning reserve margin and the certificate — no auction.)
3. What is SEEM, and what does it not do? (A bilateral fifteen-minute trading platform; not a market operator — no central dispatch, capacity auction or queue.)
4. What three things does a Georgia certificate carry? (Certified cost as a cap, construction monitoring, entry into rate base.)
5. Define pipeline, committed and contracted, and name the bankable one. (Requests received; signed requests for service short of an ESA; executed agreements with minimum bills and collateral — only contracted.)
6. What did the January 2025 rule require, and what did it leave confidential? (Extended terms above 100 MW — fifteen-year contracts, minimum bills, upstream costs, termination payments, collateral, thirty-day filing; the percentages of demand.)
7. Which lines of the bill keep moving during the base-rate freeze? (Fuel, storm recovery, separately ordered items.)
8. What did the 2025 certificate's backstop cover, and what was the dissent's objection? (2029–2031 new-plant costs kept off residential customers; three years against forty-year plants.)
9. How was Vogtle's overrun split, and what did that do to the 2025 certificate's contents? (About $7.6 billion to customers, about $3 billion to shareholders; gas, batteries, purchases and uprates — no new reactor.)
10. In which of the four channels does the utility choose the battery, and who chooses it in the PPA channel? (Utility-owned storage; the bidding developer.)
