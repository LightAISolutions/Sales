# Talen Energy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach what it means to sell power from a plant that already exists — what an independent power producer is and what its three revenue streams actually are, the difference between putting a data centre behind a generator's meter and in front of it, which contract has to be amended to allow the first, what capacity interconnection rights are and why they behave like property, what the federal regulator decided in November 2024 and — just as important — what it did not decide, how a rejected co-location was rebuilt in eighteen months as an ordinary retail supply that needs nobody's permission, why the additionality objection follows every deal of this kind, and how being paid *not* to close a plant works. ## Module 1 — The merchant generator: three revenue streams, one price-taker

**The single idea:** a company that owns plants and has no customers earns from three separate mechanisms, and confusing them is the most common mistake a newcomer makes.

1. **What "independent power producer" means.** It owns generation and sells the output. It has no captive customers, no rate base and no commission setting its prices — the opposite of the utilities taught in the Dominion, Southern, Entergy and Xcel plans. Its cost of capital and its exposure to prices are both higher.
2. **Energy.** Payment for electricity actually produced, settled every five minutes at the price set by the last unit needed to meet demand. Every unit that cleared receives that price, not its own cost. Fuel cost therefore decides *how often* a plant runs, not *what it is paid*.
3. **Capacity.** Payment for being available on a future delivery day, bought in an auction typically three years ahead. A plant that never runs still collects it. This is the stream that transformed the sector: one company's capacity revenue went from $192 million in 2024 to $485 million in 2025 to $444 million in the first half of 2026 alone, with no change to the plants. What changed was the clearing price — $49.49 per megawatt-day for the 2024/2025 year, then $269.92, $329.17, $333.44 and $325.00.
4. **Ancillary services.** Payment for the fast, small adjustments that hold system frequency steady.
5. **Why a data centre is a different buyer.** A trader buys a megawatt-hour to resell. A data centre buys certainty for fifteen or twenty years and pays a premium for it. That premium is why merchant generators are now selling to computer companies. It is also a twenty-year promise the seller has to keep in years when the market pays far more — hence make-whole clauses, credit support and letters of credit.

**Self-check:** a plant's fuel gets cheaper. Which of its three revenue streams changes directly, and which changes only through the plant running more hours? (None changes directly; cheaper fuel moves the plant down the merit order so it runs more hours and earns more energy revenue at unchanged prices.)

## Module 2 — Behind the meter, in front of it, and the document in between

**The single idea:** the physics of serving a neighbouring load is trivial; the paperwork is the whole subject.

1. **Behind the meter.** Power goes from the generator to the load without passing the point where the plant connects to the grid. There is no delivery, so there are few or no network charges. That is the commercial attraction and, simultaneously, the regulatory objection.
2. **In front of the meter.** The plant sells onto the grid; the customer draws the same amount back through an ordinary utility connection and pays the normal transmission, delivery and ancillary charges. Structurally it is two unrelated transactions that happen to net out.
3. **The interconnection service agreement.** Every generator has one — a contract between the plant, the transmission owner whose wires it uses and the grid operator. It states the point of connection, how much may be injected, the protective equipment and the studies the permission rests on. Susquehanna's is Service Agreement No. 1442, between PJM, PPL Electric Utilities and Susquehanna Nuclear.
4. **Capacity interconnection rights.** The firm megawatts of injection the plant is entitled to, established by studies someone paid for. They behave like property. The station's existing agreement already permitted **300 MW** of co-located load; the amendment asked to raise that to **480 MW**, with a longer path to 960 MW.
5. **Why this is not a private matter.** Changing what sits behind a filed connection agreement is a filing, and filings are decided by the regulator. Note also the origin of the rules: they were written for **station power** — the pumps, fans and controls a plant needs to run itself, a few percent of output. A gigawatt of computers is the same rule asked to carry a different scale.

**Self-check:** two identical data centres, one behind the meter and one in front. Which pays more, and for what? (The front-of-meter one, for transmission, delivery and ancillary services — the network it is using either way.)

## Module 3 — What the regulator decided, and the two years that followed

**The single idea:** the amendment was rejected on burden of proof, expressly without prejudice — which is not the same as a ruling that co-location is unlawful, and the difference explains everything that came next.

1. **The order.** 1 November 2024, docket ER24-2172, citation 189 FERC ¶ 61,078, two votes to one. The holding: the grid operator "has not demonstrated that the proposed non-conforming provisions … are necessary deviations from the pro forma ISA due to specific reliability concerns, novel legal issues, or other unique factors."
2. **The concurrence and the dissent.** The concurring commissioner wrote that the rejection was "without prejudice" and that the arrangement "may make sense and be acceptable under the Federal Power Act, but on this record that claim simply has not been proven." The dissenting chairman thought the majority had missed "the forest for the trees."
3. **Who was on which side.** The protest came from two utility holding companies; the market monitor also opposed. Two competing merchant generators filed *in support*. The customer's own energy subsidiary intervened. Read a docket's intervenor list as a map of who thinks they gain and who thinks they lose.
4. **What followed, in order.** A show-cause proceeding opened February 2025; a December 2025 order finding the grid operator's tariff "unjust and unreasonable" for co-located load and directing three new categories of transmission service into existence; a compliance filing in February 2026 that drew protests from four separate parties; further guidance in April 2026 recognising three interconnection pathways; and in June 2026 show-cause orders to **all six** regional grid operators, with comments due September 2026.
5. **The generalisable lesson.** When a technology outruns its rulebook, the first company through the door does not get a ruling. It gets a process — and it usually finds a commercial route around the process before the process ends.

**Self-check:** why does "rejected without prejudice on burden of proof" produce more litigation than a ruling on the merits? (Because it settles nothing. The question stays open, so it is asked again — here, four more times in twenty months.)

## Module 4 — The workaround, and why it cost so little

**The single idea:** the restructured deal was designed so that no regulator has to approve it, and the megawatts it gave back nearly paid for the concession.

1. **The structure.** The plant sells its output into the market. The seller acts as the customer's licensed retail electricity supplier in that state. The local wires utility handles transmission and delivery. Three ordinary transactions, none of which needs a new approval.
2. **What made it possible.** An unconditioned retail supplier licence the seller already held, in a state with retail choice. In a regulated state there is no retail supplier to be, and the route does not exist.
3. **The commercial terms, in the seller's own disclosure.** Up to **1,920 MW through 2042** with extension options; a ramp band of 240–960 MW in 2026 rising to 1,680–1,920 MW by 2032; about **$18 billion** of nominal cash flows at the minimum ramp; a shortfall make-whole capped at an estimated 50–65 percent of the full impact if nothing is drawn. The filing calls the pricing "fixed-price" at "anticipated premium prices"; trade press reports 2 percent annual escalators from 2028. The base price is not disclosed.
4. **What the seller got back.** Moving the load into the operator's demand forecast restored **148 MW** of capacity interconnection rights to the plant, which re-entered the capacity auction. The reliability objection disappeared because the plant's output is again fully visible to the market.
5. **What it gave up.** The customer now pays network charges. The arrangement is no longer distinctive — anyone with a plant and a supplier licence can copy it. Two years of appeal costs ended in voluntary dismissal.

**Self-check:** the same restructuring is proposed in a state without retail choice. What breaks? (There is no retail supplier licence to use, so the seller cannot be the customer's supplier; the power would have to be sold to the incumbent utility or under a different structure entirely.)

## Module 5 — Additionality, being paid not to close, and reading a contracted megawatt

**The single idea:** three separate disciplines that decide whether a deal of this kind is durable, defensible and correctly sized.

1. **Additionality.** Selling an operating reactor's output creates no new megawatt-hours; the displaced buyer is served by whatever is next in the merit order, usually gas. Sellers answer this three ways: the plant might otherwise have closed (strong for a marginal unit, weak for cheap nuclear); the contract funds new capacity (an uprate, a new reactor); the load pays its way through network charges. The policy answer arrived in August 2026, when one state conditioned preferential permitting for data centres above 25 MW on sourcing from **new** in-zone supply.
2. **Reliability-must-run.** A generator that wants to retire files a deactivation notice. If the operator finds the system cannot yet cope, it can require the plant to keep running under a cost-of-service agreement. Two plants totalling 2.0 GW earn **$312** and **$137 per megawatt-day** — about $180 million a year — plus fuel and variable cost reimbursement, with **no capacity obligation and no performance penalty**, paid by one state's ratepayers. That last feature is why the state's consumer advocate argues the payment exceeds cost by $83 million a year and has litigated it twice.
3. **Reading a contracted megawatt.** One deal produces six different real numbers: full contract quantity 1,920 MW; first-year maximum 960 MW; first-year minimum 240 MW; behind-the-meter load originally permitted 300 MW; behind-the-meter load requested and refused 480 MW; interconnection rights restored 148 MW. Never accept one figure without asking which it is.
4. **Reading the accounts.** A generator that has sold next year forward books unrealised losses when prices rise, because the hedge is now below market — even though the business improved. In one half-year this company reported a loss and generated record cash. Read the cash-flow statement.
5. **Where the risk concentrates.** One station carries the contract. A refuelling outage that ran 72 days against a 30–35-day norm cost roughly $34 million of margin, and the company's own risk factors say a significant disruption there could impair its ability to perform.

**Self-check:** a contract is announced as "1,920 MW through 2042" with a first-year band of 240–960 MW. What is the enforceable volume in year one, and what is the rest? (240 MW is the obligation; everything above it to 960 MW is the buyer's option.)

## Whole-plan self-test

1. Name the three parties to an interconnection service agreement and say what the document fixes.
2. Explain the difference between behind-the-meter co-location and front-of-the-meter supply in terms of who pays for the network.
3. State what the November 2024 order decided and what it explicitly left open.
4. Describe the mechanism by which going front-of-the-meter restored capacity interconnection rights to the plant.
5. Give the additionality objection in one sentence and the strongest answer to it.
6. Explain why a reliability-must-run plant is not a capacity resource, and who pays for it.
7. Explain why a hedged merchant can report a loss and record cash in the same six months.
8. Say what it means when three consecutive capacity auctions clear at the administrative price cap.
