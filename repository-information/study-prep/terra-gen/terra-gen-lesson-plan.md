# Terra-Gen — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Terra-Gen (a long-lived owner-operator running a multi-vendor wind, solar and storage fleet across many sites) · **Written:** 2026-09-07 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, plus the first buyer-side course in this corpus; no asset-management or contract-administration background.

**What this teaches.** The twenty to thirty years after the ribbon is cut, from the owner's chair. Asset management as a distinct function from field operations and from the trading desk — the commercial custody of the contracts and their obligations, the covenants and reporting, the twenty-year capital plan, and the decision to spend money on an asset already built — and why it is the layer that scales worst, matters most and is reliably underfunded, because its output when it works is the absence of a surprise. Four different measurements that all get called availability, with the excluded-event list identified as where the negotiation actually lives, and a fleet average identified as a reporting convenience rather than a measure of exposure. The machinery of enforcing a capacity guarantee: a demonstration that costs a day out of the market whether or not a shortfall is suspected, operating records without which the supplier's first defence stands, notice deadlines as the commonest way a sound claim is lost, and a remedy ladder whose escalation mechanism matters more than its rungs. Augmentation as a scheduled capital programme that must be designed into a site years before it is needed, with the vintage-mixing constraint that follows. The operating bill for a mixed-vendor fleet, which is where the procurement decision to diversify is actually paid. Fleet telemetry read from the owner's side, where twenty-nine normal sites are the control group that turns an anomaly into evidence. Self-perform against a wrapped service agreement, and why a wrap defers rather than removes a twenty-year problem. And the close of the buyer-side course.

**Why this gap.** The integrator course covers how a capacity promise is written, priced and kept, and it covers fleet telemetry as a supplier's product. The first buyer-side course covers dispatch. Nothing in the corpus covers the owner's own operating organisation: what it holds, what a claim actually requires, how augmentation is budgeted and designed for, or what a second equipment platform costs in year eleven. The corpus also had no guide that closed the buyer-side course, which now runs to nine guides across two sessions.

**Its place in the portfolio-owner set.** Fourth of four, and the closing guide. It receives three threads: the correlated-failure argument from the Plus Power guide, which reappears here as the reason a fleet average conceals exposure; the balance-sheet vocabulary from the NextEra Energy Resources guide, which it uses to ask who should hold a twenty-year obligation; and the technology-risk thread from the Lightsource bp guide. It closes the **buyer-side** course specifically — the four-course synthesis across supplier, integrator, contractor and buyer was written by the Samsung C&T guide and is not repeated.

## Module 1 — The layer between the desk and the field
Three jobs that all get called operations. What commercial custody consists of, expressed as obligations rather than activities. Why it scales worst: a contractor can be paid to perform an obligation but cannot be made liable for one. Why it is underfunded, and a worked example — a shortfall discovered, recorded and not notified inside the contractual window, with no technical failure anywhere in the sequence.

## Module 2 — Four things called availability
Technical, contractual, value-weighted and fleet availability: what each counts, what it is used for, how each misleads. The excluded-event list as the substance of the guarantee. And the fleet-average problem, in which the correlation argument from the first guide of this course returns in financial form — the averaging that makes a portfolio look stable stops working precisely when a common-mode fault makes it matter.

## Module 3 — Enforcing a guarantee
Three requirements, none automatic. A measurement both parties accept, costing a day out of the market on a schedule the contract rather than the equipment decides. Records proving the duty stayed inside the envelope, in a form the supplier's engineers accept, which cannot be assembled retrospectively. Notice, and the deadline that turns a valid claim into an accepted loss. Then the remedy ladder — repair, augmentation, damages, termination — and why automatic escalation is worth more than most of the numbers in the contract. The repeat-player asymmetry, and what a fleet-level claims capability is actually for.

## Module 4 — Augmentation is a date
Why degradation is predictable and the capacity floor is therefore scheduled rather than discovered. Augmentation as a rolling campaign a portfolio runs more cheaply than a project. What must be designed in years ahead: layout space, conversion and transformer headroom, switchgear positions, cable sizing, thermal and separation allowance. Who pays, depending on where the promise sits, and the cell-price assumption sitting inside a twenty-year budget. The vintage-mixing constraint and why it is resolved at string or block level rather than at module level.

## Module 5 — The operating bill for a mixed fleet
Spares that cannot be pooled, control platforms that each need integration and re-testing, platform-specific technician certification that is often a warranty condition, per-supplier claim procedures, and data that was never designed to be compared. The point of the module is not concentration — the commercial and correlated-failure arguments for a second platform both stand — but that diversity is bought at procurement and paid for in operations, in another budget and another year, by people who were not in the room.

## Module 6 — The data loop from the owner's side
What thirty near-identical sites permit that one does not, and why the control group is the whole point. The fleet dataset as the evidence base for every future claim, and the difference between having evidence and having an argument. Who owns the data — generated by the BMS and EMS, stored on a vendor's platform, accessed by a contractor, and granted to the owner as a dashboard rather than a record. Why access that ends with a contract is not ownership, and why independent extraction from day one is cheap then and impossible later.

## Module 7 — Employ the people or buy the outcome
Self-perform with vendor support against a wrapped long-term service agreement. Visible cost, accumulated knowledge, revenue-led response and data by default, against a standing hiring problem, warranty exposure, untransferred risk and a fleet-size floor. Contracted availability, certified labour, a known annual cost and a supplier's fleet-wide experience, against learning nothing, uniform response priorities, exclusions as the whole negotiation, and a term half the length of the asset's life. Why the decision is partly about who you expect to sell to.

## Module 8 — What these four guides were arguing
The nine-guide arc across both sessions and what each of the four portfolio guides owns: repetition, scale, distance, duration. The organising claim — every other party in the chain sells something with a term, and the owner holds what is left. Why that reading explains the practice that most puzzles outside readers, namely selling assets one has just finished building: the residual belongs with the cheapest, longest-horizon capital, which is rarely the party best at building. And the closing distinction between the two courses' questions — what is this project worth, against what is this obligation worth and who should carry it.

## Pacing
Eight modules, roughly 15–20 minutes each. Modules 2 and 3 are the operational core and should be read together — a reader short of time who reads only those will still know why a guarantee is not the same thing as a remedy. Module 4 is the one to read before designing anything. Module 8 is the course close and should be read last, after the other three guides in the set.
