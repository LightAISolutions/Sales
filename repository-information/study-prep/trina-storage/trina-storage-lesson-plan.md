# Trina Storage — Technology Lesson Plan

**Subject:** Trina Storage (storage arm of a listed solar manufacturer; vertically integrated into its own cells and selling fully wrapped systems with long service agreements) · **Written:** 2026-09-06 · **Baseline assumed:** high-school STEM, no warranty, service-contract or provisioning background.

**What this teaches.** The reframing the whole guide turns on: the product is a twenty-year obligation and the hardware is how it is delivered. The five distinct promises usually collapsed into 'the warranty', how each is measured and remedied, and the asymmetry by which the defect warranty expires long before the capacity guarantee. The guaranteed capacity curve as a floor rather than a forecast, steep early and flat later. Three strategies for keeping it — oversizing, augmentation, replacement — and what each bets on. The duty envelope that converts the guarantee into an energy-throughput budget, and therefore makes every dispatch decision a spend of something already paid for. The warranty reserve, what sizes it, and why aggressive terms from a thin balance sheet are a warning. What a long-term service agreement actually contains, with the exclusions as the revealing part. And the obligations that survive the twentieth year.

**Why this gap.** The corpus carried capacity-maintenance and availability-guarantee concepts and taught the buyer's side of a storage contract, but it had never described the supplier's side of the same promise: what it costs to give, how it is provisioned, why service and warranty are sold as one package, and what happens at the end. That is the commercial substance of being an integrator, and it was missing.

**Its place in the integrator set.** Sixth of seven, and the one that names what is being sold. It draws its degradation model from the Envision guide's fleet telemetry and its defect-rate assumption from the Sunwoda guide's manufacturing statistics — the two inputs that turn a reserve into a calculation. It defers revenue and dispatch to the merchant-storage guides and the offtake contract to the Arevon guide, taking the supplier-to-owner contract instead, and it hands the solvency question directly to the closing guide.

## Module 1 — The hardware is the delivery mechanism
What a buyer is actually purchasing, and why the reframing explains the coupling of warranty to service, the scrutiny of a supplier's balance sheet, and the fact that the last guide in the course is about credit rather than engineering.

## Module 2 — The five promises
Capacity retention, availability, efficiency, power and response, and freedom from defects — with what each measures and what remedy attaches. The trap in the last row: a component failing in the second decade is not a defect claim, so it is the service agreement rather than the warranty that keeps a plant running late in life.

## Module 3 — The shape of the promise
An illustrative retained-capacity schedule from one hundred percent to about seventy over twenty years. Three properties: steep early and flat later because fade slows with age, so an early test predicts poorly; a floor rather than a forecast, with the gap to the supplier's own expectation as its safety margin; and a commitment conditional on a duty assumption.

## Module 4 — Three ways to keep it
Oversizing as the simplest and least capital-efficient, independent of future supply. Augmentation as capital spent when needed and probably cheaper, dependent on reserved space, awkward generation mixing, and the supplier still existing. Replacement as the cleanest and most expensive, dependent on a form factor that the format ladder makes unlikely to survive a decade. Why the real choice is a blend and why the third dependency points at the next guide.

## Module 5 — The warranty as a budget
The duty envelope — cycles, depth of discharge, temperature, rate, state-of-charge dwell — and the throughput budget it defines. Why cycling harder to capture a price spends life already purchased. Why the envelope is what makes the promise underwritable at all. The instruments that resolve the friction, all of them variants of buying more envelope. Monitoring as evidence for both parties simultaneously, and one of the few aligned incentives in the relationship.

## Module 6 — Who pays for the promise
The warranty reserve, and the two models that size it. The structural temptation to under-reserve an aggressive curve and book margin now. Why suppliers will not extend long warranties without holding the service contract — sound in both directions — and what the coupling costs an owner in flexibility.

## Module 7 — Inside a service agreement
Eight elements with what to look at closely in each: preventive maintenance and consumables, corrective maintenance and whether the obligation is to attend or to restore, response commitments and when the clock starts, monitoring and who owns the data, spares against obsolescence, augmentation scope against reserved space, exclusions as where disputes live, and end-of-term condition and surviving obligations.

## Module 8 — What is owed at the end
End of life as an economic decision rather than a technical event. Decommissioning as a cost that must sit somewhere and increasingly be reserved for. Recycling as a design input, with the density race carrying a quiet dismantling cost. Second life as more attractive in principle than practice, and best where a complete history arrives with the modules. Environmental declarations as another market-access gate. The closing observation that none of it is worth anything unless the promising party is still solvent.

## Pacing
Eight modules, roughly 20–25 minutes each. Modules 2–4 are the technical half and belong together. Module 5 is the one that changes how a reader thinks about dispatch and pairs directly with the Jupiter Power guide. Modules 6–8 are the commercial half and lead straight into the closing guide, which should be read immediately afterwards.
