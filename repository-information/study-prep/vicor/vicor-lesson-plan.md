# Vicor — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach the in-rack power conversion chain from the silicon's side — why loss is current squared and the voltage therefore climbs, why the rack bus is 48 volts and not 100, why the best converters separate regulation from transformation, how a fixed-ratio current multiplier makes a thousand amps at one volt, why the last converter is moving under the processor package, how the chain reads under 800 VDC, and why a DC bus lets the battery sit on the bus — and only then read Vicor's catalogue, its licensing business and its litigation against that chain. Assumes high-school STEM (P = V × I, Ohm's law, what a transformer does). Company trivia stays in the dossier.

**How this plan relates to what you already have.** The rack chain is taught from three other sides in this app: Delta Electronics' guide (12 V → 48 V, the sixteenfold loss win, the last centimetre stated as a problem), LITEON's guide (the server supply as a product: standard slots, N+1, ORing and precharge, the density race) and Megmeet's guide (the legacy AC chain stage by stage and the 800 VDC boxes — SST, sidecar, shelf, BBU). This plan does not repeat them; it stands at the point of load and looks back up the chain, which is the view none of the three takes. Classroom's `inside-the-rack` and `the-800-vdc-shift` lessons will pin this guide for exactly that view.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min — the arithmetic everything else rests on), Module 2 in one sitting (~40 min — factorized power and the multiplier are the conceptual core), Module 3 in one sitting (~30 min — the last centimetre and vertical delivery), Module 4 in one sitting (~30 min — the 800-volt chain and the battery on the bus), Module 5 (~35 min) only after the first four, because it is Vicor's catalogue read against the chain and makes no sense without it. About three hours in total; the self-test at the end is worth an extra twenty minutes a week later.

## Module 1 — Current squared: the arithmetic the whole chain obeys

**The single idea:** delivering a fixed power at a lower voltage means a higher current, and the heat made in a conductor grows with the *square* of that current — so every power chain keeps its voltage high for as long as it can and converts down only at the end.

1. **Two equations.** Power delivered is voltage times current (P = V × I). Power lost in a conductor is current squared times resistance (P_loss = I² × R). The first says that a kilowatt at 12 volts is 83 amps and at 48 volts is 21 amps. The second says that the 12-volt version, pushed through the same one-milliohm stretch of busbar and connector, loses 6.9 watts as heat while the 48-volt version loses 0.43 — sixteen times less through identical copper, because a quarter of the current squared is a sixteenth of the loss.
2. **Read it as copper, not watts.** For the same loss, the higher-voltage bus carries the same power through a quarter of the copper cross-section, or four times the power through the same copper. In a rack where every cubic centimetre is contested by compute, the metal saved matters more than the watts.
3. **Amps per kilowatt down the chain.** One kilowatt of chip power is 0.04 A at a 13.8 kV grid feed, 1.25 A on an 800 VDC bus, 1.39 A at 415 V AC three-phase, 18.5 A on a 54 V busbar, 20.8 A at 48 V, 83 A on the old 12 V bus and 1,000 A at the one-volt processor rail. A modern accelerator draws more than one of those kilowatts. Nobody carries a thousand amps at one volt anywhere: it is made at the chip.
4. **The megawatt rack.** The 800-volt architecture is built for racks approaching a megawatt. At 12 V that is more than eighty thousand amps; at 48 V more than twenty thousand — a busbar no rack can hold; at 800 V it is 1,250 A. The number is the reason the hall is converting to DC once, early and high, and the reason the 48-volt bus, which solved the last problem, is now the stage being asked to give way.
5. **Where the arithmetic stops helping.** The chip does not run at 48 volts. The sixteen-to-one advantage is only available for the stretch *before* the last converter, so the whole design problem of this plan is to keep the voltage up until the very last centimetre and then make that final converter so small and so close to the die that its own output copper is almost nothing.

**Self-check:** a server's 12-volt bus carries 400 A through 0.5 mΩ of board copper; the same server is redesigned for 48 V. Compute the loss before and after (80 W → 5 W) and say why the designers then could not simply go to 96 V.

## Module 2 — Two jobs, two boxes: factorized power and the current multiplier

**The single idea:** a converter feeding a chip must both change the voltage level and hold its output steady; doing both in one stage forces the regulating switches to carry the chip's full current, and the elegant designs pull the two jobs apart.

1. **What a conventional buck does.** A buck converter chops its input on and off at a fixed rate (pulse-width modulation) and smooths the pulses with an inductor and capacitor; the output is the input times the fraction of time the switch is on. Several bucks out of phase — a multiphase buck — share the current and cancel ripple, which is how a voltage regulator module beside a processor delivers hundreds of amps. Its weakness at 48 V in and 1 V out is the duty cycle: on two percent of the time, so the switching edges dominate the loss, and every one of the chip's amps flows through the regulator's switches and inductors, which take board area next to the package.
2. **Three ways from 48 V to 1 V.** Two-stage (48 → 12 V bus converter → multiphase buck): the regulator handles full chip current and there is a whole intermediate bus to build. Direct (48 → 1 V in one buck): one stage, but an extreme step-down with the regulator still beside the chip. Factorized: a regulator upstream, at 48 V, where the current is tens of amps, feeding a fixed-ratio current multiplier at the load — the regulating stage handles a forty-eighth of the current and the multiplying stage never has to change what it does.
3. **The current multiplier is a transformer read from the output.** A 48:1 turns ratio takes 48 V at 1 A and delivers 1 V at 48 A — the gear train that trades speed for torque. DC has to be chopped into AC to use a transformer; run the chopping at a megahertz and the core shrinks to millimetres, because it only holds energy for a microsecond at a time. The obstacle is switching loss: interrupting a current wastes energy on every edge, and at a million edges a second the waste dominates.
4. **The sine-amplitude converter.** Drive the transformer's primary at the resonant frequency of its own leakage inductance and a capacitor, so the current is a pure sine wave, and switch only when that sine crosses zero — nothing to interrupt, so almost nothing lost in switching (zero-current switching, a form of soft switching). The stage is unregulated on purpose: its output tracks its input by the fixed ratio, which is what a multiplier should do, and which is what lets it run fast enough to shrink to the size that fits beside or under a processor.
5. **What the chip gets.** From the output side the resonant stage looks like a DC transformer with a very low output impedance: a load transient is answered through the transformer from the high-voltage side within a cycle — nanoseconds — where a buck needs microseconds to move its duty cycle. And because the multiplier does not regulate, its output filter is tiny, so the bulk capacitance that once sat beside the chip to cover the regulator's reaction time can shrink. The general principle — regulate at the source, transform near the load — is how a utility grid works; the modules that put it beside a processor are Vicor's whole catalogue, and Module 5 reads them by name.

**Self-check:** explain, in one sentence each, why the regulator in a factorized chain can be small, why the multiplier can be fast, and what the sense line from the point of load back to the regulator is for.

## Module 3 — The last centimetre: the power delivery network, and why the converter is going under the chip

**The single idea:** at a thousand amps every microohm between the converter and the die costs voltage, watts and reaction time, so the last converter is being pushed from the board's edge to the package perimeter and now beneath the package itself.

1. **The PDN.** Between the last converter and the transistors lie board copper planes, vias, package balls, the substrate's layers and the decoupling capacitors along the way — the power delivery network. At 1,000 A, 100 µΩ (a few centimetres of plane) drops 0.1 V — ten percent of a one-volt rail — and burns 100 W in copper. The whole-network target is tens of microohms and tightens every generation as current rises and the rail falls.
2. **Distance is the enemy twice.** Resistance grows with the length of copper crossed. And the load does not change gently: an accelerator can step hundreds of amps in a microsecond. No converter answers instantly — a resonant stage takes a cycle, a buck several — so capacitors on the package and board cover the first microseconds, and the route's inductance sets how fast that charge can arrive. A long path is an inductive path; the rail sags before the converter has noticed.
3. **Lateral power delivery.** Converters on the top of the board around the package, current running sideways into it. Conventional, serviceable, cooled from the top with the chip. Its limit: the last centimetre is a centimetre, and the package perimeter is contested — the memory stacks and the fastest signal lanes need the same edge, so the space for converters shrinks exactly as the current they must deliver grows.
4. **Vertical power delivery.** Converters on the underside of the board directly beneath the package; the current flows straight up through the board's thickness — a couple of millimetres — into the substrate. The PDN resistance and inductance fall by a large factor, the perimeter is freed for signals, and a larger current is met with a larger footprint under the package rather than a wider copper route beside it. The price: a second cooling path on the back of the board, lost underside real estate, tighter flatness tolerances, and a converter you can no longer see or swap without pulling the board.
5. **How to read a point-of-load datasheet.** Current density (A/cm²) says whether the part can deliver the chip's current from the footprint it is allowed; output impedance and transient response say how much board capacitance can be dropped; thermal resistance says whether the part survives its rating through the cooling path it is given; efficiency matters, but at this stage the binding constraints are area and heat in a square centimetre, and a part two points less efficient at half the size can be the better answer.

**Self-check:** a design moves its point-of-load converters from the package perimeter (about 20 mm of copper) to directly beneath the package (about 2 mm through the board). Say what happens to resistive drop, transient response and the cooling design, and name the one thing the lateral layout still does better.

## Module 4 — 800 volts to the chip, and the battery on the bus

**The single idea:** once the hall converts to DC once, early and high, everything after the 800-volt bus is DC-to-DC — only one stage needs an isolation barrier and only one needs to regulate — and a DC bus is a bus a battery can join directly.

1. **The chain under 800 VDC.** Hall conversion (SST or rectifier sidecar: 13.8 kV or 480 V AC → 800 VDC, regulated, isolated) → an 800-to-48 bus converter (fixed ratio, typically 16:1, isolated because 800 V is hazardous and 48 V is SELV; in the power shelf or on the tray — this is the stage that replaces the AC-DC server supply and the CRPS slot) → a 48-volt regulator (regulated, non-isolated, on the board or the shelf) → the current multiplier at the point of load (fixed ratio, non-isolated; beside or beneath the package) → and, on the bus, the BBU and supercapacitor shelves.
2. **Why isolation happens once.** Galvanic isolation — a transformer barrier with no conducting path across it — is what keeps a hazardous-voltage fault from reaching a touch-safe circuit. It costs size and efficiency, so the chain buys it exactly once, at the 800-to-48 step. Everything below 60 V is non-isolated by design.
3. **Why regulation happens once.** The bus converter and the multiplier are fixed-ratio machines; the one regulated stage between them sets the rail. This is factorized power applied to the whole rack rather than one board.
4. **The next collapse.** More than one vendor's roadmap takes 800 V straight to the board, or straight to a multiplier under the package, deleting the 48-volt stage as 48 deleted 12. Every deleted stage saves its loss and space and makes the survivor's job harder: an 800-to-1 step is a hazardous-voltage isolation barrier and a thousand-amp multiplier in the same square centimetre, a millimetre from a processor.
5. **The battery joins the bus.** In the AC chain the battery lives in the UPS room behind a rectifier and an inverter. A DC bus takes a battery shelf directly, through a bidirectional DC-DC converter that charges from the bus and discharges onto it through ORing devices. Two stores for two jobs: the BBU (seconds to minutes) provides the hold-up that lets a hall shrink or delete its central UPS; the supercapacitor shelf absorbs and returns the AI training swing within milliseconds, thousands of times an hour, which a battery cannot do without wearing out. Complements, not rivals, on the same bus.
6. **Which side of the 60-volt line the energy lives on.** Lithium cells are three to four volts each, so a 48-volt BBU is a dozen-odd cells whose voltage moves with state of charge (hence a converter, not a wire), while an 800-volt string is two hundred cells or more with a hazardous-voltage pack's insulation, balancing and fault-clearing demands. The converter managing either — charge control, ORing, precharge on insertion, current sharing with neighbouring shelves — is the same family of silicon as the rest of the chain.
7. **Living on a shared bus.** Nothing on the bus may drag it down (ORing), nothing joining may pull it down (precharge, staged connectors), everyone must carry a fair share (current sharing, with the efficiency-curve tax of running N+1 modules below their sweet spot), and the bus must hold up (the shelf's capacitors give tens of milliseconds; the BBU extends it — and the bus voltage, the BBU's ORing threshold and the converters' undervoltage limits must be designed together or the battery joins a few milliseconds after the servers have reset).
8. **Why this is a storage seller's room.** The BBU and capacitor shelves are bought by the server OEM or the operator as part of the rack's power design, specified in hold-up seconds, ORing, bus voltage and millisecond cycle counts rather than megawatt-hours. It is the closest an in-hall socket gets to a battery sale, and it is a different buyer with a different vocabulary from the plant-scale BESS conversation.

**Self-check:** the hall deletes its central UPS and runs the racks on 800 VDC with BBU shelves. Walk one grid outage through the chain — what holds the bus in the first 20 ms, what holds it for the next two minutes, and where the design fails if the BBU's turn-on threshold is set below the converters' undervoltage trip.

## Module 5 — Vicor against the chain: the catalogue, the fab, the licence, who buys and who competes

**The single idea:** Vicor makes no server supply, no shelf and no rectifier — its catalogue begins where the rack's power is already DC and ends at the processor's substrate — and it is paid for that idea twice, once as a module built in one Andover factory and once as a patent licence enforced at the border.

1. **The catalogue read stage by stage** (company figures, quoted in the dossier, none independently tested). *BCM bus converters:* 800 V or 400 V → ~48 V, isolated, fixed ratio — a resonant Sine Amplitude Converter with a transformer barrier; 500–800 V in at K = 1/16 (35 A), 260–410 V in at K = 1/8 (65 A, 97.9 percent peak, 3.4 kW/in³), an automotive 800 V → 48 V module at 2.5 kW; up to 98 percent and 2,870 W/in³ claimed for the family. *NBM converters:* 48 V ↔ 12 V, non-isolated, bidirectional; K = 1/4 at 80 A in a 22.8 × 17.3 × 5.2 mm package. *PRM regulators:* the one regulated stage of a factorized chain — a ZVS buck-boost at 400–800 W, 99.3 percent peak claimed. *VTM current multipliers:* the point of load at K = 1/40 to 1; 0.7–1.4 V at 130 A from 26–55 V, response under a microsecond, bulk capacitance 'reduced by a factor of 1600'. *Power-on-Package and VPD:* driver plus current multipliers on the substrate, lateral or vertical; 'greater than 3.0 A/mm²', gains 'greater than 40', 1.5 mm thin at 0.3 °C/W; a 2020 ChiP-set at 650 A continuous, 1,200 A peak; a second generation described only in earnings-call sentences. *DCM regulated converters:* 48 V → 12 V at 750 W–2 kW and 5 kW/in³.
2. **What each displaces.** The BCM displaces the AC-DC server supply and the shelf's rectifiers once the hall distributes DC; the NBM the 48-to-12 intermediate stage; the PRM the regulating half of a multiphase buck, moved to where the current is tens of amps; the VTM the transforming half and most of the capacitance beside the chip; VPD the lateral regulator array at the package edge and the board copper behind it.
3. **The fab.** Every Advanced Product is built on a panel process — converters arrayed across a printed-circuit panel like dies on a wafer, sawn and plated into 'ChiP' packages — in one company-owned 320,000-square-foot Andover building whose fab opened in May 2022. The company now says in every release that the fab is approaching capacity, has raised its capital-expenditure commitments sixfold in six months, has two sites short-listed for a second fab and is exploring an outside source for its vertical modules for the first time. Read the capacity story as the ceiling on the product thesis.
4. **The licence.** Under Section 337 the trade commission can bar an infringing import at the border. In February 2025 it did so over Vicor's bus-converter patents against a module maker and the contract manufacturers whose servers contained its modules; a $45 million settlement with unnamed parties followed in May, royalties reached a record rate by the autumn, and an all-inclusive OEM licence in May 2026 lifted royalties to a fifth of a quarter's revenue. The CEO's own sentence — respect for intellectual property 'is motivated by exclusion orders' — is the business model. Its exposure: a second case rests on one patent issued in 2025, the first case's own findings included redesigned products that did not infringe and a contract manufacturer already licensed by its purchase-order terms, and the accused vendors have answered with trade-secret suits.
5. **Who buys.** The processor company or the OEM designing the accelerator board, years ahead of volume, with its contract manufacturer — a design-win decided on current density, transient response and thermal behaviour, and re-competed every generation. The independent history is a list of sockets won (older NVIDIA generations, a Google TPU, an AMD accelerator, a wafer-scale engine) and lost (NVIDIA's H100, to a multiphase-buck vendor). The bus-converter stages are bought by the shelf or tray designer, which is why the shelf makers are both customers-in-principle and competitors.
6. **Who competes.** For the last centimetre: Monolithic Power Systems, Infineon, Renesas, Texas Instruments and Analog Devices with the in-package regulator company it bought — all on NVIDIA's published 800 VDC silicon lists, on which Vicor does not appear. For the bus-converter stages: Delta, LITEON and Megmeet, who build conversion into their own trays and sell the shelf, the sidecar and the BBU as a system. The dossier's judgments carry the confidence levels for each of these readings.
7. **The storage seller's landmark.** The BBU and supercapacitor shelves on the DC bus are specified by the same rack designers who buy these modules, in the same vocabulary — hold-up seconds, ORing, bus voltage, cycle life at millisecond swings — and never as a plant-scale energy project. The converter vendor beside the processor is usually already in that room.

**Self-check:** a customer tells you their next accelerator board moves from lateral regulators at the package edge to converters under the package, fed from a 48-volt tray that itself hangs on an 800-volt rack bus, and that they intend to license the design rather than buy modules. Name the four stages, which one is isolated, which one regulates, which vendor family competes at each stage, and what the licence changes about who builds what.

## Whole-plan self-test

1. Compute the loss in 1 mΩ of busbar for a kilowatt delivered at 12 V and at 48 V, and state the ratio.
2. Why 48 V and not 100 V — and why not 12 V any more?
3. State the two jobs a point-of-load converter does and where factorized power puts each.
4. A fixed-ratio resonant multiplier is deliberately unregulated. Give two consequences for the chip.
5. At 1,000 A, what does 100 µΩ of PDN resistance cost in volts and in watts?
6. Give two advantages and two costs of vertical power delivery.
7. Under 800 VDC, which in-rack stage is isolated and which regulates, and why only one of each?
8. Which store handles hold-up and which handles the AI training swing, and why can neither do the other's job?
9. ORing, precharge, current sharing: what does each protect against?
10. Explain in three sentences how an exclusion order turns into royalty revenue, and name the two findings in the 2025 determination that limit it.
