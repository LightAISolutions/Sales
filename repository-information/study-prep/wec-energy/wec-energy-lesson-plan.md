# WEC Energy Group — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach the subscription model of serving a data center — the bespoke resource, in which a very large customer subscribes to named new plants the utility builds and pays their full cost for fifteen years at a return fixed for the term; how a state commission rewrote a utility's tariff against the customer on four of five terms and left the fixed return; who pays for the transmission a campus needs and why that question is at the federal regulator; how a utility owns batteries it did not develop (build-transfer); why a system with nuclear under contract buys peakers and engines rather than combined cycles; what extending a nuclear purchase agreement does to a plan; and how a customer's credit rating became the binding constraint on the corpus's first Stargate campus — starting from high-school STEM. ## Module 1 — The subscription tariff

**The single idea:** a bespoke resource turns a data center from a ratepayer into a subscriber — it pays 100 percent of a named plant for at least fifteen years at a fixed return, and the utility owns the plant.

1. **Wisconsin's structure.** No integrated resource plan regime: every plant is its own certificate docket before the Public Service Commission of Wisconsin. A tariff that ties a customer to a named plant maps onto how the commission already approves plants — one docket, one asset, one subscriber. Returns are set by utility (We Energies 9.8 percent on about 53 percent equity) and, in this order, separately for bespoke assets.
2. **The five terms as approved (April 24, 2026; written order May 21; Docket 6630-TE-113).** Threshold: 100 MW of forecast new load (proposed 500). Term: 15 years minimum (proposed 10); 20 for wind and solar subscriptions; the depreciable life for gas and batteries. Cost model: 100 percent of the dedicated plant — the proposed 75 percent "capacity-only" option was removed. Return: 10.48–10.98 percent on 57 percent equity, fixed for the term, excluded from future rate cases and the earnings-sharing mechanism (unchanged). Exit: the remaining net book value to the extent the asset cannot be repurposed. Credit: security equal to the net book value of the investments made to serve the customer, mandatory below A- (S&P) or A3 (Moody's), with the utility's waiver removed. Transmission: revisions ordered so no cost shifts; reporting and a future adjustment mechanism added.
3. **The fixed return, worked once.** A $1 billion plant at 57 percent equity and 10.48–10.98 percent earns roughly $60–63 million a year for the utility; the same plant at 53 percent equity and 9.8 percent earns about $52 million. The bespoke return is higher, on more equity, and no future commission can cut it for the customer's term. The regulator allowed it because the customer bears all the cost and all the exit risk — a concentrated counterparty priced as such — and tightened the credit test for the same reason.
4. **What it means for a seller.** Every bespoke megawatt is a rate-base asset the utility wants to own; the tariff makes owning, not contracting, the profitable answer. VLC and bespoke assets are 15 percent of the 2030 asset base. The buyer of the turbines and batteries behind Microsoft's and Vantage's load is We Energies' major-projects group, to a commission's cost cap — not the hyperscaler.

**Self-check:** Microsoft's agreement subscribes at least to the Red Oak Ridge turbines. Who picks those turbines' manufacturer? (We Energies, or Invenergy as the site's developer — not Microsoft; the subscriber buys the plant's output and cost, not its specification.)

## Module 2 — Who pays for the wires

**The single idea:** the state tariff assigns transmission cost to the customer, but the wires belong to a different company under a federal formula, and only the federal regulator can make the transmission owner collect it that way.

1. **The formula rate.** American Transmission Company — 60 percent owned by WEC, equity-accounted — recovers its cost through a FERC formula rate: a network charge every load-serving utility in the zone pays in proportion to its load. A line built for one campus is normally spread across everyone's bills — the cost-allocation problem the state tariff was written to avoid.
2. **The fix.** In July 2026 MISO filed at FERC a Minimum Transmission Charge Agreement and four amended large-load project commitment agreements between ATC and Wisconsin Electric for Microsoft's campus: We Energies pays ATC's network rate on 100 percent of the transmission capacity requested, for fifteen years from completion, capped at the project's lifetime incremental revenue requirement, whether or not the load arrives — and passes the charge to Microsoft under the tariff. More than $500 million of upgrades at Mount Pleasant sit under it. "Cost causer, cost payer," ATC said.
3. **The fight.** Microsoft, six weeks after signing its VLC agreement, protested at FERC (ER26-3265): the early-termination charge is a "windfall", recovering construction work in progress on transmission is improper, and ATC's largest shareholder is the utility passing through the cost. The state commission's own comments called ATC's filing "far from fully responsive"; the Citizens Utility Board, the Data Center Coalition and the industrial customers filed too. FERC's June 2026 order to show cause on large-load transmission allocation makes the docket a template.
4. **The second line.** ATC's Port Washington project for the Vantage campus was filed at $1.39–1.67 billion, had its completeness revoked by the PSCW in August 2026, and was refiled in September at $2.48–2.72 billion including about $1.1 billion of Oracle-funded stabilization equipment at one substation. The customer is paying the difference; the line's approval is what WEC expects "by year-end".

**Self-check:** why can a customer accept a term at the state commission and fight it at FERC? (The generation subscription and the transmission cost allocation are separate instruments under separate regulators; the tariff assigns the cost, the FERC agreement is how the transmission owner collects it.)

## Module 3 — Build-transfer and the peaker book

**The single idea:** WEC owns batteries it did not develop and is building gas for hours rather than energy — and both facts move the equipment decision away from the utility.

1. **Build-transfer.** Every named We Energies battery — Paris (99 MW, in service June 2025), Darien, Koshkonong, Dawn Harvest — and the Badger Hollow wind farm were developed by Invenergy and bought at or before completion under a 5-BS purchase docket that approves price and project together. The developer carries site control, the interconnection queue, permitting and construction; the utility buys a project that already has a point of interconnection, owns it in rate base and can dedicate it to a subscriber at the bespoke return. The cost: the developer chose the battery and the EPC at bid stage, and no WEC document names an OEM. About 720 MW of the 2,130 MW battery plan is named; the rest is unfiled. WEC created a major-projects senior vice president in May 2026 — the self-build capability it lacked.
2. **Why peakers.** A simple-cycle combustion turbine wastes its exhaust, burns more gas per megawatt-hour, but starts in minutes and costs a fraction of a combined cycle per megawatt of capacity; a reciprocating engine (seven at Paris) starts faster still and holds efficiency at part load. Both are bought for hours: the winter peak, a wind lull, the gap while a battery recharges. The Point Beach contract supplies about a gigawatt of baseload; solar and wind supply energy; four-hour batteries shift it; turbines and engines cover what is left plus MISO's reserve margin. The contracted data-center ramp raises the height of the peak, not the energy every hour — so the plan is 3,300 MW of turbines, 180 MW of engines and 6 Bcf of LNG storage (the fuel buffer a peaker needs on the coldest morning), not combined cycles.
3. **The dockets.** Oak Creek (1,100 MW) and Paris (128 MW) approved May 2025, in service late 2027; Foundry Ridge (270 MW) and Red Oak Ridge (1,125 MW) — Invenergy-developed sites We Energies filed to acquire — pending decisions in the fourth quarter of 2026. No turbine manufacturer is disclosed for any of them; at Duke that gap would be a GE Vernova slot count.

**Self-check:** a plan shows 2,130 MW of batteries and 720 MW named. Where does the balance come from, and who chooses the hardware? (Further build-transfer purchases from developers — Invenergy's pipeline — or self-build under the new major-projects group; in the first case the developer picks the OEM.)

## Module 4 — The nuclear contract

**The single idea:** extending a nuclear purchase agreement removes a replacement decision from the plan — the same effect as Duke's 80-year licenses, achieved by contract.

1. **What it is.** We Energies buys about 86 percent of NextEra's Point Beach output (about 1 GW) under a purchase agreement that was to expire in 2030 and 2033, at a price escalating from $75.51 per megawatt-hour in 2026 toward $122 by 2033 — about a fifth of the 2027 rate request. In August 2026 WEC agreed to extend it to 2050 and 2053 on terms it says save customers money against the current contract; the commission's approval and the new price are pending.
2. **Why it matters.** For two years the company told investors that replacing Point Beach would cost roughly $2–2.5 billion per 500 MW to a gigawatt of new capacity — a decision flagged for the third-quarter 2026 call. The extension keeps a gigawatt of firm, carbon-free baseload that would otherwise need new gas or storage in 2030, and the $37.5 billion plan does not have to grow to cover it.
3. **What a seller reads.** A utility that extends a nuclear contract has decided its next gigawatt of firm capacity will not be new build; what it still needs are peakers for the peak and batteries for the shift. And the approved price becomes the benchmark for what firm, carbon-free capacity is worth in Wisconsin — the number the data-center subscriptions are compared with.

**Self-check:** name a second way in this corpus that a utility keeps baseload without building it. (Subsequent license renewals — Duke's Oconee and Robinson to 80 years.)

## Module 5 — Credit is the constraint

**The single idea:** the tariff's security rule turned a customer's rating into a cash event, and it was tested within seven weeks of the order.

1. **The sequence.** April 24: the rule is written (security at net book value below A-/A3, no utility waiver). June 12: We Energies, Vantage and Cloverleaf petition to ease it — the utility siding with its customer. June 24: Oracle sues the commission. Early July: the commission refuses rehearing. July 9: S&P cuts Oracle to BBB- — the rule binds. July 29: the CEO says "all the collateral we need … if it goes down, we already have". August 17: Oracle drops the suit; September: "Oracle has met the financial security requirements". June 1, 2027: VLC service agreements replace the payment-and-cancellation agreements.
2. **What the bridge agreements do.** Before the tariff existed, We Energies signed payment-and-cancellation agreements obliging the customer to reimburse every project cost, including ATC's, if it terminated, cut its load or approval was not received. They carry the campus until the fixed-term subscription begins.
3. **The politics.** Three commissioners appointed by a governor whose term ends in January 2027 wrote the tariff; four data-center bills died in the 2026 legislative session (one passed the Assembly and stalled in the Senate); moratorium proposals surfaced in the governor's race and in Kenosha over Microsoft's second site. The tariff is an order, not a statute; the security rule and the fixed return are both revisitable by a different commission.
4. **The whole picture.** Two campuses, 3.9 GW of forecast demand, about 45 percent of the company's electric demand growth over 2026–2030 — the most fully contracted book in the corpus in structure and, with DTE's, the most concentrated. Meta's Beaver Dam campus, sometimes counted with them, is Alliant's load. The next name the CEO has twice suggested (400–500 MW prospects) would be a colocation developer, not a hyperscaler.

**Self-check:** the customer is downgraded below A-. What does the tariff require, who decides whether to waive it, and what protects the other 1.1 million customers? (Security equal to the book value of the plants built to serve it; nobody — the commission removed the utility's waiver; the security itself.)
