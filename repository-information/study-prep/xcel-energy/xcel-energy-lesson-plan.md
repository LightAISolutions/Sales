# Xcel Energy — Technology Lesson Plan

**Purpose:** teach how a multi-state utility gets permission to build in states that plan resources rather than certify them one plant at a time — what an integrated resource plan actually decides and what it deliberately leaves open, why a plan approval is not a portfolio and a settlement is not an outcome, how the same large-load tariff comes out differently in three states and which two knobs set its strictness, what it means when a customer pays the entire cost of its own new generation instead of joining a rate class, why a hundred-hour battery is a different job from a four-hour one, why an operating company that belongs to no regional transmission organization has no capacity price and no independent load forecast to check its own, and — the question the four earlier utility plans never had to ask — how an uncapped wildfire liability becomes an input to the cost of every unrelated megawatt. Starting from high-school STEM and from the Dominion lesson plan, which this one does not repeat. No company trivia: founding dates, executives and share counts stay in the dossier. Companion: the in-app guide (Profiler → Xcel Energy → Study guide 📖) carries the condensed version, the flashcards and the self-test.

**Prerequisite.** The Dominion Energy lesson plan (`study-prep/dominion-energy/`) teaches the revenue-requirement equation, the IRP → CPCN → RFP sequence, the rate case, the anatomy of a large-load tariff, the interconnection study and the RTO layer with PJM as the example. Do that plan first. The Southern Company plan (an elected commission, a grid with no RTO, a decade certified in one order), the Entergy plan (a holding company under formula rate plans, a certificate naming one customer) and the Oncor plan (a wires-only utility in an energy-only market) are the second, third and fourth regulatory stories. This is the fifth, and the one where the binding constraint is neither permission nor market design but an open-ended legal liability.

**Suggested pacing:** Module 1 in one sitting (~30 min — the operating-company structure and why the state line is the unit), Module 2 in one sitting (~45 min — what a resource plan decides, what it leaves open, and the two-solicitation problem), Module 3 in one sitting (~40 min — the same tariff in three drafts, and the two knobs that set strictness), Module 4 in one sitting (~35 min — the customer-funded charge and the hundred-hour battery), Module 5 last (~40 min — wildfire as a financing input, and the state with no regional operator above it), then the flashcard and self-test passes in the app.

## Module 1 — Eight states, four utilities: why the state line is the unit of analysis

**The single idea:** a holding company has a ticker; its operating companies have the customers, the plants and the regulators, and every decision that matters is made one state at a time.

1. **The structure.** Four operating utilities under one parent. One serves Minnesota, North Dakota and South Dakota; one serves Wisconsin and Michigan; one serves Colorado; one serves Texas and New Mexico. Eight states, five million electric and gas customers, roughly eleven and a half thousand employees, about 44 percent of them under collective bargaining agreements. Three grid regions, and — as Module 5 returns to — one operating company in none of them.
2. **Why the group level tells you nothing.** A rate case is filed by one operating company against one state's commission, on that state's test-year convention, seeking that state's allowed return on equity. Across 2025 and 2026 this company ran seven of them and got seven answers: one state raised the allowed return to 9.60 percent, another allowed 9.8 percent, and the largest cut a *settled* increase from $225 million to $157 million and imposed a thirteen-month average historical test year the parties had not agreed to. There is no group rate case, no group tariff, and no group answer to what a gigawatt costs.
3. **The same shape you have already seen, with a different mechanism.** The Entergy plan taught a five-state holding company whose operating companies mostly adjust rates by *formula* between cases. This one mostly *litigates*. The Southern plan taught a company whose largest state has an elected commission; here every commission is appointed. And where Dominion has one dominant state, the largest here is only 43 percent of rate base — big enough to dominate the result, not big enough to be the whole story.
4. **The operating company to hold in mind.** The Colorado utility. It is the largest by rate base, it earns the lowest return of the four at 7.22 percent on a trailing basis, it belongs to no regional transmission organization, its proposed large-load tariff is the strictest of the three and is not decided until 2027, and it is where the Marshall Fire burned. Almost every hard question in this file lands there.

**Self-check:** a developer wants the same large-load contract terms in two of this company's states. Why can the utility not simply offer them? (Each state's tariff is a separate filing before a separate commission with its own thresholds, minimums and schedule — one is approved, one is proposed and one is filed.)

## Module 2 — What a resource plan decides, and the two solicitations running at once

**The single idea:** a resource-plan order finds a need and grants permission to go shopping; it does not name a project, name a developer or set a price, and the gap between the order and the contract is measured in years.

1. **The proceeding, not the document.** The utility files a demand forecast, retirement dates for existing units, and portfolios that would meet the resulting need. Intervenors — consumer advocates, industrial customers, environmental groups, commission staff — file testimony attacking the forecast and the portfolio economics. The commission issues an order finding a need in megawatts. Nothing downstream is possible without that finding, because a utility cannot rate-base a plant no order found necessary.
2. **Three things the order settles.** The load forecast (one state's commission accepted about 3 percent compound annual growth, and that acceptance is what makes everything after it defensible); the quantity and rough shape of the need (one state found roughly 5,400 MW; another authorized a 420 MW combustion turbine, a 300 MW four-hour battery, 3,200 MW of wind, 400 MW of solar and 600 MW of storage through 2030, plus nuclear licence extensions into the early 2050s); and the retirement schedule for existing coal, which is what creates the need in the first place.
3. **Three things it does not settle.** It does not name the projects, does not name the developers, and does not set the price — those come from a competitive all-source solicitation afterwards, where the utility's own self-build proposals bid against third parties behind an independent evaluator. The live demonstration: one state's plan was approved, the Phase II solicitation has bids due in the third quarter of 2026, and as of September 2026 **nothing has been selected**. Plan approval and portfolio selection are eighteen months apart.
4. **Two solicitations at once, and why.** The same commission that approved that plan separately approved ten projects totalling up to 4,100 MW in February 2026 under a *near-term procurement* — a different track. The reason is arithmetic: a plan cycle takes about three years from filing to signed contract and load does not wait. When a utility needs capacity sooner it asks for a bounded, faster proceeding justified by reliability rather than long-run portfolio economics. The commission grants it because refusing means a gap or an emergency purchase. The cost is that the second track is decided on thinner analysis. Watch for it in any state whose forecast is rising faster than its planning cycle.
5. **The third channel, and the strategic move.** Before either solicitation closed and before any tariff was approved, this company signed a strategic alliance reserving five F-class gas turbines — later restated as twelve combustion-turbine reservations with options into the 2030s — plus a capacity reservation for multiple gigawatts of wind turbines, and a joint development agreement to co-develop generation for about 2 GW of data-center capacity under a fully regulated structure mixing purchase agreements, build-own-transfers and self-builds. **The lesson generalises:** when the binding constraint is turbines and crews rather than permission, the first binding contract to sign is with the supply chain, not the customer. The cost is a conflict the commission must police — a utility holding turbine slots is not a neutral evaluator of bids that would use someone else's.
6. **A settlement is not an outcome.** Every party to one 2026 rate case signed at $225 million; the commission granted $157 million and changed the test-year convention. Commissions are not bound by settlements. Read a settlement filing as the best plausible case, never as the answer.

**Self-check:** a commission approves a plan finding 5,400 MW of need. What has the utility obtained, and what has it not? (Authority to run a solicitation; not contracts, not projects, not prices, and not — as the same state shows — any guarantee that the *separate* near-term procurement's awards came from this plan at all.)

## Module 3 — The same tariff in three drafts: the two knobs that set strictness

**The single idea:** a large-load tariff is a set of answers to five questions, and two of them — the applicability threshold and the minimum demand charge — decide almost everything about how strict it is.

1. **Why the class exists.** The Dominion plan taught this in general: a customer whose demand rivals a small city's causes investment that would otherwise be spread across everyone, so a special class exists to make the customer bear the cost it causes. The instrument is always the same shape: a threshold, a contract term, a minimum demand charge, exit provisions and credit support.
2. **The two knobs.** The **applicability threshold** is the size at which an ordinary industrial customer becomes a class of one — a lower threshold catches more customers. The **minimum demand charge** is the share of contracted capacity billed whether or not it is used — a higher minimum holds each customer to more. Low threshold plus high minimum is the strictest combination available. One of this company's states proposes 50 MW and 80 percent, which is both.
3. **Three drafts, three stages.** One state's tariff is approved and in force. One is proposed with a decision expected in early 2027. One is filed. Terms move between filing and order — that is what intervenor testimony is for — so a term sheet drawn from a proposed tariff is a negotiating position, not a price.
4. **The politics visible in the numbers.** The strictest of the three is the state that has *not* decided yet, earns the lowest return, and just had its settled rate increase cut by its commission. A commission under public pressure on affordability writes a harder tariff. That is not a coincidence; it is the mechanism. It also means a tariff is a political document with a spreadsheet attached, and the way to forecast one is to read the commission's other recent orders.
5. **Both sides of the 80 percent argument, honestly.** *For:* it converts a forecast into a receivable, prices optionality that would otherwise be free, filters speculative requests before they clog the queue, and supports the credit rating that determines the cost of debt on the whole plan. *Against:* it can price the state out of a market for genuinely mobile load, it is close to take-or-pay on an asset that ramps over years, it assumes the utility's cost estimate is right when the prudence review that would test it arrives years later, and it does not settle who pays for the shared network — that is still ordinary cost allocation, so the cross-subsidy fight moves rather than disappears.

**Self-check:** State A files a tariff at a 100 MW threshold and 60 percent minimum; State B at 50 MW and 80 percent. Which is stricter and why? (B — both knobs move the same way: it catches more customers and holds each to more.)

## Module 4 — When the customer pays for its own supply

**The single idea:** the ordinary structure has the utility build, the rate base grow and all customers pay, with a tariff limiting the spillover; a dedicated customer charge takes the asset out of general rates entirely, which answers one question and opens another.

1. **The instrument.** One filing in this file proposes 1,900 MW of new clean supply for a single data center — 1,400 MW of wind, 200 MW of solar, 300 MW of long-duration storage — recovered not through ordinary rates but through a dedicated accelerator charge under which the customer "will pay all costs for its new service for the duration of the contract". The utility projects roughly $1.1 billion of benefit to other customers, and the customer contributed $50 million to a capacity programme alongside it. A decision is expected in early 2027.
2. **Four templates, side by side.** Entergy certifies gas plants for one named hyperscaler and rate-bases them, with the customer's contract as assurance. Southern certifies a decade of capacity in one order under a base-rate freeze with a shareholder backstop. Oncor certifies nothing and simply connects, with collateral as the only commitment test. This proposes a charge outside general rates, so the asset's cost never reaches the residential class at all. **Four regulatory answers to one commercial question, and the differences are the states', not the companies'.**
3. **What it answers and what it opens.** It answers the cross-subsidy attack completely: if the customer pays every cost, no other ratepayer subsidizes the build. It opens a different question — a commission is being asked to approve a large asset on one customer's contract, outside the portfolio modelling and intervenor scrutiny a resource plan would have applied.
4. **The hundred-hour battery.** The storage inside that package is a 300 MW, 30 GWh iron-air system — a hundred-hour duration. Do the arithmetic: 30 GWh divided by 300 MW is 100 hours. An ordinary grid battery is four-hour lithium, shifting energy within a day to cover the evening peak. A hundred-hour system covers a still, cloudy week — a resource-adequacy job, not a peak-shifting one. Different chemistry, different economics, and not comparable to a lithium fleet on cost per kilowatt-hour. Note also that it is being procured inside a customer's service agreement rather than through a resource plan.
5. **Why a customer volunteers to pay.** Speed and a clean-energy claim. Waiting for a plan cycle costs years; funding the package directly skips the queue. And a customer that funds 1,900 MW of *new* wind, solar and storage can say its load is served by new clean resources rather than by the existing grid mix — a procurement objective, not a regulatory one. **The generalisable point:** when a customer's binding constraint is time and reputation rather than price, the cheapest tariff is not the one it wants.
6. **Contracted is not pipeline.** The same company publishes three data-center numbers on one slide: about 2 GW contracted or under construction, about 4 GW contracted by end-2027, more than 20 GW of additional pipeline. Only the first describes agreements that exist. The second is a target wearing the word "contracted"; the third is interest. Two details sharpen it: the contracted block did not grow between two consecutive quarterly decks, and one 200 MW project's online date slipped a year between them. A third is more honest still — four of five project rows are disclosed and the fifth is marked "not publicly disclosed". **And across companies these tiers are not comparable at all**: one reports 69 GW under executed agreements, one 298 GW of requests against 8 GW energized, one 53.8 GW contracted of which 12.0 GW is firm. Each headlines a different tier of its own funnel.

**Self-check:** a portfolio announcement quotes 5,168 MW; the same filing gives 3,121 MW accredited. Which figure tells you how much peak demand it serves, and why is the gap so large? (The accredited figure; nameplate is the rating under ideal conditions, and a wind-heavy portfolio contributes far less at peak — here about 40 percent less.)

## Module 5 — Wildfire as a financing input, and the state with no operator above it

**The single idea:** an uncapped legal liability is not a safety line item — it raises insurance premiums, pressures credit ratings and therefore raises the cost of the debt funding every unrelated megawatt in the plan.

1. **Two fires, and the shape of the disclosure.** One settled in 2025 at approximately $640 million, of which roughly $350 million came from insurance and, in the company's words, none from customers — leaving about $298 million of shareholder charges and the *only* item reconciling that year's GAAP earnings of $3.42 per share to ongoing earnings of $3.80. The other, attributed by a state forest service to the utility's own power lines after wooden poles failed, has produced $404 million of settlements against a $525 million policy period with roughly $80 million of coverage left, and the company states it "remains unable to reasonably estimate any additional loss or the upper end of the range". **Read that clause carefully:** a rate case has a number on both sides; an unbounded liability has a number on one side only.
2. **What it costs the rest of the business.** Excess liability insurance now runs roughly $135 million a year for $450 million of coverage, against about $40 million for $520 million two years earlier — three times the premium for less protection. Approximately $5 billion of a $60 billion base capital plan is wildfire mitigation. A securitization is planned for 2029 and already appears in the plan's own capital tables, meaning the company is budgeting today for an instrument that exists to spread a cost it has not yet incurred. Rating agencies carry negative outlooks on the parent and on the largest operating company.
3. **The variable that decides all of it is state law.** Whether a state applies strict liability or inverse condemnation, whether it caps aggregate damages, whether it grants an affirmative defence to a utility that followed an approved mitigation plan — these bound the consequence far more than any hardening programme bounds the probability. This company's own framework grid credits legislative wins in three states and does *not* check the one where its largest utility and its largest settled fire both sit. **So the sequence to memorize is:** a fire creates a liability → the liability's size depends on a statute the utility does not write → the statute's absence raises the premium and lowers the rating → the higher cost of capital raises the price of every unrelated megawatt.
4. **No regional operator above the largest state.** Two operating companies are in one regional transmission organization, one is in another, and the largest "is not presently a member of an RTO", though it sells into neighbouring markets and joins an imbalance market. Three consequences: **no capacity price** set by auction, so a resource's worth is whatever the plan model says; **no regional planner** allocating transmission across a footprint, which is why that state's own major transmission line is a state proceeding that drew county-level objection; and **no independent load forecast** against which the utility's own can be checked — the commission's acceptance of about 3 percent growth is the only such finding that exists.
5. **What that means commercially.** In that state, **the commission is the market**. It finds the need, approves the procurement, allocates the transmission and sets the tariff. Every megawatt of supply passes through a state solicitation, so the buying decision is not made in a procurement department — it is made in a docket, on a schedule published a year ahead, before intervenors who will read your bid. It also means the commission's mood is a business risk, which is what the August 2026 order looked like when it landed.
6. **Five things this model cannot tell you.** A commission order is not a contract and an approved plan is not a portfolio. A settlement is not an outcome. A proposed tariff is not a price. Nameplate is not accredited and contracted is not pipeline. And a liability with no statutory cap has no ceiling you can model — when a state has passed no limit, the honest answer is that the exposure is open, and the thing to watch is the legislature rather than the utility.

**Self-check:** why does an uncapped wildfire liability raise the cost of an unrelated solar project in a different state? (It raises insurance premiums and pressures the parent's credit rating; a lower rating raises the cost of the debt funding the whole capital plan, and that plan spans all eight states.)

## Whole-plan self-test

1. A commission approves a resource plan finding a need in megawatts. Name three things the order settles and three it does not.
2. Two solicitations from the same commission overlap in the same year. Why does that happen, and what is the cost of the second one?
3. State A: 100 MW threshold, 60 percent minimum. State B: 50 MW threshold, 80 percent minimum. Which tariff is stricter, and what does each knob do?
4. A utility reserves turbines and wind-turbine capacity before its tariffs are approved and its solicitation closes. Give the strategic logic and the conflict it creates.
5. A customer agrees to pay the full cost of 1,900 MW of new generation through a dedicated charge. Which ordinary regulatory argument does this eliminate, and which new question does it raise?
6. 30 GWh divided by 300 MW is 100 hours. What job does a hundred-hour battery do that a four-hour battery cannot?
7. An operating company belongs to no regional transmission organization. Name the three regional functions that disappear and the body that absorbs them.
8. A company discloses that it "remains unable to reasonably estimate the upper end of the range" for a liability. What does that sentence mean about the exposure, and what would create a ceiling?
9. A slide reads 2 GW, 4 GW and 20 GW-plus. Which describes agreements that exist, and what do the other two describe?
10. Every party to a rate case signs a settlement and the commission grants less. What does this tell you about how to read settlement filings?
